## ccp-10-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Trash cans kept within the curtilage
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 1
Prompt: Ellery Marsh keeps his trash cans beside his attached garage, 15 feet from the house and inside a gated fence. He wheels them to the curb only on pickup mornings. At 2 a.m. on a non-pickup night, detectives open the gate, walk up the driveway, and take two bags from the cans, which turn out to contain drug packaging. Was taking the bags a search?
A: No, because people have no expectation of privacy in their garbage.
B: No, because trash is abandoned once placed in a trash can.
C: No, because the cans could be seen from the street.
D: Yes. The rule that trash is unprotected rests on leaving it at the curb, exposed to the public and given to a collector. Here the cans sat within the fenced curtilage next to the house, so going through the gate to take the bags was a physical intrusion on a protected area to gather information.
Answer: D
Authorities: California v. Greenwood; United States v. Jones; Florida v. Jardines; United States v. Dunn
Explanation: Rule: Greenwood held there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in trash left at the curb for collection, because it is readily accessible to the public and handed to a third party. Separately, a physical intrusion on curtilage to gather information is a search (Jones; Jardines). Curtilage is judged by the Dunn factors. Application: Marsh's cans were within the fenced area next to the house, not at the curb. The detectives went through a gate at 2 a.m., which is beyond any implied license to approach the front door. Both the trespass test and Greenwood's own reasoning protect him. Why not the others: A and B overstate Greenwood, which depended on placement at the curb. C confuses visibility with access; seeing the cans does not license entering the curtilage to open them. Policy: Greenwood reflects that what people expose to the public is fair game. Protecting trash still within the curtilage keeps police from using garbage collection as a reason to enter the area around the home.

## ccp-10-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Real-time phone pinging to find a suspect
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 2
Prompt: Without a warrant, Brannock County detectives ask a carrier to "ping" Maxen Orrel's phone in real time every 15 minutes for six hours so they can find and arrest him on an outstanding robbery warrant. The pings lead them to a motel, where they arrest him. Which statement most accurately describes the analysis?
A: This is clearly not a search, because the location-records case involved only historical records.
B: This is clearly a search requiring a warrant, because the location-records case held that all cell-site information requires a warrant.
C: The location-records case expressly did not decide real-time tracking. Six hours of pinging is far shorter than the long-term records in that case, which helps the government. Orrel's best argument is that the government forced his phone to reveal his location on demand, including possibly inside private places. A court might also find that an exigency or the existing arrest warrant supports the action.
D: This is not a search, because Orrel lacks standing to challenge a carrier's actions.
Answer: C
Authorities: Carpenter v. United States; United States v. Knotts; United States v. Karo
Explanation: Rule: Carpenter held that obtaining at least seven days of historical cell-site records is a search but expressly declined to decide real-time location tracking or short-term data. Knotts allowed beeper monitoring on public roads; Karo held that monitoring inside a home is a search. Application: Six hours of pinging is limited and closer to Knotts, but pinging actively forces the phone to report its location, which can reveal private places such as a motel room, invoking Karo. Courts are divided. The outstanding arrest warrant and any exigency also bear on reasonableness. Why not the others: A and B treat an open question as settled. D is wrong because the data concerned Orrel's own location, so his own interest is at stake. Policy: Real-time pinging helps locate fugitives and missing people quickly. Critics argue that turning every phone into a tracking beacon on request, without judicial review, is precisely the kind of permeating surveillance Carpenter warned against.

## ccp-10-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Greenhouse as curtilage under the four factors
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 3
Prompt: Ilsa Corran's small glass greenhouse stands 50 feet from her back door, inside the same picket fence that encloses her house and patio. She grows tomatoes and herbs there, and it is screened from the road by a row of tall shrubs. Officers enter her yard, open the greenhouse door, and find illegal plants among the tomatoes. Was entering the greenhouse a search?
A: Likely yes. Under the four-factor test, the greenhouse's closeness to the house, location within the same enclosure, use for domestic gardening, and screening from public view all indicate curtilage. Entering it to look inside was a search.
B: No, because any outbuilding is part of the open fields.
C: No, because a greenhouse is made of glass and so exposes its contents to public view.
D: No, because curtilage includes only the house itself.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Dunn; Oliver v. United States; United States v. Jones
Explanation: Rule: Curtilage is determined by (1) proximity to the home, (2) whether the area is within an enclosure surrounding the home, (3) the nature of its uses, and (4) steps taken to protect it from observation (Dunn). Open fields beyond the curtilage are unprotected (Oliver). Physically entering curtilage to gather information is a search (Jones). Application: Fifty feet away, inside the same fence, used for household gardening, and screened by shrubs, the greenhouse resembles curtilage, unlike the barn in Dunn, which was far from the house, outside the inner fence, and associated with possible drug manufacturing. Why not the others: B misstates the law; outbuildings can be curtilage. C confuses glass walls with public exposure; the shrubs blocked public view, and in any case officers entered. D is too narrow; curtilage is by definition the area around the house. Policy: The curtilage doctrine extends the home's protection to the spaces where private domestic life spills outside the walls.

## ccp-10-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: General warrant for "any evidence of any crime"
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 4
Prompt: Detective Arne Calloway's affidavit establishes probable cause that Petra Lisle stole a vintage guitar. The warrant he drafts and the magistrate signs authorizes searching her house for "the stolen guitar and any other evidence of any crime." Officers find the guitar and also seize her tax papers, which show unrelated fraud. Is the fraud evidence admissible?
A: Yes, because the warrant expressly authorized seizing evidence of any crime.
B: Likely not. "Any other evidence of any crime" is a general-warrant clause that fails particularity on its face, and no reasonable officer could think it valid. The guitar clause can be severed and upheld, but evidence seized only under the general clause is suppressed unless it independently qualifies for plain view.
C: Yes, because the magistrate approved the warrant, and good faith always covers magistrate-approved warrants.
D: No, and the guitar must be suppressed too, because a single defective clause voids the entire warrant.
Answer: B
Authorities: Particularity requirement; Groh v. Ramirez; United States v. Leon; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: The Fourth Amendment forbids general warrants and requires a particular description of things to be seized. Leon's good faith does not apply to warrants so facially deficient that officers cannot reasonably presume them valid (Groh). Courts may sever valid clauses from invalid ones. Plain view permits seizing items whose incriminating character is immediately apparent while officers are lawfully searching. Application: "Any evidence of any crime" is the general warrant the Framers opposed, and its defect is obvious. The guitar clause is supported by probable cause and is severable. Tax papers are not obviously incriminating at a glance, so plain view likely does not save them. Why not the others: A ignores particularity. C misstates Leon's limits. D ignores severability. Policy: Particularity is the Fourth Amendment's answer to the colonial general warrant. Severance avoids penalizing the government for evidence properly within the warrant's valid scope.

## ccp-10-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Delay in executing a warrant and stale probable cause
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 5
Prompt: On May 1, a magistrate issues a warrant to search Torin Abbe's apartment for a specific stolen painting that an informant saw there on April 30. Detectives get busy and do not execute it until May 26. During that time they learn that Abbe has been advertising the painting for sale online and that a buyer arranged to pick it up on May 10. They search on May 26 anyway and find other stolen art. What is the strongest argument for Abbe?
A: The warrant was invalid when issued because the informant saw the painting only once.
B: Warrants must be executed within 24 hours of issuance.
C: A warrant must be executed while the probable cause supporting it still exists. By May 26, the officers knew facts strongly suggesting the painting was gone, so the probable cause had gone stale and executing the warrant was unreasonable.
D: The officers needed to announce the planned date of the search to Abbe in advance.
Answer: C
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Reasonableness of warrant execution
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause must exist when the search occurs, not just when the warrant issues. The assigned warrant-execution material treats time limits as part of reasonable execution: delay can make a search unreasonable if the facts supporting probable cause no longer hold. Application: The warrant was valid on May 1. By May 26, police knew of a sale and a planned May 10 pickup, which strongly undercuts any fair probability that the painting was still there. Executing a stale warrant after learning such facts is Abbe's best argument. Why not the others: A ignores Gates's acceptance of a recent firsthand observation. B invents a 24-hour rule. D has no basis. Policy: Time limits on execution keep a magistrate's judgment tied to current facts. Without them, warrants could function as open-ended licenses to search whenever convenient.

## ccp-10-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Observed hand-to-hand exchange and probable cause to arrest
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 6
Prompt: At 1 a.m. on a corner with frequent drug arrests, an officer with ten years in narcotics work watches Caspar Druin take cash from three people in 15 minutes. Each time he reaches into a hollow in a nearby tree, retrieves something small, and hands it over. When a marked car passes, he walks away fast. The officer arrests him. Did the officer have probable cause?
A: No, because the officer never saw what was handed over.
B: No, because presence in a high-crime area never contributes to probable cause.
C: No, because probable cause requires proof that is more likely than not.
D: Likely yes. Under the totality of the circumstances, repeated cash exchanges, retrievals from a hidden stash, and flight at the sight of police, interpreted through the officer's training and experience, create a fair probability of drug dealing. Each fact alone would be weaker.
Answer: D
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Maryland v. Pringle; United States v. Watson
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause is a fluid, practical concept: a fair probability under the totality of the circumstances, viewed from the standpoint of an objectively reasonable officer with relevant training and experience (Gates; Pringle). It need not meet a preponderance standard. A warrantless felony arrest in public is permitted on probable cause (Watson). Application: The pattern of repeated cash exchanges, a hidden stash, and flight, viewed by an experienced narcotics officer, is far more than mere presence in a high-crime area. Why not the others: A demands certainty about the object. B misstates the law; location can be a factor, just not enough alone. C overstates the probable-cause standard. Policy: Allowing officers to draw on experience makes probable cause workable. Requiring a concrete pattern rather than location alone protects residents of high-crime areas from arrest based only on where they are.

## ccp-10-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Warrantless arrest for a misdemeanor committed in the officer's presence
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 7
Prompt: A Brannock County officer watches Hester Vane slip a $20 phone charger into her bag and walk out of a store without paying, a misdemeanor. The officer arrests her in the parking lot without a warrant and, incident to the arrest, finds a stolen credit card in her pocket. Was the warrantless arrest valid?
A: No, because officers need a warrant to arrest for any misdemeanor.
B: Yes. A warrantless arrest in a public place is valid for a misdemeanor committed in the officer's presence, as well as for a felony on probable cause. The search of her person incident to that custodial arrest was also valid.
C: No, because officers may never make a custodial arrest for a minor offense.
D: Yes, but the search was invalid, because shoplifting a charger leaves no evidence to find on the person.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Watson; United States v. Robinson; Payton v. New York
Explanation: Rule: Officers may make warrantless public arrests for felonies on probable cause and for misdemeanors committed in their presence (Watson; the common-law rule reflected in the outline). A custodial arrest justifies a full search of the person without case-specific justification (Robinson). Payton's warrant requirement applies to entries into homes, not public arrests. Application: The officer personally saw the shoplifting in public, so the arrest was valid and the search of Vane's person was lawful. Why not the others: A and C overstate limits on misdemeanor arrests. D imports Gant's vehicle-specific evidence prong into searches of the person, which Robinson does not require. Policy: The in-presence rule gives officers authority to stop offenses they see while requiring a warrant for misdemeanors they learn about only later. Critics argue that custodial arrests for minor offenses invite searches and humiliation out of proportion to the offense.

## ccp-10-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Vehicle search incident to arrest: evidence of the crime of arrest
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 8
Prompt: Officers watch Rowan Tallis sell heroin from the driver's seat of his parked car through the window to a buyer. They arrest him, handcuff him, and put him in the patrol car. Then they search his car's passenger compartment and find more heroin and a ledger in the console. Is the search valid as incident to arrest?
A: Yes. Although Tallis was secured, it was reasonable to believe evidence of the crime of arrest, a drug sale made from the car, would be found in the passenger compartment.
B: No, because once the arrestee is secured, a vehicle may never be searched incident to arrest.
C: No, because officers needed a warrant to search a car.
D: Yes, because the arrest of any recent occupant automatically allows a search of the passenger compartment.
Answer: A
Authorities: Arizona v. Gant; Chimel v. California; Carroll v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Gant allows a vehicle search incident to arrest only if the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the passenger compartment, or if it is reasonable to believe evidence of the crime of arrest might be found in the vehicle. Application: Tallis was secured, so the first prong fails. But he was arrested for a drug sale conducted from the car, so it is reasonable to believe more drugs or records are in the passenger compartment. The second prong applies. Officers likely also had probable cause under the automobile exception (Carroll). Why not the others: B ignores Gant's second prong. C ignores both Gant and the automobile exception. D states the broad pre-Gant reading that Gant rejected. Policy: Gant's evidence prong recognizes that some crimes are naturally linked to the car, while preventing arrests for unrelated offenses from justifying routine car searches.

## ccp-10-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Opening closed containers under a standardized inventory policy
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 9
Prompt: After Delia Morrow is arrested for drunk driving, her van is lawfully impounded. The county's written policy requires officers to open and list the contents of all closed containers found in impounded vehicles. Following that policy, an officer opens a closed backpack and finds stolen jewelry. Is the evidence admissible?
A: No, because opening closed containers always requires a warrant.
B: Yes. An inventory carried out under standardized criteria, including a standardized policy on opening containers, is reasonable without a warrant or probable cause, because it protects the owner's property, protects police from claims, and protects against danger.
C: No, because Morrow could have arranged for someone to pick up her belongings, so officers had to let her.
D: No, because inventory searches require probable cause.
Answer: B
Authorities: Colorado v. Bertine; Illinois v. Lafayette
Explanation: Rule: Bertine upheld an inventory search that included opening closed containers, including a backpack, under standardized police procedures. It held that officers need not give the arrestee a chance to make other arrangements and that the existence of less intrusive alternatives does not make an inventory unreasonable. Lafayette upheld inventories of arrestees' belongings at the station. Application: The policy required opening all containers, which limits officer discretion as Bertine requires. Nothing suggests pretext. Why not the others: A ignores Bertine. C states the less-intrusive-alternative argument Bertine rejected. D imposes a probable-cause requirement inventories do not have. Policy: Standardized inventories serve real caretaking functions and protect police from claims of lost property. Requiring uniform policies prevents officers from choosing whose containers to open.

## ccp-10-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Drug testing students who want parking permits
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 10
Prompt: Brannock County High requires random, suspicionless drug tests for students who apply for permits to park on campus. Results go only to the student's parents and a counselor, never to police, and a positive test only suspends the permit. Student Lark Avery challenges the policy. Which outcome is most likely?
A: The policy is likely valid. Extending the reasoning that allowed testing students in voluntary extracurricular activities, driving to school is a voluntary privilege, the school has a safety interest in preventing impaired driving on campus, and results are kept away from law enforcement with only non-punitive consequences.
B: The policy is invalid, because drug testing of students requires individualized suspicion.
C: The policy is invalid, because only athletes may be subjected to suspicionless testing.
D: The policy is valid, because schools may conduct any search of students without suspicion.
Answer: A
Authorities: Vernonia School District v. Acton; Board of Education v. Earls; Ferguson v. City of Charleston
Explanation: Rule: Vernonia upheld random testing of student athletes, and Earls extended it to all students in competitive extracurricular activities, based on the school's custodial responsibility, students' reduced privacy expectations, the voluntary nature of participation, and results kept from police with limited consequences. Ferguson invalidates programs whose primary purpose is law enforcement. Application: No court has squarely addressed parking permits, but the Earls factors favor the school: voluntary participation, a safety interest in impaired drivers, confidential results, and non-punitive consequences. Why not the others: B contradicts Vernonia and Earls. C ignores Earls's extension beyond athletes. D overstates school authority; even school searches require balancing or reasonable suspicion. Policy: Schools have special responsibility for students' safety. Critics argue that each extension of suspicionless testing conditions ordinary school life on surrendering privacy.

## ccp-10-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Objector lawfully arrested and removed before consent
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 11
Prompt: Responding to a report of an assault, officers see a bleeding woman, Mira Ostrow, at her door. Her partner, Lucan Hale, steps forward and says, "You're not coming in without a warrant." Officers lawfully arrest Hale for the assault on probable cause and drive him to the station. An hour later they return, and Ostrow, who lives there, consents to a search. They find a gun. Is the search valid?
A: No, because Hale's objection continues to bind the household while he is in custody.
B: No, because removing an objector to obtain another resident's consent always invalidates the consent.
C: Yes. A present co-occupant's objection overrides another's consent only while he is physically present. An objector who has been removed through a lawful arrest stands in the same position as one who is absent for any other reason, so Ostrow's consent controls.
D: Yes, because Hale lost all Fourth Amendment rights when he was arrested.
Answer: C
Authorities: Fernandez v. California; Georgia v. Randolph; United States v. Matlock
Explanation: Rule: Randolph's exception to co-occupant consent requires a physically present objector. Fernandez held that an occupant who was absent because of a lawful detention or arrest stands in the same shoes as an occupant who is absent for any other reason, so the remaining occupant may consent. Police may not remove an objector for the purpose of preventing an objection. Application: Hale was arrested on probable cause for a real assault, not to silence his objection. Once he was gone, Ostrow, with common authority (Matlock), could consent. Why not the others: A states the continuing-objection argument Fernandez rejected. B ignores that the removal was lawful and not pretextual. D overstates the effect of arrest. Policy: Fernandez provides a workable rule and protects co-occupants, often domestic violence victims, whose consent might otherwise be held hostage by an abuser's objection.

## ccp-10-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Scope of a hot-pursuit search after the suspect is caught
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 12
Prompt: Officers chasing an armed carjacker, Tobiah Rusk, see him run into his girlfriend's apartment. They follow within seconds, search the rooms for him and for the gun, and find and arrest him in a closet. With Rusk handcuffed and the gun found under the bed, an officer opens a small jewelry box on the dresser and finds stolen rings. Which items are admissible?
A: All items, because hot pursuit allows a full search of the home once officers are inside.
B: None, because officers could not enter a third party's home without a warrant.
C: Only the rings, because they were stolen property.
D: The gun, and Rusk himself, are admissible because hot pursuit justified entering and searching places where the suspect or a weapon could be. Opening the jewelry box after Rusk and the gun were secured went beyond the exigency.
Answer: D
Authorities: Warden v. Hayden; United States v. Santana; Michigan v. Fisher
Explanation: Rule: Hot pursuit of a fleeing felon permits a warrantless entry and a search for the suspect and for weapons that could be used against officers, in places where they might be found (Hayden; Santana). The search must be strictly limited to the exigency and end when it does. Application: The pursuit was immediate and continuous. Searching rooms and under the bed for Rusk and the gun was within its scope. After Rusk was cuffed and the gun recovered, nothing justified opening a jewelry box. Why not the others: A ignores scope limits. B ignores that hot pursuit can justify entry into a dwelling where the suspect flees; the third party's own privacy claims raise separate issues not affecting Rusk's case here. C has the analysis backward. Policy: Hot pursuit keeps suspects from escaping justice by running into a home. Scope limits prevent the pursuit from turning into a general search.

## ccp-10-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Bare anonymous tip of gun possession
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 13
Prompt: An anonymous caller, who will not give a name and whose number is blocked, tells police that a young man in a blue hoodie at the public library has a gun in his waistband. An officer arrives and sees Corin Wilde, in a blue hoodie, reading quietly. He makes no threatening moves and shows no bulge. The officer frisks him and finds a pistol. Was the frisk lawful?
A: No. A bare anonymous tip that a described person is carrying a gun, with no indication of how the caller knows and no predictive detail officers could verify, does not provide reasonable suspicion. There is no special exception for firearms.
B: Yes, because tips about guns are so serious that they justify a frisk without corroboration.
C: Yes, because the hoodie matched, which corroborated the tip.
D: Yes, because libraries are sensitive places where officers may frisk anyone on a tip.
Answer: A
Authorities: Florida v. J.L.; Terry v. Ohio; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: J.L. held that an anonymous tip that a person at a particular location wearing particular clothing is carrying a gun does not provide reasonable suspicion for a stop and frisk, because it shows no basis of knowledge or means of testing the tipster's credibility. The Court rejected a firearms exception. Reasonable suspicion requires that the tip be reliable in its assertion of illegality, not just in identifying a person. Application: This tracks J.L. The hoodie confirms only who the caller meant, not that he had a gun. Wilde did nothing suspicious. Why not the others: B states the exception J.L. rejected. C mistakes identification for corroboration of illegality. D invents a location rule. Policy: Without J.L.'s rule, anyone could subject another person to a police frisk with an anonymous phone call. Requiring reliability protects against harassment while allowing police to act on credible information.

## ccp-10-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Blocking a parked car as a seizure
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 14
Prompt: Isolde Warrick is sitting in her parked car in a nearly empty lot at night. Two patrol cars pull in, one directly in front of her bumper and one directly behind, turn on their spotlights and flashing lights, and two officers approach on either side. She stays put. Has she been seized?
A: No, because officers did not touch her.
B: No, because she could have walked away from the car.
C: No, because she never tried to leave, so there was no show of authority.
D: Yes. Boxing in her car, using flashing lights and spotlights, and approaching from both sides is a show of authority that would lead a reasonable person to believe she was not free to leave, and she submitted by staying put.
Answer: D
Authorities: United States v. Mendenhall; California v. Hodari D.
Explanation: Rule: A seizure by show of authority occurs when, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to leave (Mendenhall), and the person submits (Hodari D.). Relevant factors include the threatening presence of several officers, use of lights, and physical blocking of movement. Application: Blocking her car front and back, using emergency lights, and approaching from both sides communicate that she is being detained. Remaining in the car is submission. Why not the others: A confuses force and show of authority; touching is not required. B asks the wrong question; abandoning one's car is not a realistic option. C confuses submission with the show of authority; the officers made the show, and she submitted. Policy: The free-to-leave test lets police make consensual contact but recognizes when their conduct is in effect a detention requiring suspicion. That matters especially in situations where people feel they cannot safely refuse.

## ccp-10-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Police deception about evidence and voluntariness
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 15
Prompt: During a properly warned, 90-minute interview, a detective falsely tells Garrett Morn, a 38-year-old with a college degree, that his co-worker has already confessed and named him and that his fingerprints were found on the cash box. Neither is true. Morn waives and confesses to the theft. He argues the lies made his confession involuntary. What is the most likely ruling?
A: The confession is involuntary as a matter of law because police lied.
B: The confession is likely voluntary. Deception about evidence is a factor in the totality of the circumstances, but by itself it usually does not overbear the will of a capable adult in a short, properly warned interview without threats or promises.
C: The confession is involuntary because Morn was not told he could leave.
D: The confession is voluntary because Morn received warnings, and warnings always make a confession voluntary.
Answer: B
Authorities: Colorado v. Connelly; Brown v. Mississippi; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Voluntariness is judged by the totality of the circumstances, focusing on whether coercive police conduct overbore the suspect's will (Brown; Connelly). Deception is relevant but is weighed with factors such as length, conditions, threats or promises, and the suspect's characteristics. Miranda warnings do not settle voluntariness. Application: Morn is an educated adult, the interview was brief and warned, and there were no threats or promises. False claims about evidence, alone, rarely make a confession involuntary in that setting. Why not the others: A states a per se rule courts have not adopted. C confuses custody with voluntariness. D overstates the effect of warnings. Policy: Allowing some deception reflects the view that police need flexibility to obtain confessions from guilty suspects. Critics point to research linking false-evidence tactics to false confessions, especially among the young and vulnerable.

## ccp-10-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Testimonial questions during a sobriety test
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 16
Prompt: Ansel Corvo is under arrest for drunk driving and in custody at the station. Without warnings, the officer (1) asks his name, address, and date of birth for booking; (2) videotapes his slurred speech and unsteady walk; and (3) asks, "What was the date of your tenth birthday?" Corvo cannot answer. Which evidence is barred by Miranda?
A: All three, because all were obtained during custodial questioning.
B: Only the slurred speech and walk, because they show intoxication.
C: Only his inability to answer the birthday question. Booking questions fall within a routine-booking exception, and physical evidence such as slurred speech is not testimonial. The birthday question called for a testimonial answer whose content revealed impairment.
D: None, because sobriety testing is exempt from Miranda.
Answer: C
Authorities: Pennsylvania v. Muniz; Rhode Island v. Innis; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Miranda protects only testimonial communications made during custodial interrogation. Muniz held that slurred speech and lack of coordination are physical, not testimonial, evidence. Routine booking questions fall within an exception. A question about the date of a sixth birthday was testimonial, because the inability to answer revealed an incriminating mental state, and so required warnings. Application: The booking questions are routine. The videotaped speech and gait are physical evidence. The tenth-birthday question is the Muniz sixth-birthday question in substance. Why not the others: A ignores the testimonial requirement and the booking exception. B gets the physical/testimonial line backward. D ignores Muniz's holding on testimonial questions. Policy: The privilege protects against forcing people to reveal the contents of their minds, not against physical evidence. That line lets police gather physical proof while preserving the core of the privilege.

## ccp-10-q17
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Deliberate two-step interrogation without curative measures
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 17
Prompt: Following a deliberate department strategy, Detective Lyle questions Corinna Peake at the station for 40 minutes without warnings until she confesses to arson. After a 15-minute coffee break in the same room, Lyle gives warnings, gets a waiver, and says, "So let's go over what you told me before." She repeats the confession. Is the warned confession admissible?
A: Yes, because a second, warned confession is always admissible after a voluntary first one.
B: Yes, because the 15-minute break separated the two interrogations.
C: Yes, because the warnings were complete and accurate.
D: No. Police deliberately used a question-first technique, and the second session continued the first: same room, same detective, a short break, and an explicit reference to the earlier confession. Without curative measures, the midstream warnings could not function effectively, so the warned confession is excluded.
Answer: D
Authorities: Missouri v. Seibert; Oregon v. Elstad
Explanation: Rule: Seibert addressed a deliberate two-step technique. The plurality asked whether midstream warnings could effectively advise the suspect. Justice Kennedy's concurrence excludes warned statements when police deliberately used the two-step strategy and took no curative measures, such as a substantial break in time and circumstances or an explanation that the first statement was inadmissible. Elstad governs good-faith mistakes. Application: Everything here mirrors Seibert: a deliberate strategy, continuity of place and officer, a brief pause, and reference to the earlier confession, which signaled that she had already given it up. No curative measures. Why not the others: A ignores Seibert. B treats a brief break as curative. C ignores that adequate warnings can still fail to function in this context. Policy: Seibert prevents police from evading Miranda by design, which would turn the warnings into a formality after the confession has already been obtained.

## ccp-10-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Break in custody shorter than the required period
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 18
Prompt: Desmond Lark asks for a lawyer during questioning about a burglary, and questioning stops. He is released without charges and goes home. Ten days later, a different detective arrests him on new evidence, takes him to the station, gives fresh warnings, and asks to talk. He waives and confesses. He never received counsel and did not initiate the contact. Is the confession admissible?
A: Yes, because his release from custody ended his earlier invocation.
B: No. The protection that bars police-initiated questioning after a request for counsel ends only after a break in custody of at least 14 days. Ten days is not enough, so his waiver of police-initiated questioning is presumed involuntary.
C: Yes, because a different detective conducted the second interrogation.
D: No, because a suspect who has once asked for a lawyer can never be questioned again.
Answer: B
Authorities: Maryland v. Shatzer; Edwards v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Edwards bars police-initiated interrogation after a request for counsel unless counsel is present or the suspect initiates. Shatzer held that the Edwards presumption ends after a break in Miranda custody lasting at least 14 days, enough time for the suspect to return to normal life, consult friends and counsel, and shake off any coercive effects. Application: Lark was released for only ten days before police initiated contact, so the Edwards presumption still applied. Why not the others: A ignores Shatzer's 14-day requirement. C is irrelevant, because Edwards bars all police-initiated custodial interrogation, by any officer, about any crime. D overstates Edwards. Policy: Shatzer gives police and courts a clear rule while protecting suspects for a period long enough for the pressure of custody to fade.

## ccp-10-q19
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reliability factors when there is a long delay
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 19
Prompt: Eight months after a home invasion, police show the victim, Selene Hart, a single photo of Bram Udell, telling her, "We think this is the guy." During the crime, Hart had looked at the intruder's unmasked face under bright kitchen lights for several minutes while he demanded jewelry, and she gave a detailed description that matched Udell. On seeing the photo, she says she is "absolutely certain." Is the identification admissible?
A: No, because a single-photo display is always excluded.
B: No, because an eight-month delay automatically defeats reliability.
C: Likely yes, though it is close. The single photo with a suggestive comment was unnecessarily suggestive, but under the totality of the circumstances, her prolonged, close, well-lit view, her attention, her accurate detailed description, and her certainty may outweigh the long delay and the suggestion.
D: Yes, because suggestiveness matters only at live lineups.
Answer: C
Authorities: Neil v. Biggers; Manson v. Brathwaite
Explanation: Rule: Even when an identification procedure is unnecessarily suggestive, the identification is admissible if reliable under the totality of the circumstances. Courts weigh the opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of the prior description, level of certainty, and time between the crime and the confrontation against the corrupting effect (Biggers; Manson). Biggers admitted an identification made seven months after the crime because the other factors were strong. Application: The procedure was clearly suggestive. But Hart's view was long, close, and well lit, she was attentive, and her description was accurate. The eight-month delay weighs against reliability, and her certainty may be inflated by the suggestion. Courts would likely admit it, as in Biggers. Why not the others: A states a per se rule Manson rejected. B treats one factor as dispositive. D is wrong because due process screening covers photo displays too. Policy: The reliability approach admits identifications likely to be accurate despite flawed procedures. Critics argue that certainty is the least trustworthy factor because suggestive procedures inflate it.

## ccp-10-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Good faith fails for a bare-bones affidavit
Set: CCP-10
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Brannock County
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 7, Question 20
Prompt: Detective Ondine Rusk's affidavit states only: "Affiant has received reliable information and has reason to believe that illegal drugs are present at 9 Fennel Road." It gives no source, no facts, and no corroboration. A magistrate signs the warrant, and the search finds cocaine. The prosecution argues good faith. What result?
A: Suppress. Good faith does not apply when an affidavit is so lacking in indicia of probable cause that belief in its existence is entirely unreasonable. A wholly conclusory, bare-bones affidavit gives a reasonable officer no basis to rely on the magistrate's decision.
B: Admit the evidence, because officers may always rely on a magistrate's approval.
C: Admit the evidence, because the drugs were in fact there, which proves the affidavit was accurate.
D: Suppress, because good faith never applies to drug cases.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Leon; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: Leon admits evidence obtained in objectively reasonable reliance on a warrant, but not when (1) the affiant misled the magistrate, (2) the magistrate abandoned the judicial role, (3) the affidavit is so lacking in indicia of probable cause that belief in its existence is entirely unreasonable, or (4) the warrant is facially deficient. Gates condemns "bare bones" affidavits that state only conclusions. Application: Rusk's affidavit is a pure conclusion with no facts. A reasonable, well-trained officer would know it cannot establish probable cause. Why not the others: B ignores Leon's exceptions. C judges the warrant by its results, which is never the test. D invents a crime-based rule. Policy: Leon rests on deterrence: punishing a magistrate's error does little good. A bare-bones affidavit is police failure, and suppression there gives officers an incentive to provide real facts.
