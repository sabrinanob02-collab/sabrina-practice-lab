## ccp-05-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Aerial surveillance by low-hovering drone over curtilage
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 1
Prompt: Tallow Creek police suspect that Mireille Dacosta is growing illegal plants behind her house. Her backyard is surrounded by an eight-foot privacy fence. Without a warrant, an officer flies a small camera drone over the yard. It hovers about 30 feet above the yard for six minutes on each of five separate days, recording high-definition video. Nobody else's drones or aircraft fly that low over the neighborhood, and the video shows the plants. Is this most likely a search?
A: No. The airspace is public, and anything visible from any point in the air has been knowingly exposed to the public.
B: Yes, because any observation of curtilage by any means is a search.
C: Likely yes, or at least Dacosta has the stronger argument. The aerial-observation cases rested on observation from the altitude and in the manner that members of the public routinely fly. Repeated low-altitude hovering over a fenced yard, with a recording device, is not a vantage point the public ordinarily uses, and it intrudes on the curtilage's core privacy.
D: No, because a homeowner has no legitimate expectation of privacy in a backyard that can be seen from above.
Answer: C
Authorities: California v. Ciraolo; Kyllo v. United States; United States v. Dunn; Katz v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Naked-eye observation of curtilage from public navigable airspace, at altitudes and in the manner the public routinely uses, is not a search (Ciraolo). Kyllo treats technology not in general public use as relevant. A fenced backyard next to the home is classic curtilage (Dunn factors). Application: The aerial cases assumed the public could see what the officer saw. A drone hovering 30 feet over a fenced yard, repeatedly and with recording, is a vantage the public does not routinely use. That brings Dacosta's case closer to the protected core. Courts have not settled drone surveillance, but her argument is stronger. Why not the others: A treats Ciraolo as a rule about all airspace, ignoring its emphasis on routine public use. B is too broad, because Ciraolo shows curtilage is not shielded from every observation. D contradicts the curtilage doctrine. Policy: The aerial cases do not ask police to shield their eyes from what any air traveler sees. Applying them to cheap, hovering drones would erase backyard privacy, a central part of the home's protection, and allow persistent neighborhood surveillance.

## ccp-05-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Investigative genetic genealogy and abandoned DNA
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 2
Prompt: Investigators upload a DNA profile from a cold-case crime scene to a public genealogy website whose users opted in to allowing law enforcement matching. The site flags a likely second cousin of the suspect. Through public records, detectives narrow their attention to Ansel Brightwater. They follow him to a cafe, wait until he throws his coffee cup into a public trash can, retrieve the cup, and test it. It matches. Which statement best describes the Fourth Amendment analysis under the assigned cases?
A: Probably no search occurred at either step. The genealogy match used profiles that relatives voluntarily shared with a third party, not Brightwater's own data, and he abandoned the cup in a public place. His best counterargument is that DNA reveals far more than ordinary third-party data.
B: Both steps were searches, because collecting DNA is always a search, as with booking swabs.
C: Both steps were searches, because the government may never obtain information held by any third party without a warrant.
D: The genealogy step was a search, because Brightwater shares genes with his cousin and so has a legitimate expectation of privacy in the cousin's profile.
Answer: A
Authorities: Smith v. Maryland; California v. Greenwood; Carpenter v. United States; Maryland v. King; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Information voluntarily conveyed to third parties is generally unprotected (Smith). Items left in public, such as curbside trash, are exposed and abandoned (Greenwood). Carpenter carved out a narrow exception for comprehensive location records. King treated a cheek swab of an arrestee as a search, though a reasonable one. Fourth Amendment rights are personal (Rakas). Application: The uploaded profiles belonged to relatives who chose to share them. Brightwater has no personal Fourth Amendment interest in a cousin's profile. He discarded the cup in a public bin. His strongest argument is that DNA is uniquely revealing, a Carpenter-style claim courts have not resolved. Why not the others: B misreads King, which involved a physical swab of the person. Here nothing touched Brightwater. C overreads Carpenter, which called itself narrow. D conflicts with the rule that rights are personal. Policy: Genealogy searches have solved violent cold cases. Critics warn they turn every relative's choice into a waiver for the entire family and could build a genetic surveillance network without legislative safeguards.

## ccp-05-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Implied license to approach the home: route and purpose
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 3
Prompt: At 3 a.m., Officer Brandt goes to Rufus Kell's house to "knock and talk" about a stolen generator. Before going to the front door, he walks through a side gate into the narrow side yard, which can be reached only through that gate, and shines a flashlight through a bedroom window. He sees the generator. Then he goes to the front door and knocks. Was there a search?
A: No. Officers have the same license as any visitor to approach a home and knock, at any hour and by any route.
B: No, because the side yard is an open field.
C: No, because an officer's purpose in approaching a home never matters to whether a search occurred.
D: Yes. The implied license lets a visitor approach the front door by the normal path, knock, wait briefly, and leave. Going through a side gate at 3 a.m. to peer into a bedroom window exceeds that license's route and purpose, so it was a physical intrusion on curtilage to gather information.
Answer: D
Authorities: Florida v. Jardines; United States v. Jones; United States v. Dunn; Oliver v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under the trespass approach, a physical intrusion on a constitutionally protected area, including curtilage, to obtain information is a search (Jones). Jardines recognized an implied license for visitors, including police, to approach the front door, knock, wait briefly, and leave, but that license is limited in area and purpose. Application: A side yard reachable only through a gate, right next to the bedroom, is curtilage under the Dunn factors. The license extends to the front path, not side yards. The 3 a.m. timing and the window-peering show a purpose no ordinary visitor's license covers. Why not the others: A ignores Jardines's limits. B misapplies open fields, which covers land beyond the curtilage (Oliver). C overstates the point. Subjective motive usually does not matter, but Jardines looked at whether the officer's conduct, viewed objectively, fit the license, and peering into windows does not. Policy: The implied license reflects social norms everyone understands: you may knock at the front door, but you may not prowl the side yard at night. Linking police authority to those norms protects the home without forbidding ordinary investigative contact.

## ccp-05-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Challenging warrant affidavits: falsehoods and omissions
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 4
Prompt: Detective Oyelaran's affidavit says that a "reliable informant who has provided accurate information in the past" saw stolen tools in Hollis Grange's shed. In fact, the informant had never given police information before, and the detective knew it. The affidavit also leaves out that the informant had a pending theft charge and was promised leniency. Grange makes a detailed offer of proof showing both facts. What should happen?
A: Nothing. Grange must prove that the informant, not the detective, lied, because only an informant's falsehoods matter.
B: Grange is entitled to a hearing. If he proves by a preponderance that the detective deliberately or recklessly included the false reliability claim and left out material facts, the court sets aside the falsehood, adds the omitted facts, and asks whether what remains still establishes probable cause.
C: The warrant is void only if Grange shows the detective was negligent in checking the informant's history.
D: The warrant is void because the affidavit contained an inaccuracy. Any inaccuracy in an affidavit invalidates a warrant.
Answer: B
Authorities: Franks v. Delaware; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: A defendant who makes a substantial preliminary showing that the affiant knowingly and intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth, included a false statement necessary to the finding of probable cause is entitled to a hearing (Franks). If he proves this by a preponderance, the falsehood is set aside. Courts extend the same logic to deliberate or reckless material omissions. If the corrected affidavit lacks probable cause, the warrant is void and the evidence is suppressed. Application: The affiant knew the informant had no track record, which is a deliberate falsehood. The omitted pending charge and leniency deal bear directly on veracity. Without the false track record and with the omissions added, a first-time, motivated informant's word alone may not establish probable cause under Gates. Why not the others: A has it backwards; Franks targets the affiant's falsity, not the informant's. C and D overstate the remedy; innocent or negligent errors do not trigger Franks. Policy: The warrant process depends on the magistrate getting truthful information. Franks deters police from manipulating it, while the high threshold protects against routine evidentiary hearings attacking every affidavit.

## ccp-05-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Detaining occupants during warrant execution: immediate vicinity
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 5
Prompt: Officers are preparing to execute a warrant to search Lucia Ferrand's apartment for counterfeit cash. Watching from down the street, they see Ferrand leave and drive away. They let her drive a mile before stopping her car, handcuff her, and drive her back to watch the search. They had no specific suspicion about her beyond the warrant for the apartment. Was the detention lawful?
A: No. The categorical authority to detain occupants during a warrant search is limited to the immediate vicinity of the premises. Stopping her a mile away needed some independent justification, such as reasonable suspicion or probable cause specific to her.
B: Yes, because a warrant to search a place carries categorical authority to detain its occupants wherever they are found.
C: Yes, because handcuffing her for officer safety made the detention reasonable.
D: No, because police may never detain occupants while executing a search warrant.
Answer: A
Authorities: Michigan v. Summers; Bailey v. United States; Muehler v. Mena
Explanation: Rule: Officers executing a search warrant may detain occupants found on the premises without individualized suspicion. That authority serves officer safety, facilitates the search, and prevents flight (Summers; Muehler, which permitted handcuffing during a lengthy search). Bailey limits the categorical authority to the immediate vicinity of the premises. Application: Ferrand was a mile away when stopped. The interests behind categorical detention, such as stopping interference with the search, do not apply to someone who has driven away. Her stop needed justification under ordinary rules. Why not the others: B ignores Bailey's spatial limit. C confuses how a detention is carried out with whether it is authorized; handcuffing cannot justify a stop that lacked authority to begin with. D contradicts Summers and Muehler. Policy: Categorical detention is tolerated because it is closely tied to the search and its safety concerns. A clear spatial limit keeps the warrant from becoming a roving license to seize people anywhere.

## ccp-05-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Probable cause for multiple occupants: rideshare variation
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 6
Prompt: At 2 a.m., an officer stops a rideshare car for a broken taillight. It carries a professional driver and two passengers who were matched separately through the app and do not know each other. With the driver's consent, the officer finds five baggies of cocaine tucked into the rear seat pocket, which both passengers could reach. All three deny ownership. Does the officer have probable cause to arrest all three?
A: Yes, because whenever drugs are found in a car where all occupants could reach them, there is automatically probable cause to arrest everyone inside.
B: No, because being close to contraband can never contribute to probable cause.
C: Probably not as to all three. Arresting every occupant rests on a reasonable inference of a common enterprise among people sharing a small car late at night. Strangers matched by an app, plus a commercial driver, weaken that inference, so the officer needs more facts tying each person to the drugs.
D: Yes as to the driver only, because whoever is driving a car always has constructive possession of everything in it.
Answer: C
Authorities: Maryland v. Pringle; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause is a practical, totality-of-the-circumstances judgment, particularized to each person seized. Pringle found probable cause to arrest all three occupants of a small car where cocaine was accessible to all, because it was reasonable to infer a common enterprise among them, given the quantity and dealing context. Application: Pringle depended on that inference. Strangers who share a rideshare, driven by a professional who carries many riders, lack the relationship that makes a joint-dealing inference reasonable. The passengers near the seat pocket are closer cases; the driver is weakest. Why not the others: A turns Pringle's fact-specific inference into a per se rule. B goes too far; proximity is relevant, just not always sufficient. D invents a rule, and a commercial driver is the least likely to know what a passenger hid. Policy: Particularized suspicion protects people from guilt by association. Pringle's inference serves effective drug enforcement in cars, but extending it to commercial transportation would expose countless riders and drivers to arrest for other people's contraband.

## ccp-05-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Warrantless arrest at the threshold; retreat into the home
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 7
Prompt: Officers have probable cause, but no warrant, to arrest Odette Lavelle for a felony armed robbery that happened an hour earlier. They pull up as she stands in the open doorway of her house, fully visible from the street. When they shout "Police!", she steps back into the front hall. They follow her inside and arrest her there. Was the arrest lawful?
A: No, because officers may never enter a home to arrest someone without a warrant.
B: Yes. Standing in the open doorway, she was in a public place for arrest purposes. Officers with probable cause could begin an arrest there, and her retreat inside could not defeat it; the pursuit justified following her.
C: No, because officers need a warrant to arrest anyone for a felony unless they personally saw the crime.
D: Yes, because officers needed only reasonable suspicion to make the arrest.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Santana; Payton v. New York; United States v. Watson; Warden v. Hayden
Explanation: Rule: Police may make a warrantless felony arrest in a public place on probable cause (Watson). Payton requires a warrant to enter a home for a routine felony arrest. Santana treated a suspect standing in her open doorway as in a public place. Her retreat inside, once police had set the arrest in motion, did not defeat it, because the brief pursuit was hot pursuit. Application: Lavelle was visible in the doorway, and the officers had probable cause for a serious felony. She retreated in direct response to them. This is the Santana pattern. Why not the others: A ignores the hot pursuit exception. C misstates the arrest rules: officers may arrest for a felony on probable cause without seeing it, and the in-presence requirement applies to misdemeanors. D lowers the standard; arrest requires probable cause. Policy: Santana keeps a suspect from defeating a lawful arrest by stepping back across the threshold, while Payton preserves the home's protection against planned, routine warrantless entries.

## ccp-05-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Vehicle searches incident to arrest after the arrestee is secured
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 8
Prompt: Bastian Ojo is arrested for driving on a suspended license, handcuffed, and locked in the back of a patrol car. Officers then search his car's passenger compartment "incident to arrest" and find a handgun under the seat. Department policy would have required the car to be towed and inventoried, because it was parked in a tow-away zone with no one to drive it. Is the gun admissible?
A: Yes, because arresting any recent occupant of a car automatically authorizes a search of its passenger compartment.
B: Yes, because a gun could have been in the car, so officer safety justified the search.
C: Yes, because evidence of a driving offense can always be found in a car.
D: The search was not valid as a search incident to arrest, because Ojo was secured and it was not reasonable to believe evidence of a suspended-license offense was in the car. The gun may still come in if the prosecution shows it would have been found in a lawful, standardized inventory after impoundment.
Answer: D
Authorities: Arizona v. Gant; Chimel v. California; Colorado v. Bertine; Nix v. Williams
Explanation: Rule: Under Gant, police may search a vehicle incident to a recent occupant's arrest only if (1) the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the passenger compartment, or (2) it is reasonable to believe evidence of the crime of arrest is in the vehicle. Inventory searches are valid when made under standardized procedures after lawful impoundment (Bertine). Inevitable discovery admits evidence the prosecution proves would have been found lawfully (Nix). Application: Ojo was handcuffed in a patrol car, and a suspended license leaves no evidence in a car. The search-incident rationale fails. But mandatory towing and an inventory policy make an inevitable-discovery argument available. Why not the others: A states the broad pre-Gant reading that Gant rejected. B ignores that Ojo was secured. C fails because some offenses, such as a suspended license, leave no physical evidence. Policy: Gant ties the exception back to its Chimel justifications, safety and evidence preservation, preventing routine traffic arrests from becoming licenses for full car searches. Inevitable discovery avoids putting the government in a worse position than it would have been in without the violation.

## ccp-05-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Automobile exception: probable cause limited to one container
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 9
Prompt: Officers watch Mercer Quill buy a red cooler from a man they know sells stolen prescription pills, then put the cooler in his trunk and drive off. They have probable cause that the pills are in the cooler, but no other reason to suspect anything else in the car. They stop him, open the trunk, and search the cooler, finding pills. They then search the glove box and find a ledger. What is admissible?
A: Both, because probable cause to search any part of a car allows a search of the whole car.
B: Neither, because officers need a warrant to open a closed container even when they have probable cause.
C: Neither, because containers in cars may be searched only incident to arrest.
D: The pills only. Probable cause tied to a specific container in a car allows a warrantless search of that container, but not of the rest of the car.
Answer: D
Authorities: California v. Acevedo; United States v. Ross; Carroll v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under the automobile exception, the scope of a warrantless search is defined by the object of the search and the places where there is probable cause to believe it may be found. With probable cause for the whole car, police may search the entire vehicle and any container that could hold the object (Ross). With probable cause only for a particular container, they may search that container but not the rest of the vehicle (Acevedo). Application: The officers' probable cause pointed to the red cooler. Opening the cooler was lawful. Searching the glove box went beyond the probable cause they had. Why not the others: A ignores Acevedo's scope limit. B restates the container-warrant rule Acevedo abandoned. C confuses search incident to arrest with the automobile exception. Policy: Acevedo adopted one clear rule for containers in cars, so the outcome does not depend on whether police had probable cause for the car or for the container. Linking the scope to probable cause still keeps a specific suspicion from becoming a general search of the vehicle.

## ccp-05-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Special needs: suspicionless testing shared with police
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 10
Prompt: Tallow Creek State University requires students who live in its voluntary honors residence hall to submit to random, suspicionless urine drug tests. Under a written agreement, positive results go to campus police, who use them to file possession charges. The policy statement calls the program "a tool for identifying and prosecuting campus drug users." Is the program a valid special-needs search?
A: Probably not. A special-needs search must serve a primary purpose beyond ordinary crime control, but this program's stated and structural aim is to produce evidence for prosecution. Voluntarily joining the residence hall does not save a program whose immediate objective is law enforcement.
B: Yes, because students who choose an optional program have reduced privacy expectations, which validates any testing program.
C: Yes, because universities, like elementary and high schools, act in place of parents and may test students for any purpose.
D: No, because special-needs searches are allowed only in elementary and secondary schools.
Answer: A
Authorities: Vernonia School District v. Acton; Board of Education v. Earls; Ferguson v. City of Charleston; New Jersey v. T.L.O.
Explanation: Rule: Suspicionless searches can be valid when they serve special needs beyond normal law enforcement, weighed against the privacy intrusion (Vernonia; Earls, which upheld testing of students in extracurricular activities). But when the program's immediate objective or primary purpose is to generate evidence for prosecution, special needs fails (Ferguson, where a hospital shared drug-test results with police). Application: Here the program's text and the arrangement with police show that prosecution is its purpose. Voluntary participation lowered expectations in Earls, but it cannot convert law enforcement into a special need. Why not the others: B treats voluntariness as dispositive. C extends the school custodial-care rationale to adult university students and ignores the purpose problem. D is too narrow; special needs applies in other contexts, such as hospitals and regulated employment. Policy: The special-needs doctrine allows balancing where traditional rules do not fit the government's protective role. Preventing police from borrowing that doctrine keeps a needed exception from swallowing the probable-cause requirement.

## ccp-05-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consent obtained through a fake emergency
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 11
Prompt: Lacking probable cause, an undercover officer dressed as a gas-utility worker knocks on Imogen Strell's door and says, "We've detected a gas leak in this building. I need to come in right now to check your lines or it could explode." Strell lets him in. He walks through her rooms with a fake meter and notices a stolen painting on her wall. Is her consent valid?
A: Yes, because a person who invites someone in takes the risk that the visitor is a police officer.
B: Probably not. Voluntariness depends on the totality of the circumstances, including deception. A lie that invents an emergency and leaves the occupant no real choice is different from an undercover officer who is invited in for the very purpose he appears to have.
C: Yes, because officers need not tell anyone they have the right to refuse.
D: No, because officers must always identify themselves before asking for consent.
Answer: B
Authorities: Schneckloth v. Bustamonte; Lewis v. United States; Hoffa v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances (Schneckloth). Relevant factors include coercion, claims of authority, and deception. Knowledge of the right to refuse is relevant but not required. The misplaced-trust cases (Hoffa; Lewis) hold that a person who invites an undercover agent in for a transaction assumes the risk that the agent will report what he sees within the scope of the invitation. Application: Strell did not choose to trust a visitor for a purpose she understood. She was frightened by a fake life-threatening emergency into admitting a supposed safety inspector. That deception removes meaningful choice. Many courts treat consent obtained through a fabricated emergency as involuntary. Why not the others: A misapplies misplaced trust, which covers invitations for the purpose the agent appears to have. C is true as far as it goes but does not answer the deception problem. D invents a rule; undercover work is lawful. Policy: Courts allow ordinary undercover deception because it does not override free choice. Fake emergencies exploit people's need to stay safe and would undermine trust in real emergency responders.

## ccp-05-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Hot pursuit: broken continuity
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 12
Prompt: Officers chase Dashiell Morrow after an armed convenience-store robbery but lose sight of him in a park. Twenty-five minutes later, tracking data from the stolen phone shows it inside a house on Wren Street, and a neighbor says a man ran in "a while ago." Officers do not seek a warrant. They kick in the door, find Morrow, and seize the cash. Was the warrantless entry justified by hot pursuit?
A: Yes, because probable cause that a felon is inside a home is always enough to enter without a warrant.
B: Yes, because a suspect who flees creates his own exigency and loses Fourth Amendment protection wherever he goes.
C: Probably not. Hot pursuit requires an immediate and continuous chase. Losing him for 25 minutes and finding him later through data broke the pursuit, so the entry needed a warrant unless some other, specific exigency made getting one impracticable.
D: Yes, because the seriousness of the felony excuses any amount of delay.
Answer: C
Authorities: Warden v. Hayden; United States v. Santana; Payton v. New York; Michigan v. Fisher
Explanation: Rule: Exigent circumstances excuse a warrant only when an emergency makes getting one impracticable. The search must be limited to the exigency, and probable cause is still required. Hot pursuit applies to an immediate or continuous pursuit of a fleeing suspect, considering the continuity, speed, and timing of the chase (Hayden; Santana). Application: The chase ended in the park. A 25-minute gap followed by data-driven detective work is investigation, not pursuit. Officers would need specific facts, such as an imminent threat to someone inside or imminent destruction of evidence, for another exigency. They could also have secured the house while seeking a warrant. Why not the others: A contradicts Payton, which requires a warrant to enter a home even with probable cause. B states an extreme version that no case adopts. D ignores the continuity requirement. Policy: Exigency is a narrow necessity exception. Once officers have time to deliberate, the reasons for skipping the neutral magistrate disappear, and the home's protection returns.

## ccp-05-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reasonable suspicion from an automated gunshot alert vs. an anonymous tip
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 13
Prompt: At 1:40 a.m., Tallow Creek's acoustic gunshot-detection system reports three shots at Sixth and Rook. An officer arrives within 60 seconds and sees only one person nearby, Corin Vaile, walking quickly away from the intersection and glancing back. The officer stops him. Was the stop supported by reasonable suspicion?
A: Likely yes. An automated report of a crime that just happened, together with close proximity in time and place and the fact that Vaile was the only person there and was moving away quickly, gives specific, articulable facts. A bare anonymous tip of concealed gun possession lacks those indicators.
B: No, because anonymous information can never support a stop.
C: No, because stopping someone suspected of having a gun requires probable cause.
D: Yes, because presence in a high-crime area late at night is enough by itself.
Answer: A
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Florida v. J.L.; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: A Terry stop requires reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot, judged on the totality of the circumstances, including the reliability of the source. In J.L., a bare anonymous tip that a person at a bus stop had a gun was not enough, because it showed no basis of knowledge or predictive detail and alleged only concealed possession. Application: Whether gunshot-detection systems are reliable enough is debated, but here the alert reported a crime that had just occurred at a precise place. The officer then saw corroborating facts: one person, seconds later, moving quickly away and looking back. That is more than a bare tip. Why not the others: B overstates J.L., which did not bar all anonymous information. C imposes the wrong standard. D contradicts the rule that a high-crime area alone is not enough. Policy: Courts allow stops on reasonable suspicion so police can respond quickly to fresh violent crime. Reliability requirements guard against stops based on unverified accusations or automated errors that burden innocent people in heavily monitored neighborhoods.

## ccp-05-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Protective sweep scope
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 14
Prompt: Officers execute an arrest warrant for Tamsin Loake, wanted for armed robbery, and arrest her in her living room. A reliable informant had told them that two armed accomplices "often sleep in her basement." Officers go to the basement, look behind the furnace and in a closet, and find no one. One officer then opens a small dresser drawer and finds a bag of stolen jewelry. Is the jewelry admissible?
A: No, because a protective sweep may never extend beyond the room where the arrest happened.
B: Yes, because a protective sweep may include searching for evidence of the crime of arrest.
C: Yes, because weapons could be hidden in a drawer, so officers may search wherever a weapon could be.
D: No. Specific facts suggesting dangerous accomplices in the basement could justify a quick sweep there, but a sweep is a cursory look at places where a person could hide. Opening a small drawer exceeded that scope.
Answer: D
Authorities: Maryland v. Buie; Chimel v. California
Explanation: Rule: Incident to an in-home arrest, officers may, without suspicion, look in closets and spaces immediately next to the place of arrest from which an attack could be launched. Going further requires articulable facts that the area harbors a dangerous person. Either way, a sweep is a quick, cursory visual inspection of places where a person might hide, and it lasts no longer than needed to dispel the danger (Buie). Application: The informant's statement supplied articulable facts for the basement. Looking behind the furnace and in the closet fits. A small drawer cannot hold a person. Why not the others: A ignores Buie's second category. B confuses a sweep, which is about people, with an evidence search. C extends Buie into a weapons search; Chimel limits searches for weapons to the arrestee's grab area. Policy: Buie lets officers protect themselves from ambush during dangerous arrests, while the "people, not things" limit keeps arrests from becoming pretexts for searching the home.

Topic: Due process voluntariness: mental illness without police coercion
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 15
Prompt: Elowen Brisk, who has untreated schizophrenia, walks into the Tallow Creek police station. She tells the desk officer that "the voices" ordered her to confess to a burglary from last spring and then describes it in detail. The officer asks no questions and simply writes down what she says. She later moves to suppress the statement as involuntary under the Due Process Clause. What result?
A: Suppress, because a confession by a person with a serious mental illness is involuntary as a matter of law.
B: Suppress, because she received no warnings before speaking.
C: Deny the motion as to due process. Due process involuntariness requires coercive police activity, and the officer did nothing to overbear her will. Concerns about reliability belong to the rules of evidence and to the jury.
D: Suppress, because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel bars any statement made without counsel.
Answer: C
Authorities: Colorado v. Connelly; Brown v. Mississippi; Miranda v. Arizona; Massiah v. United States
Explanation: Rule: A confession violates due process if, under the totality of the circumstances, it was involuntary because coercive police conduct overbore the suspect's will (Brown v. Topic: Functional equivalent of interrogation: targeted appeals
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 16
Prompt: Rowan Pike has invoked his right to remain silent after his arrest for a hit-and-run that killed a child. Driving him to jail, an officer who knows Pike has a daughter the same age as the victim plays a news clip of the victim's mother sobbing. He then says to his partner, "Imagine never knowing what happened to your little girl." Pike blurts out, "I didn't see her until it was too late." Was this interrogation?
A: No, because the officer's remark was addressed to his partner, not to Pike.
B: Likely yes. Interrogation includes words or actions the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Playing the clip and making a remark aimed at Pike's known vulnerability as a parent goes beyond the offhand exchange in the leading case.
C: No, because Pike's statement was spontaneous and volunteered.
D: No, because only express questioning can be interrogation.
Answer: B
Authorities: Rhode Island v. Innis; Miranda v. Arizona; Michigan v. Mosley
Explanation: Rule: Interrogation means express questioning or its functional equivalent, meaning words or actions (other than those normally attendant to arrest and custody) that police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. ## ccp-05-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Inevitable discovery and the "we would have gotten a warrant" argument
Set: CCP-05
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Tallow Creek
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 2, Question 20
Prompt: Detectives have probable cause to search Ozias Penhale's phone but do not seek a warrant. They search it anyway and find a map pin marking where a stolen safe is buried in the woods. At the suppression hearing, the prosecution argues the safe is admissible because "we had probable cause and certainly would have gotten a warrant." No warrant application was ever started. What is the best answer?
A: Most courts would reject the argument. Inevitable discovery requires proof by a preponderance that lawful means actually underway would have found the evidence. Letting the bare existence of probable cause for an unsought warrant satisfy it would effectively erase the warrant requirement.
B: Admissible, because probable cause existed, so a warrant was inevitable.
C: Admissible under the independent source doctrine, because probable cause was an independent source.
D: Admissible because of attenuation, since enough time passed between the search and the digging.
Answer: A
Authorities: Nix v. Williams; Segura v. United States; Murray v. United States; Brown v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Inevitable discovery admits evidence if the prosecution proves by a preponderance that it ultimately or inevitably would have been discovered by lawful means (Nix, where an organized search party was closing in on the body). The independent source doctrine admits evidence actually obtained through a lawful source independent of the illegality (Segura). Attenuation asks whether the connection has become so weak that the taint dissipates (Brown). Application: Nothing lawful was underway. Courts commonly reject "we would have gotten a warrant" when officers never sought one, because otherwise the doctrine would excuse every warrantless search backed by probable cause. Why not the others: B converts probable cause into inevitability. C misapplies independent source, which requires an actual lawful acquisition, not a hypothetical one. D ignores that the map pin led directly to the safe; time alone, with no intervening circumstances, does not attenuate. Policy: Inevitable discovery puts the government in the same, not a better, position than it would have been in without the violation. Allowing hypothetical warrants would remove any incentive to get one.
