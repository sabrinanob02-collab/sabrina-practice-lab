## clii-01-q01

Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 2 (Aug. 20, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: A county archive declines to hire applicants who are enrolled in a legally supervised medication program for a prior substance dependency, citing concerns about reliability around sensitive records. A rejected applicant sues, arguing the policy is arbitrary. Assuming no suspect classification or fundamental right is at stake, what must the county establish for the policy to survive?
A: That the policy serves a legitimate government interest and that the classification is rationally related to achieving it.
B: That the policy is the single most effective method available to protect the archive's records.
C: That every applicant excluded by the policy would, in fact, pose a real risk.
D: That the legislature considered and rejected every less restrictive alternative before adopting the policy.
Answer: A
Authorities: Rational basis two-prong test
Explanation: Under rational basis review, a classification survives if it serves a legitimate government interest and is rationally related to achieving that interest; the government need not choose the best or only available method. B and D import a narrow-tailoring requirement that belongs to heightened scrutiny, not rational basis, and C demands individualized accuracy that the deferential standard does not require.

## clii-01-q02

Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 2 (Aug. 20, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 2
Prompt: A city ordinance allows businesses to display advertising for their own products on their own vehicles, but bars any vehicle from displaying paid advertising for someone else's business. A challenger argues the line the city drew is arbitrary and does not really track its stated traffic-safety interest. How does rational basis review typically treat this kind of legislative line-drawing?
A: The court independently investigates the traffic-safety data before accepting any legislative judgment.
B: Courts generally presume the facts on which the classification rests are true and defer to a reasonably conceivable legislative judgment, allowing government to address a problem one step at a time rather than all at once.
C: Any distinction that fails to treat identically situated advertisers exactly alike is automatically struck down.
D: The classification is invalid unless the city can show it was the actual, sole motivation of every council member who voted for it.
Answer: B
Authorities: Actual-purpose review; step-by-step legislative regulation
Explanation: Rational basis review affords a strong presumption in favor of the facts underlying a classification and tolerates incremental, step-by-step regulation rather than requiring government to solve an entire problem at once. A demands a level of judicial fact-finding the deferential standard does not perform, C ignores the tolerance for imperfect fit built into the standard, and D asks for a subjective-motive showing far beyond what rational basis requires.

## clii-01-q03
Course: Constitutional Law II
Topic: Historical due-process arguments for slavery
Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 3 (Aug. 25, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 3
Prompt: An early Supreme Court decision reasoned in part that freeing an enslaved person taken into a free territory would deprive the owner of property without due process, and treated the Constitution's structure as confirming that enslaved people could not be citizens entitled to sue in federal court. What is the strongest criticism of that due-process reasoning?
A: It correctly balanced individual liberty against government efficiency.
B: It was procedurally sound because the owner received a hearing before any deprivation.
C: It reasoned backward from the Constitution's toleration of slavery to treat a human being as property, rather than questioning whether a person could be property at all.
D: It applied the correct heightened-scrutiny standard but reached the wrong result on the facts.
Answer: C
Authorities: Historical due-process reasoning treating a person as property
Explanation: The central flaw was treating the premise as given rather than examined: the reasoning simply assumed a person could be property and then applied ordinary property-deprivation logic to that assumption, rather than confronting whether the underlying premise was legitimate at all. A mischaracterizes the reasoning as a defensible balancing act, B focuses on a procedural question the criticism does not turn on, and D anachronistically imports a scrutiny framework that did not yet exist and misses the deeper problem with the premise itself.

## clii-01-q04
Course: Constitutional Law II
Topic: Political rights versus social rights framing
Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 3 (Aug. 25, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 4
Prompt: A state statute required separate railway cars for different races but described the cars as equal in quality. A challenger argued this violated the Fourteenth Amendment. In upholding the statute, the Court distinguished between two categories of rights, treating only one category as protected against this kind of state-mandated separation. Which distinction did the Court draw?
A: A distinction between rights held by citizens and rights held by residents.
B: A distinction between rights enforceable in state court and rights enforceable in federal court.
C: A distinction between economic rights and noneconomic rights.
D: A distinction between political rights, which the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees, and social rights, which the Court treated as unprotected against state-mandated separation so long as facilities were nominally equal.
Answer: D
Authorities: Political-rights/social-rights distinction in state-mandated separation
Explanation: The decision's framing rested on treating political rights as constitutionally guaranteed while placing social arrangements like railway seating outside that guarantee, so long as separate facilities were nominally equal — a distinction later rejected as unworkable. A, B, and C each substitute a different line the reasoning did not actually draw.

Title: Question 5
Prompt: In holding that state-mandated separate schools for different races violated equal protection, the Court relied on more than a simple comparison of physical facilities. Which feature of the reasoning was distinctive?
A: The Court emphasized the psychological and educational harm of state-imposed separation itself, given the importance of public education, rather than resting solely on whether tangible facilities were comparably equipped.
B: The Court held that separation is always constitutional so long as funding per pupil is identical.
C: The Court extended its holding to private, non-state-run schools on the same reasoning.
D: The Court based its holding entirely on a cost-benefit analysis of school district budgets.
Answer: A
Authorities: Education-specific harm reasoning in school desegregation
Explanation: The decision looked beyond tangible equality of facilities to the harm inherent in state-imposed separation itself, particularly given education's importance, and its holding was expressly limited to state action. B misstates the outcome as turning on funding parity, C overstates the reach of a state-action-limited holding, and D substitutes an economic rationale the reasoning did not rely on.

## clii-01-q06
Course: Constitutional Law II
Topic: What equal protection prohibits
Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 3 (Aug. 25, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 6
Prompt: A state statute categorically barred people of a particular race from serving on any jury. The Court struck the statute down. Which set of harms best reflects why the decision treated this kind of categorical exclusion as constitutionally distinct from an ordinary, race-neutral eligibility rule?
A: It only mattered because jury duty is inconvenient for those excluded.
B: The exclusion amounted to legal discrimination, implied the inferiority of the excluded group in civil society, and tended to reinforce that inferiority going forward.
C: It mattered solely because the excluded individuals lost wages from jury pay.
D: The Court treated the exclusion as a purely administrative matter with no bearing on equal protection.
Answer: B
Authorities: Categorical race-based exclusion from civic participation
Explanation: A categorical racial exclusion from a core civic function was treated as more than an inconvenience — it worked legal discrimination, branded the excluded group as inferior in the eyes of the law, and perpetuated that inferiority, which is why it was treated as constitutionally distinct from an ordinary eligibility rule. A and C trivialize the harm into a practical inconvenience, and D denies the exclusion any equal-protection significance at all.

## clii-01-q07
Course: Constitutional Law II
Topic: Scrutiny triggered by facial racial classification
Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 3 (Aug. 25, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 7
Prompt: An executive order authorizes designated officials to exclude persons of a particular ancestry from specified geographic areas, including their own homes, during a declared national emergency. Without resolving whether the order can ultimately survive review, what standard of review does a classification drawn expressly along racial lines like this trigger?
A: Ordinary rational basis review, deferring to the emergency judgment of the officials involved.
B: No judicial review at all, because the order was issued during a declared national emergency.
C: A standard requiring a compelling government interest and narrow tailoring, because racial classifications are immediately treated with suspicion.
D: Review turns entirely on whether the excluded group can show economic harm from the exclusion.
Answer: C
Authorities: Early strict-scrutiny formulation for facial racial classifications
Explanation: A classification drawn expressly along racial lines was treated as immediately suspect, triggering a standard demanding a compelling interest and narrow tailoring rather than ordinary deference — a standard about the level of review required, independent of how the case is ultimately resolved on the merits. A applies far too little scrutiny to a facial racial classification, B assumes emergencies place government action beyond judicial review entirely, and D substitutes an economic-harm showing for the trigger the doctrine actually uses.

## clii-01-q08
Course: Constitutional Law II
Topic: When heightened review applies
Set: CLII-01
Coverage: Session 3 (Aug. 25, 2026)
Theme: Meridian County Records
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 8
Prompt: A city adopts a hiring test that does not mention race on its face, but the test's content was selected knowing it would disqualify a disproportionate share of one racial group compared to others, with no significant connection to job performance. A separate city ordinance requires all municipal contracts to state that at least half must go to businesses owned by a specific racial group. Which framework correctly sorts these two policies for purposes of deciding whether heightened review applies?
A: Neither policy can trigger heightened review unless it uses an explicit racial label somewhere in its text.
B: Both automatically escape heightened review, because neither policy criminalizes anything.
C: Only the facially race-neutral hiring test can ever trigger heightened review; a policy that expressly advantages a racial group is categorically exempt from it.
D: A facially neutral classification adopted with the purpose of disadvantaging a racial group can trigger heightened review despite its neutral text, and a classification that expressly advantages a particular racial group is also treated as triggering heightened review, not exempted from it.
Answer: D
Authorities: Race-specific, race-neutral-with-disparate-purpose, and race-advantaging classifications
Explanation: Heightened review is not limited to policies that name a race on their face — a facially neutral rule adopted to disadvantage a racial group can still trigger it, and a rule that expressly advantages a particular racial group is treated as triggering the same heightened review rather than escaping it. A and C both wrongly assume that only an express, disadvantaging racial label can trigger heightened scrutiny, and B assumes a criminal-law limitation the framework does not contain.
