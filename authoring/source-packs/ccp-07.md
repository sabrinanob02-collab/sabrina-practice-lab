## ccp-07-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Slipping a Bluetooth tracker into a bag (trespass test)
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 1
Prompt: On a commuter train, an undercover Wexford Point detective slips a coin-sized Bluetooth tracker into an open outer pocket of Nadia Kerr's backpack while she naps. For the next two days, detectives follow the tracker's pings around town as she makes stops on public streets. Was placing and monitoring the tracker a search?
A: Yes. Physically placing a device into a person's effect in order to obtain information is a search under the trespass test, even if every place she later went was public.
B: No, because she traveled only on public roads, where her movements were exposed to anyone.
C: No, because Bluetooth trackers are consumer products in general public use.
D: It became a search only if and when the tracker entered her home.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Jones; United States v. Knotts; United States v. Karo; Kyllo v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Jones holds that the government's physical intrusion on a constitutionally protected area (persons, houses, papers, effects) to obtain information is a search, whatever the privacy expectation in the information gathered. Knotts and Karo concern monitoring a beeper that had been placed with the consent of the container's owner at the time. Application: A backpack is an effect. Putting a tracker into it without Kerr's consent, to follow her, is the physical intrusion plus information-gathering purpose Jones describes. That it then tracked her only on public streets does not matter under the trespass test. Why not the others: B applies Knotts, which involved no trespass. C applies Kyllo's general-public-use idea to a trespass question, where it has no role. D confuses Karo's in-home monitoring rule (an independent basis for a search) with a requirement; the trespass already made it a search. Policy: The trespass test keeps the Fourth Amendment tied to its property origins, giving a clear rule that officers cannot tamper with your belongings to track you, while Katz covers intrusions without trespass.

## ccp-07-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Tower dumps after the cell-site location case
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 2
Prompt: After three late-night pharmacy robberies, Wexford Point investigators obtain, without a warrant, "tower dumps" from carriers: records of every phone that connected to the towers serving each pharmacy for 30 minutes around each robbery. About 4,000 phones appear. Only one, belonging to Ezra Kinsale, shows up at all three. He moves to suppress. Which statement is most accurate?
A: The government will plainly lose, because the location-records case held that all cell-site data requires a warrant.
B: The government will plainly win, because the dialed-numbers case settles that cell-site data is unprotected third-party information.
C: The location-records case expressly declined to decide tower dumps. A short-window dump reveals far less about any one person than months of tracking, which helps the government. Kinsale's best arguments are that the dump sweeps in thousands of innocent people and reveals location data of the same revealing type.
D: Kinsale lacks standing, because the dump collected data on thousands of other people, not just him.
Answer: C
Authorities: Carpenter v. United States; Smith v. Maryland; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Carpenter held that obtaining seven or more days of historical cell-site location records is a search, but described its decision as narrow and expressly declined to address real-time tracking or tower dumps. Smith governs ordinary third-party records. Standing requires the defendant's own privacy interest (Rakas). Application: A 30-minute window at a single place is far less comprehensive than months of location history, which favors the government. Kinsale's best arguments are the dragnet character of the dump and the revealing nature of location data, though his strongest version needs Carpenter's comprehensiveness reasoning, which is weaker here. Why not the others: A overreads Carpenter. B ignores that Carpenter rejected mechanically applying Smith to location data. D is wrong because Kinsale's own location records were obtained, so his personal interest is at stake whatever happened to others' data. Policy: The government stresses that dumps solve serial crimes and reveal only a moment of each person's location. Critics emphasize that thousands of innocent people are swept in, and courts have looked for particularity and minimization safeguards.

## ccp-07-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Overnight live-in caregiver as a guest
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 3
Prompt: Lenore Vasquez, a nanny, stays overnight every weekend at her employer's house, sleeping in the guest room where she keeps a suitcase of clothes and toiletries. While the family is out on Saturday, officers investigating Lenore for check fraud enter without a warrant, search her suitcase in the guest room, and find forged checks. Can Lenore challenge the search?
A: No, because she is there for a commercial reason, as an employee, so she is like a short-term business visitor.
B: Yes. As a regular overnight guest who sleeps in the home and keeps belongings in the room, she has a legitimate expectation of privacy there, at least in the guest room and her own luggage. Her employment does not make her stay a brief business visit.
C: No, because only the homeowner may challenge a search of a home.
D: Yes, and her standing extends equally to every room of the house, including the employer's locked study.
Answer: B
Authorities: Minnesota v. Olson; Minnesota v. Carter; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: An overnight guest has a legitimate expectation of privacy in the host's home (Olson), reflecting the social expectation that guests sleeping there are protected. Carter denied protection to visitors present for a few hours for a purely commercial transaction. Rights are personal and attach to areas the defendant has a legitimate interest in (Rakas). Application: Lenore sleeps there regularly, keeps personal belongings, and uses a designated room. That looks like Olson, not Carter's brief drug-packaging visit. Her expectation is strongest in the guest room and her suitcase. Why not the others: A stretches Carter, which turned on a short, purely commercial presence. C contradicts Olson. D overextends her interest to areas she has no use of or access to. Policy: Olson protects the ordinary experience of staying in someone else's home, where we are vulnerable while asleep. Tying standing to actual use keeps it realistic, because a guest's privacy does not reach the host's private spaces.

## ccp-07-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Facially deficient warrant: blank items-to-be-seized section
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 4
Prompt: Detective Ruane uses the county's e-warrant system. His affidavit lists the stolen marine radios he wants. Because of an upload glitch, the warrant the magistrate signs has a blank "items to be seized" field and does not incorporate the affidavit. Ruane, who drafted both documents, does not reread the signed warrant. He searches only for radios, finds them, and leaves everything else. Is the evidence admissible?
A: Yes, because Ruane limited the search to exactly what the affidavit described.
B: Yes, because the magistrate signed the warrant, and that signature cures any defect.
C: Yes. The warrant was invalid, but a search that was reasonable in scope makes the defect harmless.
D: Likely not. A warrant that describes nothing to be seized fails particularity on its face, and an affidavit cannot cure it unless it is incorporated and attached. Because the officer who prepared the warrant could have seen the obvious defect, reliance on it is not objectively reasonable.
Answer: D
Authorities: Groh v. Ramirez; United States v. Leon; Massachusetts v. Sheppard
Explanation: Rule: The Fourth Amendment requires that the warrant itself particularly describe the things to be seized. An affidavit can supply particularity only if the warrant incorporates it and it accompanies the warrant (Groh, where the "items" section was effectively blank). Leon's good-faith exception does not apply when a warrant is so facially deficient that officers cannot reasonably presume it valid. Sheppard excused a judge's error when the officer reasonably relied on the judge's assurance that the form would be corrected. Application: The signed warrant described nothing. Ruane prepared it, and a glance would have shown the blank field. Like Groh, that is a facially deficient warrant. The glitch makes his situation more sympathetic, but a reasonable officer checks the document he executes. Why not the others: A rests on the reasoning Groh rejected, that restraint in practice cures a deficient warrant. B overstates the magistrate's role. C ignores that particularity is required in the warrant itself. Policy: Particularity tells the person whose home is searched what the officers may take and constrains the officers. Excusing a blank warrant would hollow out that protection. Sheppard shows that reasonable reliance on a judge's assurances, unlike an officer's own oversight, remains protected.

## ccp-07-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Detaining occupants after it is clear they are not the suspects
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 5
Prompt: At 6 a.m., officers execute a valid warrant for a house where two men in their twenties were believed to run an armed robbery crew. Unknown to them, the house had just been sold. Inside are an elderly couple in their eighties, whom officers handcuff at gunpoint in their nightclothes. Within five minutes officers confirm no one else is in the house and that the couple are the new owners. They keep the couple handcuffed in the living room for 90 minutes while they finish searching. Was the detention reasonable?
A: Yes, because officers may always detain occupants in handcuffs for the entire length of a search.
B: No, because occupants who are not suspects may never be detained during a search.
C: Yes, because the warrant was valid, so everything done while executing it was reasonable.
D: The initial brief detention was reasonable given the danger officers anticipated, but keeping obviously uninvolved, unarmed elderly occupants handcuffed for 90 minutes, after any threat was dispelled, was not.
Answer: D
Authorities: Los Angeles County v. Rettele; Muehler v. Mena; Michigan v. Summers
Explanation: Rule: Officers may detain occupants during a warrant search (Summers) and may use reasonable force, including handcuffs, given safety concerns (Muehler). But detention must be reasonable in its manner and duration. Rettele upheld a brief detention of unexpected occupants because it ended quickly once officers realized their mistake. Application: The first minutes, an armed-robbery warrant executed at dawn with unknown occupants, justify control. Once officers confirmed the house was safe and the couple were the new owners, continuing to handcuff them for 90 minutes served no safety purpose. Why not the others: A ignores that restraint must remain reasonable throughout. B contradicts Summers. C conflates a valid warrant with reasonable execution. Policy: Detention power exists for safety and orderly searching. Limiting it to what those needs require protects innocent people who happen to be present, often in their own homes, from unnecessary humiliation.

## ccp-07-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Informant's screenshot evidence and probable cause
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 6
Prompt: A first-time informant gives Detective Orla Finch screenshots of a text conversation in which a contact named "Q" offers to sell her "two zips" of meth, "pickup at my place, 19 Sorrel Lane, after 8." Finch confirms through carrier records that the number belongs to Quentin Vail, that he lives at 19 Sorrel Lane, and that he has two prior meth-distribution convictions. She gets a warrant for the house. Was there a substantial basis for probable cause?
A: No, because a first-time informant can never supply probable cause.
B: Yes. Under the totality of the circumstances, the screenshots show the basis of the informant's knowledge, and verifying the phone number, the address, and Vail's record corroborates key details. Together they give a fair probability that drugs will be found at the house.
C: No, because the screenshots are hearsay, and hearsay cannot support a warrant.
D: No, because probable cause requires that officers personally see a drug sale before seeking a warrant.
Answer: B
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Spinelli v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Gates asks whether, under the totality of the circumstances, there is a fair probability that contraband will be found. Basis of knowledge and veracity are important factors, and strength in one, or corroboration, can make up for weakness in another. Application: The informant has no track record, but the screenshots document how she knows: the target's own words offering a sale at his home. Corroborating the number, address, and prior record ties the documents to Vail and the house. Why not the others: A is wrong because a first-time informant's information can count when corroborated or well grounded. C is wrong because probable cause may rest on hearsay. D invents a firsthand-observation requirement. Policy: The totality approach lets officers use reliable new forms of evidence, such as digital records, while still requiring something more than a bare accusation. Critics worry that screenshots can be fabricated, which is why corroboration matters.

## ccp-07-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Arrest warrant authority to enter the suspect's own home
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 7
Prompt: Officers hold a valid arrest warrant for Corbin Aldous for felony assault. At 7 a.m., they see his car in the driveway of his own house, the kitchen lights on, and his shadow at the window. They knock and announce, get no response, enter, and arrest him in the hallway. Did they need a search warrant?
A: No. An arrest warrant founded on probable cause carries limited authority to enter the suspect's own dwelling when there is reason to believe he is inside.
B: Yes, because entering a home always requires a search warrant, even with an arrest warrant.
C: Yes, unless there were exigent circumstances, because an arrest warrant alone never permits entry into a home.
D: No, and an arrest warrant would also have let them enter any home where they thought he might be staying.
Answer: A
Authorities: Payton v. New York; United States v. Watson
Explanation: Rule: Payton forbids warrantless, nonconsensual home entry to make a routine felony arrest, but holds that an arrest warrant founded on probable cause implicitly carries limited authority to enter the suspect's own dwelling when there is reason to believe the suspect is within. Watson allows warrantless arrests in public on probable cause. Application: Officers had an arrest warrant, and the car, lights, and shadow gave reason to believe Aldous was home. No separate search warrant was needed to enter his own house to arrest him. Why not the others: B and C contradict Payton's holding about arrest warrants. D overextends Payton: entering a third party's home to look for a suspect implicates the third party's own rights and requires a search warrant for that home. Policy: Payton puts a neutral magistrate between the government and the home before police enter to arrest. Once a judge has found probable cause to arrest, letting officers enter the suspect's own home to execute it balances his privacy against effective law enforcement.

## ccp-07-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Full search of the person incident to a custodial arrest
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 8
Prompt: Riordan Hale is lawfully taken into custody for driving with a revoked license. Searching his person, the officer finds a crumpled cigarette pack in his jacket, opens it, and finds tiny baggies of heroin. Hale argues that a revoked-license arrest leaves no evidence to find and that the officer had no reason to think he was armed. Is the heroin admissible?
A: No, because a search incident to arrest requires a case-specific reason to believe the arrestee has weapons or evidence.
B: No, because for traffic arrests officers may only pat down for weapons.
C: Yes. A lawful custodial arrest justifies a full search of the arrestee's person, including physical containers found on the person, without case-by-case proof of weapons or evidence.
D: No, because there is no evidence of the crime of arrest to find, so no search incident to arrest is allowed.
Answer: C
Authorities: United States v. Robinson; Chimel v. California; Riley v. California; Arizona v. Gant
Explanation: Rule: Robinson held that a custodial arrest based on probable cause is reasonable, and a full search of the person incident to it requires no further justification. It upheld opening a crumpled cigarette package found on the arrestee. Riley carved out digital data, and Gant's "evidence of the crime of arrest" limit applies to vehicles, not to searches of the person. Application: Hale was in custody, and the pack was a physical container on his person. Robinson's categorical rule applies, regardless of the offense. Why not the others: A demands the case-by-case showing Robinson rejected. B misstates the law for custodial traffic arrests. D imports Gant's vehicle rule into a search of the person. Policy: Robinson's categorical rule is easy for officers to apply quickly and reflects the danger and evidence-loss risks of every custodial arrest. Critics argue it lets minor arrests become a pretext for full searches.

## ccp-07-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Automobile exception without actual exigency
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 9
Prompt: Officers develop probable cause that stolen laptops are in Mireya Holt's sedan, legally parked on a public street outside her apartment. Holt is upstairs asleep, the keys are with her, and two officers could easily have guarded the car while a third sought a warrant. Instead they search it on the spot and find the laptops. Is the search valid?
A: No, because the car could not be driven away at that moment, so there was no exigency.
B: Yes. The automobile exception requires only probable cause and a readily mobile vehicle. It rests on the inherent mobility of cars and their reduced expectation of privacy, not on case-specific proof that the vehicle was about to leave.
C: Yes, but only because Holt was not physically present to object.
D: No, because officers had time to get a warrant, and the exception applies only when they do not.
Answer: B
Authorities: Carroll v. United States; California v. Acevedo; California v. Carney
Explanation: Rule: Under the automobile exception, officers with probable cause may search a readily mobile vehicle without a warrant. The justification is the inherent mobility of vehicles and the reduced privacy expectation that follows from their pervasive regulation. No separate, case-specific exigency is required (Carroll; Carney; Acevedo). Application: A sedan on a public street is readily mobile even if the owner is asleep with the keys. Probable cause existed, so the warrantless search was valid. Why not the others: A and D import a case-by-case exigency requirement the exception does not have. C invents a presence requirement; Randolph-type objections concern consent, not probable cause. Policy: A categorical rule gives officers clear guidance and avoids litigating whether a particular car could have moved. Critics argue that modern vehicles hold extensive private information and that the reduced-privacy rationale is outdated, especially when warrants can be obtained quickly.

## ccp-07-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: School search of a student's phone
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 10
Prompt: Two students tell Wexford Point High's assistant principal that classmate Sorrel Imrie sent a group message last night saying, "Bringing my dad's gun tomorrow for the people who laughed." The administrator takes Sorrel's phone, opens the messaging app, and finds the message. She then scrolls through Sorrel's photo gallery and finds pictures of marijuana, which she reports to police. Which analysis is correct?
A: The entire search was unlawful, because school officials need probable cause and a warrant to search a phone.
B: The entire search was lawful, because school officials may search students' property without any suspicion.
C: The school-search standard does not apply to phones because they are not physical containers.
D: Under the reasonable-suspicion standard for schools, opening the messaging app to confirm a reported threat was justified at its inception and reasonably related in scope. Scrolling through unrelated photos was not related to the threat, especially given how much private information phones hold, so that part likely exceeded the permissible scope.
Answer: D
Authorities: New Jersey v. T.L.O.; Safford Unified School District v. Redding; Riley v. California
Explanation: Rule: Public school officials may search students on reasonable suspicion, without a warrant. The search must be justified at its inception and reasonable in scope, meaning reasonably related to the objectives of the search and not excessively intrusive given the student's age and the nature of the suspected infraction (T.L.O.). Safford held that the scope must match the suspicion. Riley recognized that phones contain uniquely private information. Application: Two firsthand reports of a gun threat justify looking at the specific message. The photo gallery has no logical connection to verifying a threat in a message, and Riley's privacy concerns heighten the scope inquiry. Courts have not settled school phone searches, but this analysis follows T.L.O. and Safford. Why not the others: A imposes the wrong standard for schools. B ignores T.L.O.'s suspicion requirement. C is wrong because T.L.O. governs searches of students' effects generally, and phones are effects. Policy: Schools need flexibility to respond quickly to threats. Scope limits keep a safety concern from becoming a pretext for searching a student's private life, which matters given how much students' lives are stored on their phones.

## ccp-07-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consent by a teenager to search the family home
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 11
Prompt: While his parents are at work, 16-year-old Jasper Lindgren answers the door. Officers ask whether they can look around for stolen bikes connected to his father. Jasper says, "Sure," and leads them through the family room and kitchen, then opens his parents' locked bedroom with a key from the kitchen drawer. Officers find stolen bikes in the family room and a ledger in the parents' bedroom. Which statement is most accurate?
A: Courts have not settled when a minor can consent. A teenager who lives in the home and routinely uses the family room may have common authority over that shared space, but his authority is unlikely to extend to his parents' locked bedroom. The ledger is vulnerable to suppression even if the bikes are not.
B: All evidence must be suppressed, because minors can never consent to a search.
C: All evidence is admissible, because a resident child has authority over every part of the family home.
D: All evidence must be suppressed, because officers did not tell Jasper he could refuse.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Matlock; Illinois v. Rodriguez; Schneckloth v. Bustamonte
Explanation: Rule: Third-party consent rests on common authority, meaning mutual use and joint access or control (Matlock), or on objectively reasonable apparent authority (Rodriguez). Consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances, and knowledge of the right to refuse is not required (Schneckloth). Application: Courts consider age, maturity, and the space involved when a minor consents. A 16-year-old who uses the family room likely has common authority there. A locked parental bedroom shows the parents' exclusive control, and officers would have reason to doubt the teen's authority over it. Why not the others: B states a categorical rule no case adopts. C ignores how authority depends on the space and on the locked door. D misstates Schneckloth. Policy: Shared living arrangements mean that co-residents assume some risk that others will let police in. Limiting that risk to the spaces each person actually controls protects private areas within the home and avoids exploiting the deference minors often show to authority.

## ccp-07-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Emergency aid after a domestic-violence hang-up call
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 12
Prompt: Dispatch reports a 911 call from a Wexford Point duplex in which a woman screamed, "Stop, you're hurting him!" before the line went dead. Officers arrive within minutes. Daria Keene opens the door with a fresh cut on her cheek and says, "Everything's fine, you can go." Behind her they hear a child crying and a man shouting. She refuses entry. Officers push past her and check the rooms for anyone injured, finding an injured child and, on a shelf in plain sight, a stolen handgun. Is the entry lawful?
A: No, because Keene, the resident present at the door, expressly refused entry.
B: No, because emergency aid applies only when officers have probable cause that a crime is in progress.
C: Likely yes. The cut-off call, the visible injury, the crying child, and the shouting give an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside was seriously injured or in imminent danger. A sweep for victims, limited to finding people, is permissible, and the gun in plain sight may be seized.
D: Yes, and once inside the officers could search drawers and closets for any weapons in the home.
Answer: C
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Horton v. California; Georgia v. Randolph
Explanation: Rule: Officers may enter without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe an occupant is seriously injured or imminently threatened. The search must be limited to the emergency (Fisher). Items in plain view during a lawful entry may be seized (Horton). Randolph noted that its consent rule does not override police authority to enter to protect a resident from domestic violence. Application: A 911 call about someone being hurt, a visibly injured resident trying to send police away, a crying child, and shouting supply the objective basis. The sweep sought people, and the gun was in plain sight. Why not the others: A treats refused consent as dispositive, but the entry rests on exigency, not consent. B misstates the standard; emergency aid asks about an objectively reasonable belief in a need for aid. D exceeds the emergency's scope. Policy: Domestic violence victims are often pressured to turn police away. Emergency aid lets officers protect vulnerable people, especially children, while scope limits prevent abuse.

## ccp-07-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Identified 911 caller vs. anonymous tip
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 13
Prompt: Caller Hester Doyle gives her name and callback number to 911 and says that, right now, a man in a red rain jacket is waving a knife at people at the Harbor Street bus shelter. Ninety seconds later an officer arrives and sees Brannock Teal, the only person in a red rain jacket, standing at the shelter with his hands in his pockets. No knife is visible. The officer stops and frisks him and finds a knife. Was the stop and frisk lawful?
A: Yes. A named caller who reports a contemporaneous, firsthand observation of an ongoing danger can be held accountable for a false report and gives far more reliable information than a bare anonymous tip. With the matching description and timing, the officer had reasonable suspicion to stop Teal and reason to believe he was armed.
B: No, because the officer did not personally see a knife, so the tip could not be relied on.
C: No, because a person suspected of having a weapon can be detained only on probable cause.
D: The frisk was lawful, but the stop was not, because a tip can justify a frisk but not a stop.
Answer: A
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Florida v. J.L.; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: Reasonable suspicion is judged on the totality of the circumstances, including the reliability of the information. J.L. held that a bare anonymous tip of a concealed gun, with no indication of how the tipster knew or any means of testing credibility, does not justify a stop. Terry permits a frisk on reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous. Application: Doyle identified herself, so she could be found and held responsible. She reported what she was seeing in real time, which shows her basis of knowledge, and she described an ongoing threat rather than mere possession. The description and timing matched. The report of a brandished knife supports both the stop and the frisk. Why not the others: B misreads J.L., which did not require officer observation of a weapon. C imposes the wrong standard. D gets the order backwards; a frisk depends on a lawful stop. Policy: Requiring indicators of reliability protects people from stops based on unverifiable accusations, while allowing police to act quickly on credible reports of ongoing violence.

## ccp-07-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: When a consensual encounter becomes a seizure
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 14
Prompt: On a train platform, two plainclothes officers approach Ottilie Marsh, identify themselves, and ask to see her ticket and ID. She hands them over. Without returning them, they ask whether she is carrying drugs and say they would like her to come to a small office "to talk." She goes with them, still without her ticket and ID. Has she been seized?
A: No, because officers never touched her or told her she was under arrest.
B: Yes, by the time officers kept her ticket and ID and moved her to the office. A reasonable person whose travel documents are held while officers question her about drugs would not feel free to leave, so the encounter needed reasonable suspicion.
C: No. The encounter stayed consensual throughout, because she voluntarily handed over her documents.
D: Yes, from the instant the officers identified themselves as police.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Mendenhall; Florida v. Royer
Explanation: Rule: A person is seized when, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would believe she was not free to leave (Mendenhall). Factors include the threatening presence of several officers, display of weapons, physical touching, and language suggesting compliance is required. Approaching someone and asking questions is not a seizure. In Royer, officers who kept a traveler's ticket and ID and moved him to a small room had seized him. Application: The initial approach and request were consensual. Keeping her ticket and ID while asking about drugs, and moving her to an office, means a reasonable traveler could not leave without abandoning her documents and her trip. Why not the others: A treats touching or a formal arrest as required. C ignores how the encounter changed. D is wrong because simply identifying oneself as police is not a seizure. Policy: The free-to-leave test allows police to make ordinary voluntary contact while preventing officers from using control over someone's belongings or movement to create a detention without suspicion.

## ccp-07-q15

Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point

Title: Exam 4, Question 15
Prompt: After Damaris Quell is arraigned for embezzlement, police place an informant in her cell with instructions only to "keep your ears open and don't ask her anything." The informant follows instructions and never brings up the case. Over several days, Quell spontaneously describes how she moved the money, and the informant reports it. Are the statements admissible?
A: No, because the informant was a government agent and the right to counsel had attached.

Authorities: Kuhlmann v. Wilson; Massiah v. United States; Illinois v. Perkins
Explanation: Rule: After formal charges, the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the accused without counsel (Massiah). Quell spoke on her own. That is a passive listening post. Why not the others: A ignores that the Sixth Amendment test is deliberate elicitation, not mere agency. B is answered by Perkins. Topic: Custody and a juvenile's age
Set: CCP-07

Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 16
Prompt: A uniformed school resource officer pulls 13-year-old Tobin Crale out of his math class and questions him for 30 minutes in a closed conference room with the assistant principal present about a string of laptop thefts. No one tells Tobin he can leave or call his parents. Tobin confesses. The officer knew Tobin's age. Was Tobin in custody?
A: No, because the custody test is purely objective, so a suspect's age is irrelevant.
B: No, because questioning at school is never custodial.
C: Likely yes. When a child's age is known to the officer or objectively apparent, it informs how a reasonable person in the suspect's position would perceive the situation. A reasonable 13-year-old pulled from class by a uniformed officer into a closed room would likely not feel free to leave.

Answer: C
Authorities: J.D.B. v. North Carolina; Stansbury v. California; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Custody is objective (Stansbury). Policy: Considering age keeps the custody test accurate for the people actually questioned. Juveniles are especially susceptible to coercion and false confessions, and they often believe adults in authority must be obeyed.

## ccp-07-q17

Topic: Booking questions that elicit incriminating facts
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 17
Prompt: Lorcan Dree is arrested in a drug-trafficking case, and the officers know the key evidence is a series of sale arrangements texted from one particular phone number. At booking, without warnings, the booking officer, who is also the case detective, asks Dree for his name and date of birth and then asks, "What's the cell number you use?" Dree gives the number that matches the drug texts. Is that answer admissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief?
A: Yes, because all questions asked during booking fall within the booking exception.
B: No, because every booking question requires warnings first.
C: Probably not. The booking exception covers routine biographical questions asked for administrative purposes. A question officers should know is reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response, given the facts of the case, falls outside it, especially when asked by the case detective.
D: Yes, as long as Dree's answers were written on a booking form rather than spoken.
Answer: C
Authorities: Pennsylvania v. Muniz; Rhode Island v. Innis; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Muniz recognized a routine booking question exception: questions seeking biographical data needed to complete booking or pretrial services, asked for record-keeping purposes, are not interrogation. The exception does not cover questions designed to elicit incriminating admissions. Innis defines interrogation by whether police should know the words are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Application: A phone number can be routine, but here the case detective knew the number was central evidence of the crime. Asking it gets incriminating information under the cover of booking. Courts split on such questions, and the incriminating link favors the defense. Why not the others: A ignores the limits of the exception. B ignores Muniz. D relies on a meaningless distinction; the issue is the question, not the medium. Policy: The booking exception exists so jails can process people without turning paperwork into interrogation. Limiting it to administrative purposes keeps it from becoming a loophole for questioning about the crime.

## ccp-07-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Waiver when police keep a retained lawyer away
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 18
Prompt: Philippa Strand is arrested for a fatal stabbing but not yet charged. Her brother hires a lawyer, who calls the station and says she will be there in 20 minutes and that Strand should not be questioned. Officers tell the lawyer no questioning is planned. They then warn Strand, who does not know a lawyer has been hired or that one called, and she waives and confesses before the lawyer arrives. Is the confession admissible under Miranda?
A: Yes. A valid waiver depends on the suspect's own knowledge and choices. Events she did not know about, including police deception of a lawyer she did not know she had, do not affect whether her waiver was knowing and voluntary.
B: No, because once a lawyer is on the way, any waiver is invalid.
C: No, because police must tell a suspect when a lawyer is trying to reach her.
D: No, because hiring a lawyer for her caused the Sixth Amendment right to attach.
Answer: A
Authorities: Moran v. Burbine; Kirby v. Illinois; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: A Miranda waiver is valid if voluntary, made with full awareness of the rights and the consequences of abandoning them. Burbine held that police failure to tell a suspect about an attorney's efforts to reach him, and even deception of the attorney, does not invalidate an otherwise valid waiver, because events unknown to the suspect cannot affect his capacity to understand and waive his rights. The Sixth Amendment attaches only at formal charging (Kirby). Application: Strand received adequate warnings and waived. She did not know about the lawyer. Under Burbine, the waiver stands, though the Court suggested that sufficiently egregious police deception could raise a due process issue. Why not the others: B and C state rules Burbine rejected. D misplaces attachment, which turns on formal proceedings, not on retaining counsel. Policy: Burbine treats Miranda as protecting the suspect's own informed choice, not as a broader guarantee of access to counsel. The dissent and many state courts argue that police deception of a lawyer undermines the adversary system and the fairness of the process.

Title: Exam 4, Question 19

## ccp-07-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Standing: owning the item vs. privacy in the place searched
Set: CCP-07
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Wexford Point
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 4, Question 20
Prompt: Moments before officers arrive at a bus station, Caius Prent drops his stolen credit cards into his friend Adela's handbag. Adela had known him for two days and had never let him use the bag before. Officers search the handbag without a lawful basis and find the cards. Prent admits they are his and moves to suppress. Can he challenge the search of the handbag?
A: Yes, because a person always has standing to challenge the seizure of property he owns.
B: Yes, because the search was aimed at him, which gives him standing.
C: Yes, because anyone charged with possessing an item automatically has standing to challenge the search that found it.
D: No. Owning the seized cards does not give Prent a legitimate expectation of privacy in Adela's handbag. Given his short acquaintance, his lack of any right to exclude others from the bag, and his sudden use of it, he cannot challenge its search.
Answer: D
Authorities: Rawlings v. Kentucky; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Fourth Amendment rights are personal. A defendant must show a legitimate expectation of privacy in the place searched. Rakas rejected "target" standing, and Rawlings held that a defendant who put drugs in an acquaintance's purse had no legitimate expectation of privacy in the purse, even though he owned the drugs. Application: Prent's ownership of the cards does not give him privacy in Adela's bag, which he had no right to use or control. Why not the others: A confuses ownership of the item with privacy in the place. B states the target theory Rakas rejected. C states the automatic-standing rule that later cases abandoned in favor of the personal-expectation inquiry. Policy: Standing limits keep exclusion focused on the person whose privacy was invaded and reduce the costs of suppressing reliable evidence. Critics argue this lets police deliberately violate third parties' rights to obtain evidence against a suspect.
