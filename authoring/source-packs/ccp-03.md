## ccp-03-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: The two distinct probable-cause showings (search vs. arrest)
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: Detectives believe an informant's information supports arresting a person for running an illegal betting operation, and separately want to search that person's garage for the ledger books used to track wagers. Which pair of showings must probable cause separately establish for each action?
A: A single, unified showing that a crime occurred is sufficient for both the arrest and the search, since both arise from the same underlying investigation.
B: For the arrest, that a crime was committed and that this person committed it; for the search, that evidence of the crime exists and can be found at the garage.
C: For the arrest, that evidence exists somewhere; for the search, that a crime was committed and that this person committed it.
D: Probable cause for the arrest requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt, while probable cause for the search requires only a generalized reasonable suspicion.
Answer: B
Authorities: Two distinct probable-cause showings (to search vs. to arrest)
Explanation: Probable cause to arrest requires proof that a crime was committed and that the person to be arrested committed it, while probable cause to search requires proof that evidence, fruits, or instrumentalities of a crime exist and can be found at the place to be searched — two related but independent showings, each keyed to its own action. A wrongly collapses these into one undifferentiated showing, C swaps which showing belongs to which action, and D substitutes the wrong standards entirely (probable cause is neither proof beyond a reasonable doubt nor mere generalized reasonable suspicion).

## ccp-03-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Probable cause as a practical, non-technical standard
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 2
Prompt: A magistrate reviews a warrant application whose facts would not persuade a jury beyond a reasonable doubt that a crime occurred, and might not clearly satisfy a more-likely-than-not showing on every element, but which, taken together, would lead an ordinarily careful and sensible person to believe an offense had probably been committed. How should the magistrate treat this application?
A: Deny it, because probable cause requires a showing that would satisfy a jury applying the criminal trial's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard.
B: Deny it, because without a preponderance-of-the-evidence showing on every element, probable cause cannot exist as a matter of law.
C: Grant it only if the affiant is a sworn law-enforcement officer with specialized training, since a magistrate's own lay judgment cannot establish probable cause.
D: Grant it, because probable cause is judged by the practical, everyday judgment of a reasonable and prudent person weighing the totality of the facts, not by rigorous or technical evidentiary standards.
Answer: D
Authorities: Probabilistic, practical-considerations standard for probable cause
Explanation: Probable cause turns on probabilities assessed through the factual and practical considerations of everyday life on which reasonable and prudent people act, not legal technicians applying rigorous evidentiary tests — it is deliberately less demanding than "probable" sounds in ordinary usage. A imports the criminal trial's standard, B imposes a preponderance requirement the doctrine does not demand, and C wrongly limits the judgment to trained officers rather than the reasonable-person standard the doctrine actually uses.

## ccp-03-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Mere presence in a high-activity area
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 3
Prompt: Police know a particular block has an unusually high rate of hand-to-hand drug transactions. An officer patrolling after dark wants to justify probable cause to arrest anyone found standing there, relying solely on the block's reputation and each person's presence on it. Which statement is most accurate?
A: A location's reputation for criminal activity can be one relevant circumstance, but presence alone, without more individualized facts connecting that particular person to criminal conduct, does not establish probable cause as to that person.
B: Presence alone in a high-crime area is sufficient to establish probable cause as to every person found there, since the area's reputation supplies the necessary suspicion collectively.
C: A location's reputation for criminal activity is entirely irrelevant to the probable-cause analysis and may never be considered.
D: Presence in a high-crime area shifts the burden to the individual to disprove suspicion before probable cause can be defeated.
Answer: A
Authorities: High-crime-area presence and individualized suspicion
Explanation: A location's reputation can factor into the totality of circumstances, but it does not by itself supply probable cause as to every individual found there absent some individualized connection to criminal conduct. B treats an area-wide reputation as automatically transferable to any individual present, C wrongly excludes a genuinely relevant circumstance from consideration altogether, and D inverts the burden the doctrine actually places on the government.

## ccp-03-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Refusal of consent after a lawful stop
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 4
Prompt: After a lawful traffic stop, an officer asks the driver for consent to search the trunk. The driver calmly and politely declines. The officer wants to rely on that refusal itself, combined with nothing else beyond the initial valid basis for the stop, as adding to probable cause to search the trunk anyway. Which statement is correct?
A: A driver's refusal to consent to a search, standing alone, adds meaningfully to probable cause, since an innocent driver would have nothing to hide.
B: Refusal converts an otherwise lawful stop into an unlawful seizure, requiring the officer to immediately release the driver.
C: A driver's refusal to consent to a search is the exercise of a constitutional right and does not itself supply or add to probable cause or reasonable suspicion.
D: Refusal is legally irrelevant only when the driver is represented by counsel at the time of the stop.
Answer: C
Authorities: Refusal of consent does not itself supply suspicion
Explanation: Declining a request to search is the exercise of a right, and adding weight to a refusal would effectively penalize the exercise of that right; refusal alone does not supply or bolster probable cause or reasonable suspicion. A treats the refusal as inherently suspicious in a way the doctrine rejects, B invents a consequence (mandatory release) unrelated to the refusal's actual legal effect, and D conditions an outcome on representation by counsel, a fact irrelevant to whether refusal itself carries evidentiary weight.

## ccp-03-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Crediting a claimed invitation against an inviter's own admission
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 5
Prompt: Officers respond to a noise complaint at a house and find a late-night gathering underway. The homeowner, contacted separately, states she never gave anyone permission to be there. Several guests tell officers a named individual invited them, but when officers locate that individual, he admits he had no authority from the homeowner to invite anyone either. Officers arrest the guests for trespassing. Which statement about probable cause is most accurate?
A: Officers may credit the guests' claimed invitation and still have probable cause to arrest for trespass, because the purported inviter's own admission that he lacked authority to invite anyone undermines the very permission the guests relied on.
B: Probable cause is defeated automatically whenever an arrestee offers any innocent explanation for their presence, regardless of whether that explanation is corroborated.
C: Because the guests reasonably believed the invitation was valid, no probable cause can exist no matter what the purported host later admits.
D: Probable cause requires officers to independently verify with the homeowner, before any arrest, that no permission was given to any individual guest by name.
Answer: A
Authorities: Crediting a claimed invitation against the inviter's own lack of authority
Explanation: Even taking the guests' claimed invitation at face value, probable cause for trespass can still exist once the purported inviter himself admits he had no authority to extend the invitation, because that admission undercuts the only basis for believing the guests' presence was authorized. B treats any innocent explanation as automatically dispositive, C ignores that reasonable belief in an invitation does not survive the inviter's own admission that the invitation was baseless, and D imposes a name-by-name verification requirement the standard does not demand.

## ccp-03-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: The superseded two-pronged informant test
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 6
Prompt: Under the framework that governed informant tips before it was replaced, a reviewing court was required to separately assess two showings, both of which had to be independently satisfied before a tip could support probable cause. Which pair correctly identifies them?
A: Whether the tip named a specific location, and whether the tip specified a specific date.
B: Whether the informant was a paid source, and whether the informant had previously testified in court.
C: Whether the crime described was a felony, and whether the tip was corroborated by a second, independent informant.
D: How the informant came to know the information (basis of knowledge), and separately, whether there was reason to credit the informant as honest or the information as reliable (veracity).
Answer: D
Authorities: Superseded two-pronged informant test (basis of knowledge; veracity/reliability)
Explanation: The pre-existing framework required courts to test an informant's tip against two independent prongs: basis of knowledge (did the tip reveal how the informant came by the information) and veracity or reliability (separate reason to credit the informant's honesty or the information's reliability), both of which had to be satisfied. A, B, and C each substitute considerations (naming a location or date, payment or courtroom history, felony classification, or a second informant) that were not the two required prongs under that framework.

## ccp-03-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Corroboration of innocent details under the rigid two-pronged test
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 7
Prompt: An anonymous letter to police predicts that a couple will travel out of state to obtain drugs and return with the drugs hidden in their car, and specifies their approximate travel pattern. Officers corroborate the couple's flight, hotel stay, and drive back along the predicted route, but have no independent information establishing how the letter-writer learned of any drug activity, or any reason to trust the writer's honesty. Applying the superseded two-pronged test in a strictly rigid fashion, how would a court most likely treat this corroboration?
A: Corroborating any detail in an anonymous tip automatically satisfies both required showings, regardless of whether the corroborated detail relates to criminal activity.
B: The corroboration of the couple's ordinary travel details, without any independent showing of how the writer knew of criminal activity or reason to credit the writer's honesty, would not satisfy both required showings, even though the travel predictions came true.
C: The two-pronged framework does not apply to anonymous tips at all, only to tips from known, named informants.
D: Because the travel predictions came true, honesty is automatically established, making any showing about how the writer knew of the activity unnecessary.
Answer: B
Authorities: Corroboration of innocent details insufficient under a rigid two-pronged reading
Explanation: Under a rigid application of the two-pronged test, corroborating innocent, unremarkable details like a flight and a hotel stay does not by itself establish either the tipster's veracity or basis of knowledge as to the criminal activity itself, so both prongs remain unsatisfied even when the predicted innocent details prove accurate. A and D treat corroboration of any detail as automatically sufficient, and C wrongly exempts anonymous tips from the framework rather than recognizing they are simply harder to satisfy under it.

## ccp-03-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Totality-of-the-circumstances replacing the rigid two-pronged test
Set: CCP-03
Coverage: Class 6 (Sept. 2, 2026)
Theme: Norwood Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 8
Prompt: Reconsidering the same facts as the prior question under the totality-of-the-circumstances approach that replaced the rigid two-pronged test, which analysis is correct?
A: The outcome is identical under either approach, since weighing the totality of the circumstances still requires each showing to be independently satisfied on its own.
B: Under this approach, corroboration of any detail at all, criminal or innocent, is legally irrelevant to the probable-cause determination.
C: A deficiency in one factor, such as an unknown basis of knowledge, can be offset by strength in another, such as substantial independent police corroboration of specific, predicted future conduct that an innocent person could not likely have guessed — the magistrate weighs the whole picture rather than checking separate boxes.
D: This approach eliminates any need for police corroboration whatsoever, so long as the tip itself sounds plausible on its face.
Answer: C
Authorities: Totality-of-the-circumstances approach to probable cause
Explanation: The totality-of-the-circumstances approach treats basis of knowledge and veracity as closely intertwined factors that inform one commonsense, practical judgment rather than separate, independently mandatory boxes, so substantial corroboration of specific predicted future conduct an innocent bystander could not likely have guessed can compensate for a weak or unknown basis of knowledge. A wrongly claims the two approaches reach the same result by the same method, B and D each discard corroboration's relevance in opposite, equally incorrect ways.
