Difficulty: medium

Title: Question 1
Prompt: Shoppers on a crowded online marketplace repeatedly buy replacement filters bearing a familiar symbol without reopening every seller's technical specifications. Which rationale most directly supports protecting the symbol when it identifies source?
A: Protection gives the seller perpetual control over the filter's useful dimensions.
B: Protection prevents competitors from truthfully naming the product category.
C: Protection can reduce consumer search costs and deception while preserving incentives to maintain consistent goodwill.
D: Protection rewards originality in the same way as copyright law.
Answer: C
Authorities: Trademark functions and search-cost rationale
Explanation: Trademark protection helps consumers identify source, reduces search costs and deception, and supports investment in consistent goodwill. It does not create perpetual control over functional features, suppress truthful category names, or duplicate copyright's originality rationale. Thus A, B, and D describe objectives trademark law does not ordinarily pursue.

Title: Question 2
Prompt: A cycling-equipment company coins VELQUOR for protective helmets. The record shows no prior meaning or recognized connection between the word and helmets. The designation is best classified as:
A: Generic.
B: Descriptive.
C: Suggestive.
D: Fanciful.
Answer: D
Authorities: Abercrombie distinctiveness spectrum
Explanation: A coined term with no prior meaning is fanciful and inherently distinctive when used as a mark. VELQUOR does not name the product, describe an attribute, or evoke one through an existing meaning. A, B, and C therefore do not fit the facts.

Title: Question 3
Prompt: A coffee producer uses TWELVE-HOUR STEEP for concentrate made by steeping grounds for twelve hours. Buyers immediately understand the phrase as conveying the production method, and competitors reasonably need similar language to describe their concentrates. The designation is most likely:
A: Descriptive.
B: Suggestive.
C: Arbitrary.
D: Fanciful.
Answer: A
Authorities: Abercrombie; Zatarains descriptive analysis
Explanation: The phrase immediately conveys a significant characteristic and method of production, and competitor need points in the same direction. That makes it descriptive rather than suggestive. It is neither arbitrary nor coined, so C and D also fail.

Title: Question 4
Prompt: A sleep-audio service uses NIGHTBRIDGE. The phrase does not directly state any feature of recorded stories, but consumers may infer that the service helps carry them from wakefulness to sleep. Competitors have ample ordinary language to describe sleep recordings. Which fact most strongly supports suggestiveness?
A: Competitors cannot describe their services without using NIGHTBRIDGE.
B: Buyers immediately receive a literal description of the recordings' content.
C: Connecting the metaphor to the service requires imagination, and alternative descriptive wording remains available.
D: The service has used the phrase continuously for five years.
Answer: C
Authorities: Abercrombie; Zatarains imagination and competitor-need tests
Explanation: Suggestive matter requires imagination or perception to connect the term to the goods or services, and low competitor need supports that classification. A and B instead describe evidence of descriptiveness. D may bear on acquired distinctiveness but does not determine the term's conceptual category.

Title: Question 5
Prompt: A Nevada producer sells olive oil under BLUE RIDGE GROVE. Blue Ridge is a recognized agricultural region, relevant buyers are likely to believe the oil comes from there, and that belief materially affects purchasing decisions. The olives and oil originate entirely in Nevada. The strongest initial concern is that the designation is:
A: Primarily geographically deceptively misdescriptive.
B: Fanciful because geographic wording has no ordinary meaning for food.
C: Automatically generic for every type of olive oil.
D: Inherently distinctive merely because this producer used it first.
Answer: A
Authorities: Geographic marks and Section 2(e)(3) analysis
Explanation: A known place, a likely but false goods-place association, and materiality support primarily geographically deceptively misdescriptive treatment. Geographic wording is not automatically fanciful or generic, and first use does not erase geographic significance. B, C, and D are therefore categorical errors.

Title: Question 6
Prompt: A travel-trunk company applies to register BRIN in ordinary lettering. Directory evidence shows BRIN is an uncommon surname, the founder is Elena Brin, and the record identifies no ordinary non-surname meaning. Which assessment is strongest?
A: The term cannot be perceived as a surname because directory listings are never relevant.
B: The founder connection, directory evidence, lack of another meaning, and ordinary presentation support primarily-surname perception even though the name is uncommon.
C: The term is automatically fanciful because it has only four letters.
D: Consumer enthusiasm for the trunks conclusively resolves the inquiry.
Answer: B
Authorities: Primarily merely a surname factors
Explanation: Surname analysis considers frequency, applicant connections, other meanings, and structure or presentation. Rarity matters but is not conclusive; the founder connection, surname evidence, ordinary lettering, and absence of another meaning point toward surname perception. A discards relevant evidence, C makes word length dispositive, and D substitutes product popularity for perception of the term.

Title: Question 7
Prompt: A mortgage platform uses QMS, and relevant borrowers commonly understand those letters to mean QUICK MORTGAGE SERVICE. If the full phrase immediately describes the service, the initials are most likely:
A: Automatically fanciful because three letters form a new visual unit.
B: Descriptive as well, absent acquired distinctiveness, because consumers understand them as the descriptive phrase.
C: Evaluated without regard to what the public understands them to abbreviate.
D: Generic in every financial market regardless of consumer perception.
Answer: B
Authorities: Descriptive abbreviations and initialisms
Explanation: An abbreviation generally shares the descriptive significance of its understood underlying phrase. Letter form alone does not make it fanciful, consumer understanding cannot be ignored, and genericness requires a separate perception inquiry. A, C, and D therefore overstate.

Title: Question 8
Prompt: An office-furniture seller claims acquired distinctiveness in a chair's crescent-shaped back, paired side openings, and three-button seam. Its survey shows the complete showroom display, including a large registered house mark, and asks who made the chair. Most respondents name the seller. What is the principal weakness?
A: Consumer surveys can never support acquired distinctiveness.
B: Respondents must identify the individual factory that assembled each chair.
C: The responses may reflect the house mark or overall display rather than source association with the precise feature combination claimed.
D: Product design is automatically inherently distinctive.
Answer: C
Authorities: TBL Licensing claim-specific acquired-distinctiveness analysis
Explanation: The claimant must show that the specifically asserted design features identify source. A prominent house mark offers a competing explanation for recognition. A rejects probative evidence categorically, B demands unnecessary factory identification, and D contradicts the treatment of product configuration.

Title: Question 9
Prompt: A salt company claims that the braided rim on its metal tin has acquired distinctiveness. Its advertising for four years repeatedly says, "Find our salt by the braided rim," while its sales data do not isolate why buyers choose the product. Which evidence most directly supports the claim?
A: Gross sales alone, regardless of what buyers perceive.
B: The repeated feature-focused instruction teaching purchasers to use the braided rim as a source cue.
C: Reviews discussing the tin's capacity but never the rim.
D: A competitor's copying of the rim solely because it strengthens stacking.
Answer: B
Authorities: Secondary-meaning factors; TBL Licensing
Explanation: Feature-focused or "look-for" advertising directly teaches consumers to treat the claimed rim as a source identifier. Sales and capacity reviews are indirect without a source link, and copying for stacking strength does not itself show source significance. A, C, and D are therefore weaker.

Title: Question 10
Prompt: A regional food festival licenses its compass seal to dozens of independent vendors that meet common standards. Buyers understand the seal as indicating approval and affiliation with the festival even though they know different vendors prepared the products. The multiple licensees:
A: Necessarily destroy source significance because one factory must make every marked item.
B: Matter only if each buyer has read the individual license agreement.
C: Make the seal generic as a matter of law.
D: Are consistent with source significance if buyers understand the seal as identifying a single source of authorization or control.
Answer: D
Authorities: Anonymous-source and licensing principles
Explanation: Trademark source can mean a single source of authorization or quality control even when consumers do not know its name and multiple licensees physically produce the goods. A imposes a single-factory requirement, B demands knowledge of legal arrangements, and C confuses licensing with genericness.

Title: Question 11
Prompt: Consumers use POD BITES as the ordinary name for small coffee portions sold in dissolvable pods. One seller was first and remains strongly associated with the product. Which fact is central to whether the phrase is generic?
A: Whether the seller was the first commercial user.
B: Whether the seller spent more on advertising than all competitors combined.
C: Whether the phrase appears in at least two dictionaries.
D: Whether the relevant public primarily understands the phrase as naming the product category rather than identifying source.
Answer: D
Authorities: Marvin Ginn; Kellogg primary-significance principle
Explanation: Genericness turns on the relevant genus and the public's primary understanding of the term as product name or source identifier. First use, advertising, and dictionaries may contribute evidence but are not substitutes for the central perception inquiry. A, B, and C make individual facts dispositive.

Title: Question 12
Prompt: An online event marketplace uses TICKETS.APP. An examiner argues that combining a generic product word with a top-level domain can never create a registrable designation. Which statement is best?
A: The combination is automatically generic without evidence of consumer perception.
B: The designation must be evaluated as a whole based on how relevant consumers understand it; no categorical rule resolves every generic-word-plus-domain combination.
C: Every domain name is inherently distinctive.
D: Registration would necessarily give the applicant exclusive rights to every ordinary use of "tickets."
Answer: B
Authorities: Booking.com whole-mark consumer-perception analysis
Explanation: The composite must be evaluated as a whole through consumer perception rather than a per se rule. That does not make every domain distinctive or grant control over ordinary generic usage. A applies the rejected categorical approach, while C and D overstate any possible protection.
