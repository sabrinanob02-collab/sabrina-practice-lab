## ccp-08-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Invited informant wearing camera glasses
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 1
Prompt: Working for police, informant Mabry Coyle visits Sterling Hask's house to buy stolen tools, wearing camera glasses that record video and audio. Hask invites her into the kitchen and garage, shows her the tools, and haggles over prices. When Hask steps outside for a call, Coyle wanders into his upstairs office, where she was never invited, and records papers on his desk. Which recordings are a search?
A: Both, because any recording inside a home is a search.
B: Neither, because once Hask invited her in, she could record anywhere in the house.
C: Only the office recording. A person who invites someone in takes the risk that the visitor is working for police and will record what she is shown in the areas and for the purposes of the invitation. Going into a room she was never invited into exceeds that invitation.
D: Only the kitchen and garage recordings, because video is more intrusive than audio.
Answer: C
Authorities: Hoffa v. United States; Lewis v. United States; United States v. White
Explanation: Rule: Under the misplaced-trust cases, a person has no Fourth Amendment protection against a confidant who turns out to be an informant and reports or records what was said and shown to her (Hoffa; Lewis; White, which allowed electronic recording by a wired informant). That protection extends only as far as the invitation: Lewis upheld an undercover agent's entry because he did only what he was invited in to do. Application: The kitchen and garage recordings capture what Hask chose to show. The office was outside the invitation's scope; entering it to record documents was a separate intrusion. Recording glasses are new, but the scope principle does not change. Why not the others: A ignores the misplaced-trust doctrine. B ignores the scope limit. D reverses the analysis by focusing on the medium rather than the invitation. Policy: Misplaced trust rests on the idea that we choose our confidants and bear the risk of betrayal. Limiting it to the invitation's scope stops undercover informants from becoming roving searchers inside homes.

## ccp-08-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Retail purchase records and the third-party doctrine
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 2
Prompt: After a fertilizer-based explosive is found under a bridge, Saltmarsh Junction investigators ask a farm-supply store, without a warrant, for its loyalty-program list of everyone who bought that fertilizer in large quantities in the past two months. The list includes Garrow Petty's name and two purchase dates. He moves to suppress. Was getting the list a search?
A: Yes, because all digitally stored records are now protected after the cell-site location case.
B: Yes, because asking for the list was a physical intrusion on the store's property.
C: Yes, but Petty cannot challenge it, because the records belong to the store.
D: Probably not. Petty voluntarily gave the store his name and purchases as part of an ordinary commercial transaction, and two purchase entries are nothing like a comprehensive, continuous record of his movements or private life. The third-party doctrine likely applies.
Answer: D
Authorities: Smith v. Maryland; Carpenter v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Smith holds that a person has no legitimate expectation of privacy in information voluntarily conveyed to a third party in the ordinary course of business. Carpenter declined to extend that rule to comprehensive historical location data, but called its holding narrow and left conventional business records untouched. Application: Loyalty-program purchase records are typical business records. Petty knowingly gave the store his name and purchases, and two transactions reveal little. That is Smith territory. Why not the others: A misreads Carpenter as covering all digital records. B is wrong because a request to a third party is not a trespass on the defendant's property. C misstates the analysis: the question is whether Petty had a protected expectation in the records, not whether he has standing to assert a search that harmed only the store. Policy: The third-party doctrine allows investigators to follow ordinary transaction trails without probable cause at the earliest stages. Carpenter's limits show the Court's concern that comprehensive data can become a surveillance tool, which a two-entry purchase list is not.

## ccp-08-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Viewing curtilage from a public alley
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 3
Prompt: Tamsin Rook's detached woodworking garage sits 40 feet behind her house, inside her fenced backyard. The fence has a gap between two boards facing a public alley. Walking down the alley, an officer stops at the gap and, without a flashlight or any device, sees through the garage's open door several stolen chainsaws matching a theft report. Was the officer's observation a search?
A: Yes, because a curtilage structure can never be observed without a warrant.
B: No. The garage is likely curtilage, but observation with the naked eye from a public place where the officer had a right to be is not a search, even if what he sees is within the curtilage. Entering the yard or garage would be a separate question.
C: No, because a detached garage is part of the open fields.
D: Yes, because the officer stopped and looked deliberately rather than glancing in passing.
Answer: B
Authorities: California v. Ciraolo; United States v. Dunn; Katz v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Curtilage is determined by proximity to the home, enclosure with it, use, and steps taken to protect it from observation (Dunn). Even so, Ciraolo holds that observing curtilage from a public vantage point where the officer has a right to be is not a search, because what a person knowingly exposes to the public is unprotected (Katz). Application: A garage 40 feet away, inside the fence and used for a hobby, is likely curtilage. But the officer stood in a public alley and looked through a gap without enhancement. Rook exposed the view to anyone in the alley. Entering to seize the saws would require a warrant or an exception. Why not the others: A contradicts Ciraolo. C misapplies open fields to a structure next to the home inside its fence. D adds a deliberateness requirement Ciraolo does not have. Policy: Curtilage protection guards against physical intrusion and against surveillance from places the public cannot go. It does not require police to ignore what any passerby could see.

## ccp-08-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Good faith and overbroad warrants reviewed by superiors
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 4
Prompt: After Orrin Lavell fires a sawed-off shotgun at his ex-partner during an argument, Detective Pym obtains a warrant to search Lavell's mother's house, where he stays, for "all firearms and ammunition" and "all evidence of gang membership." Lavell is a known gang member, but nothing ties the shooting to the gang. A supervisor and a deputy prosecutor reviewed the application before a magistrate approved it. Officers find gang ledgers. Is the evidence likely suppressed?
A: Probably not. Even if the gang clause was overbroad, officers who rely on a magistrate-approved warrant act in objective good faith unless the warrant is so plainly lacking in probable cause or particularity that no reasonable officer would rely on it. The prior review by a supervisor and prosecutor supports reasonable reliance.
B: Yes, because evidence seized under any overbroad clause is suppressed automatically.
C: Yes, all evidence from the house, because one overbroad clause voids the whole warrant.
D: Yes, because the good-faith exception does not apply to particularity defects.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Leon; Messerschmidt v. Millender; Groh v. Ramirez
Explanation: Rule: Under Leon, evidence obtained in objectively reasonable reliance on a warrant is not suppressed, unless the affidavit is so lacking in indicia of probable cause, or the warrant so facially deficient, that reliance is unreasonable. Messerschmidt, a civil immunity case built on Leon's standard, held that officers could reasonably rely on a warrant for all firearms and gang materials in a non-gang shooting, noting the review by superiors and a prosecutor. Groh shows the line: a warrant that describes nothing is facially deficient. Application: The gang clause may be overbroad, but it is not facially empty, and layers of review make reliance reasonable. Suppression is unlikely under Leon's good-faith exception. Why not the others: B and C ignore good faith and severability. D is wrong because good faith covers particularity defects unless they are obvious, as Groh shows. Policy: Good faith focuses suppression on culpable police conduct. Critics argue that relying on prosecutors and magistrates spreads responsibility so thin that no one is accountable for overbroad warrants.

## ccp-08-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: No-knock entry on particularized reasonable suspicion
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 5
Prompt: Officers hold a warrant to search Ravel Ostrander's house for illegal firearms. They know he fired at officers during an arrest two years ago, that he keeps a loaded rifle by his front door according to a reliable informant, and that four exterior cameras feed live video to a monitor inside. Without knocking, they breach the door. Ostrander argues the no-knock entry was unconstitutional. What result?
A: The entry was reasonable. Officers may dispense with knock-and-announce when they have reasonable suspicion, based on the particular circumstances, that announcing would be dangerous. His violent history toward police, the reported rifle by the door, and cameras that would warn him of their approach supply it.
B: The entry was unreasonable, because no-knock entries are never allowed unless a judge specifically authorizes them in the warrant.
C: The entry was reasonable, because officers may always skip announcing in firearms cases.
D: The entry was unreasonable, because skipping knock-and-announce requires probable cause of danger.
Answer: A
Authorities: Richards v. Wisconsin; Wilson v. Arkansas; Hudson v. Michigan
Explanation: Rule: Knock-and-announce is part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness (Wilson). Officers may skip it when they have reasonable suspicion that knocking would be dangerous, futile, or would allow evidence to be destroyed. That judgment must be made case by case, and blanket exceptions for categories of crime are not allowed (Richards). Application: Specific facts about Ostrander, not the category of crime, support suspicion of danger. Reasonable suspicion is a low bar, and these facts clear it. Even had the entry been unreasonable, Hudson would bar suppression. Why not the others: B is wrong because officers may decide on the scene based on reasonable suspicion; advance judicial authorization is not required. C states the blanket rule Richards rejected. D applies too high a standard. Policy: Case-by-case analysis protects the privacy and dignity interests of knock-and-announce while letting officers avoid real danger. Blanket rules would swallow the requirement in the areas where it matters most.

## ccp-08-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Factors that do not add up to probable cause
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 6
Prompt: During a lawful traffic stop in a neighborhood police consider high-crime, an officer notices that the driver, Linus Brack, is nervous and fidgety. A records check shows a drug arrest six years ago. The officer asks to search the car, and Brack refuses. The officer then arrests him for "drug possession" and searches the car, finding cocaine. Did the officer have probable cause to arrest?
A: Yes, because refusing to consent is itself a sign of guilt under the totality of the circumstances.
B: Yes, because a prior drug arrest establishes probable cause that a person is currently in possession.
C: No. Refusing consent cannot be counted against a suspect. Presence in a high-crime area and nervousness are weak on their own, and an old arrest adds little. Together they fall well short of a fair probability that Brack possessed drugs then.
D: Yes, because several factors were present, and several factors together always establish probable cause.
Answer: C
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Maryland v. Pringle; Schneckloth v. Bustamonte
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause requires a fair probability, under the totality of the circumstances, that a crime has been committed and that the person committed it (Gates; Pringle). Mere presence in a high-crime area does not establish probable cause, and refusing to consent, which is exercising a constitutional right, cannot supply it. Probable cause to search does not automatically follow from probable cause to arrest, and the reverse is also true. Application: Remove the refusal, and what remains is nervousness, location, and a six-year-old arrest. These are generic factors common to many innocent drivers, not particularized evidence of present possession. Why not the others: A penalizes exercising a right. B treats an old record as current evidence. D states a counting rule Gates rejected; the question is quality, not the number of factors. Policy: If refusal or generic traits could supply probable cause, the consent doctrine would become a trap and people in heavily policed areas would face arrest based on where they live. The probable-cause standard requires particularized grounds to protect against that.

## ccp-08-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Plain view during a digital search
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 7
Prompt: A warrant authorizes searching Ione Varrick's phone for text messages from March 1 to March 31 about fentanyl sales. While reading March messages, as the warrant allows, a detective opens a March 12 message that plainly describes a burglary Varrick committed. She then searches the phone's photo gallery for burglary evidence, finding pictures of stolen jewelry. What is admissible?
A: Neither, because plain view does not apply to digital searches.
B: The burglary message likely, because the detective was where the warrant authorized and its incriminating nature was immediately apparent. The photo-gallery search for burglary evidence went beyond the warrant and needed a new warrant.
C: Both, because once the detective saw evidence of another crime she could search the whole phone for it.
D: Neither, because the burglary message was not found inadvertently.
Answer: B
Authorities: Horton v. California; Arizona v. Hicks; Riley v. California; Particularity requirement
Explanation: Rule: Plain view allows seizing evidence when the officer is lawfully at the vantage point, has lawful access, and the item's incriminating character is immediately apparent (Horton; Hicks). Inadvertence is not required. A warrant's scope is defined by its particular description. Riley stresses the breadth of data on phones, which is why courts scrutinize digital plain view. Application: The detective was reading March messages under the warrant when she saw the burglary message, which was obviously incriminating. Going into the photo gallery to look for burglary evidence was a new search outside the warrant's terms (only March texts about fentanyl). Courts debate how far digital plain view should reach, but the gallery search goes well beyond any version of it. Why not the others: A is too categorical. C would turn a particular warrant into a general one. D relies on inadvertence, which Horton rejected. Policy: Allowing plain view in digital searches avoids requiring officers to unsee evidence. Because digital devices hold vast mixed data, strict scope limits keep particular warrants from becoming general rummages.

## ccp-08-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Searching an arrestee's bag after he is secured
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 8
Prompt: Officers arrest Marlow Theel on a sidewalk for robbery. He drops his backpack, is handcuffed, and is placed in the back of a locked patrol car. A minute later, an officer unzips the backpack, still on the sidewalk 15 feet from the car, and finds a stolen wallet. Theel argues the search was not a valid search incident to arrest. What is the best analysis?
A: The search was valid, because the full-search rule covers any property near the arrest scene regardless of the arrestee's location.
B: The search was valid, because the limits on searching secured arrestees apply only to vehicles, so nothing restricts searching personal property.
C: The search was valid, because the backpack might contain evidence of the robbery.
D: Courts disagree on this, but the strongest analysis under the assigned cases is that the search-incident exception rests on the arrestee's ability to reach weapons or destroy evidence. Once Theel was handcuffed and locked in the car, the backpack was no longer within his reach, so the reasoning applied to secured arrestees in the vehicle context suggests the exception did not apply. A warrant or another exception was needed.
Answer: D
Authorities: Chimel v. California; Arizona v. Gant; United States v. Robinson
Explanation: Rule: Chimel limits searches incident to arrest to the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control, meaning the area from which he could grab a weapon or destroy evidence. Robinson covers the person and items on the person. Gant held that when the arrestee is secured and cannot access the vehicle, the safety rationale disappears. Application: The backpack was not on Theel's person when searched; it was on the sidewalk while he was locked in a patrol car. Lower courts split on whether Gant's logic reaches containers outside vehicles, but Chimel's own justification supports Theel. Why not the others: A extends Robinson beyond the person. B misreads Gant as a narrow car-only rule that leaves no limits elsewhere; Chimel's limits still apply. C borrows Gant's evidence prong, which the Court tied to vehicle searches. Policy: Linking the exception to its rationale prevents arrests from becoming automatic licenses for full searches. Officers who want to look inside a secured bag can hold it and seek a warrant.

## ccp-08-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Pretextual impoundment and inventory
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 9
Prompt: Saltmarsh Junction's written policy says an arrestee's car "shall be impounded only when no licensed driver is available to take it." After arresting Fenna Quist on an old warrant, an officer refuses to let her licensed passenger, who is sober and willing, drive the car away. He impounds it and inventories it, finding stolen checks. He later admits he wanted to "see what was in there." Is the evidence admissible under the inventory exception?
A: Yes, because any car searched under a written inventory policy is validly inventoried.
B: No. A valid inventory requires a lawful impoundment conducted under standardized criteria. Here the impoundment departed from the department's own policy, because a licensed driver was available, which shows the inventory was a pretext for an investigative search.
C: Yes, because an officer's subjective motive is always irrelevant under the Fourth Amendment.
D: No, because inventories require probable cause.
Answer: B
Authorities: Colorado v. Bertine; Illinois v. Lafayette
Explanation: Rule: Inventory searches are valid without a warrant or probable cause when they follow standardized procedures, serve administrative purposes, and are not a pretext for investigation (Bertine). The decision to impound must itself follow standardized criteria. Application: The policy allowed impounding only when no licensed driver was available. One was. Departing from the policy defeats the standardized-procedures foundation, and the officer's admission confirms the investigative purpose. Why not the others: A ignores that the policy was not actually followed. C overstates the irrelevance of motive. Inventory doctrine expressly asks whether the search was a pretext, and departure from policy is objective evidence of one. D imposes an inapplicable standard. Policy: Inventories are tolerated because they serve caretaking functions. If officers could impound cars at will to search them, the exception would be a back door around the probable-cause requirement.

## ccp-08-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Destructive border searches
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 10
Prompt: As Quillon Arvane's sailboat arrives at Saltmarsh Junction from abroad, customs officers, with no suspicion, drill six holes through the hull's interior paneling and fiberglass to check for hidden compartments. They find none, and the boat takes on water. They also unscrew and remove an engine-access panel, which they put back, and find smuggled cash. Which statement is most accurate?
A: Both searches were valid, because at the border any search, however destructive, is permitted without suspicion.
B: Both searches were invalid, because searching a vessel at the border requires probable cause.
C: Removing and replacing the panel resembles the disassembly and reassembly upheld at the border without suspicion. Drilling holes that damage the vessel's structure raises the question the Court reserved about potentially destructive searches, and Arvane has a strong argument that it required at least reasonable suspicion.
D: The border exception does not apply at a seaport.
Answer: C
Authorities: United States v. Flores-Montano; Border search exception
Explanation: Rule: At the border, routine searches need no suspicion. Flores-Montano upheld removing, disassembling, and reassembling a gas tank without suspicion, emphasizing that the process caused no lasting damage. The Court left open whether some searches might be so destructive that they require particularized suspicion. Application: Removing and replacing a panel fits Flores-Montano. Drilling holes that compromise the hull is the kind of damaging search the Court reserved, and lower courts have required reasonable suspicion for similar actions. Why not the others: A treats Flores-Montano as approving unlimited destruction. B imposes a probable-cause rule border doctrine rejects. D ignores that ports of entry are the border or its functional equivalent. Policy: The border exception reflects the government's sovereign interest in controlling what enters the country. Requiring suspicion for highly destructive searches protects travelers from serious property damage without suspicion.

## ccp-08-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consent induced by a false claim of a warrant
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 11
Prompt: Officers who lack probable cause tell Imelda Strathe at her door, "We've got a warrant coming anyway, so you might as well let us in now and save us breaking the door." No warrant application exists. Strathe steps aside, and the officers find stolen goods. Two hours later they get a warrant based in part on what they saw. Is her consent valid?
A: Yes, because officers may use deception to obtain consent.
B: Yes, because she did not have to be told she could refuse.
C: Yes, because officers did later get a warrant, so the deception made no difference.
D: Likely not. Consent obtained by a false claim of lawful authority is acquiescence to authority rather than free choice. Telling her a warrant was coming, which suggested refusal was pointless and her door would be broken, makes her consent involuntary under the totality of the circumstances.
Answer: D
Authorities: Schneckloth v. Bustamonte; Illinois v. Rodriguez; Murray v. United States
Explanation: Rule: The government must prove consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances (Schneckloth). A claim of lawful authority, especially a false one, can reduce consent to submission. Knowledge of the right to refuse is relevant but not required. Application: The false warrant statement, plus the threat of a broken door, told Strathe that refusing would be futile. That is submission, not consent. The later warrant cannot cure the problem if it relied on what was seen in the unlawful entry; an independent source must be genuinely independent (Murray). Why not the others: A overstates permissible deception; trickery about authority differs from undercover deception. B is true but does not address the false claim of authority. C ignores that the later warrant was itself tainted. Policy: Consent searches depend on real choice. Allowing false claims of authority would turn consent into a formality, and it would erode public trust in what officers say about their legal powers.

## ccp-08-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Welfare-check entry on objectively alarming facts
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 12
Prompt: A neighbor calls police: 84-year-old Cyril Bannock, who lives alone and uses a walker, hasn't been seen in three days. Newspapers are piling up, his car is in the driveway, and a strong foul odor is coming from the house. Officers knock repeatedly, get no answer, force the door, and find Bannock collapsed but alive. In the hallway they also see unregistered firearms. Was the entry lawful?
A: Yes. The facts give an objectively reasonable basis to believe an occupant was seriously injured or in need of immediate aid, so emergency aid justified entering to find and help him, and items in plain view during that entry may be seized.
B: No, because emergency aid requires probable cause that a crime has occurred.
C: No, because officers heard no screams or calls for help.
D: Yes, and once inside the officers could search the house for other evidence of crimes.
Answer: A
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs emergency assistance. The test is objective and does not require ironclad proof (Fisher). The scope is limited to the emergency. Items in plain view during a lawful entry may be seized (Horton). Application: An elderly, mobility-limited man living alone, unseen for days, with his car home, newspapers piling up, and a foul odor: a reasonable officer would believe he might be seriously hurt or dead. The firearms were in plain view along the path to finding him. Why not the others: B confuses emergency aid with a criminal-investigation standard. C demands a particular kind of evidence the objective test does not require. D exceeds the emergency's scope. Policy: Emergency aid reflects society's expectation that police will help people in danger, especially vulnerable people living alone. Scope limits keep a welfare check from becoming a criminal search.

## ccp-08-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Terry stop length and de facto arrest
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 13
Prompt: With reasonable suspicion that Wystan Corle is the man seen breaking into parked cars, officers stop him, handcuff him, and place him in a patrol car while they wait for the lead detective, who is busy and arrives 90 minutes later. The detective questions him and gets incriminating admissions. Officers had no probable cause during the wait. What is the best analysis?
A: The detention was lawful, because a Terry stop has no time limit as long as officers have reasonable suspicion.
B: The detention was lawful, because Corle cooperated and did not object to waiting.
C: The detention was lawful, because handcuffing does not affect a Terry analysis.
D: The detention likely became a de facto arrest. A Terry stop must last no longer than necessary to carry out its purpose, with officers acting diligently. Holding a handcuffed suspect in a patrol car for 90 minutes for a detective's convenience exceeds those limits, and without probable cause the admissions are fruit of an unlawful seizure.
Answer: D
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Florida v. Royer; Brown v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: A Terry stop must be temporary, last no longer than necessary to accomplish its purpose, and use the least intrusive means reasonably available. Officers must pursue the investigation diligently (Royer). A stop that goes beyond these limits becomes an arrest requiring probable cause. Statements obtained by exploiting an unlawful seizure are suppressed unless attenuated (Brown). Application: Ninety minutes in handcuffs in a patrol car, waiting for a busy detective, is neither brief nor diligent. Without probable cause, the arrest was unlawful, and the admissions came directly from it. Why not the others: A ignores Terry's duration limit. B confuses submission with consent. C ignores that the degree of restraint is relevant to whether a stop has become an arrest. Policy: Terry allows a limited intrusion on less than probable cause because it is brief. Letting stops stretch for an investigator's convenience would erase the line between stops and arrests and the protection probable cause provides.

## ccp-08-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A momentary pause is not submission
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 14
Prompt: A patrol car with lights flashing pulls alongside Tobias Venn, who is walking. The officer says over the loudspeaker, "Stop right there." Venn stops for about two seconds, looks at the officer, then sprints away. Mid-chase he tosses a handgun into a dumpster. He is caught a block later. Assume the officer had no reasonable suspicion. Should the gun be suppressed as fruit of an unlawful seizure?
A: No. A seizure by show of authority requires submission. A two-second pause before fleeing is generally not submission, so Venn was not seized until he was caught, and he abandoned the gun beforehand.
B: Yes, because he was seized the moment the lights came on.
C: Yes, because his brief stop was submission, so he was seized and the gun was the fruit of that seizure.
D: Yes, because without reasonable suspicion any evidence found during the encounter must be suppressed.
Answer: A
Authorities: California v. Hodari D.; United States v. Mendenhall; Torres v. Madrid
Explanation: Rule: A show of authority does not seize a person who does not submit (Hodari D.). Property discarded during flight is abandoned, not the fruit of a seizure. Torres addresses seizures by physical force, which did not occur here. Application: The order to stop was a show of authority. A two-second pause followed by immediate flight is not genuine submission; most courts require more than a momentary hesitation. Because Venn was not seized when he threw the gun, it was abandoned, not seized. Why not the others: B treats a show of authority as a seizure without submission. C treats a fleeting pause as submission, which courts generally reject. D skips the threshold question of whether there was a seizure to which the evidence could be fruit. Policy: Hodari encourages compliance by denying flight any constitutional benefit and gives police a clear rule. Critics argue it lets officers make baseless shows of authority with no consequence whenever the person runs.

## ccp-08-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Conduct that shocks the conscience
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 15
Prompt: Officers see Pell Drummond swallow two small baggies as they approach. They drive him to a hospital, where, at their direction and over his repeated objections, staff restrain him and force a tube through his nose into his stomach to administer an emetic until he vomits the baggies. Which statement best describes the constitutional problem?
A: There is none, because destruction of evidence made it urgent to recover the baggies.
B: There is none, because medical staff, not police, performed the procedure.
C: Forcibly invading his body in this way, at police direction and over his objection, is conduct that shocks the conscience and violates due process, so the evidence is inadmissible.
D: It raises only a Fourth Amendment issue, and suppression is not available for intrusions into the body.
Answer: C
Authorities: Rochin v. California; Michigan v. Fisher
Explanation: Rule: Under Rochin, government conduct that shocks the conscience, such as forcibly pumping a suspect's stomach to recover swallowed evidence, violates the Due Process Clause and makes the evidence inadmissible. Exigent circumstances do not suspend those limits; even when an exigency exists, police conduct remains subject to constitutional constraints. Application: The facts track the brutality Rochin condemned: physical restraint and a forced tube over repeated objection, done at police direction to get evidence. The exigency of swallowed drugs does not excuse it. Staff acting at police direction act for the state. Why not the others: A ignores Rochin. B ignores state action through people police direct. D misstates the law; Rochin excluded the evidence. Policy: Some methods of obtaining evidence are too offensive to human dignity to be tolerated, whatever the evidence's value. Rochin's rule protects the integrity of the justice system and bodily autonomy.

## ccp-08-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Confronting a silent suspect with evidence as interrogation
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 16
Prompt: After warnings, Ferris Holloway says, "I'm not saying anything." The detective, without speaking, lays out on the table in front of him six surveillance stills showing him carrying boxes out of a looted electronics store, taps each photo, and waits. After two minutes Holloway says, "Those boxes were empty." Was this interrogation?
A: No, because the detective never spoke.
B: Likely yes. Interrogation includes actions police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Silently confronting a custodial suspect with a display of the evidence against him, and waiting, is designed to provoke a reply, and it fails to honor his invocation.
C: No, because Holloway's response was volunteered.
D: Only if the detective had followed the display with an express question.
Answer: B
Authorities: Rhode Island v. Innis; Michigan v. Mosley; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Interrogation includes express questioning and its functional equivalent, meaning words or actions that police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response (Innis). After an invocation of silence, police must scrupulously honor it (Mosley). Application: Laying out evidence, tapping each photo, and waiting is an action meant to get a response. It is not a step normally attendant to arrest and custody, and its whole point is to provoke an answer. Holloway's statement is the result, not a volunteered remark. Why not the others: A treats silence as a safe harbor, but Innis expressly includes "actions." C mislabels a provoked statement as volunteered. D adds a question requirement Innis does not impose. Policy: Without a functional-equivalent test, officers could pressure suspects through staged confrontations rather than questions and empty the invocation of meaning.

## ccp-08-q17
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Unintentional unwarned statement followed by warned confession
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 17
Prompt: Two officers go to Annick Prowse's apartment with an arrest warrant for a jewelry theft. In her living room, one officer, who mistakenly thinks questioning before the formal arrest does not require warnings, asks, "Did you take the necklaces?" She says, "Yes." An hour later at the station, another officer gives full warnings. She waives and gives a detailed confession. Nothing suggests a deliberate two-step strategy. What is admissible?
A: Neither statement, because the second confession is fruit of the first.
B: Both statements, because she later waived her rights.
C: The warned station-house confession only. The living-room answer was unwarned custodial interrogation and is excluded, but a voluntary unwarned statement does not taint a later knowing and voluntary warned confession when police did not deliberately use a two-step technique.
D: Neither statement, because once a suspect has made an unwarned admission the later warned one is involuntary as a matter of law.
Answer: C
Authorities: Oregon v. Elstad; Missouri v. Seibert; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Unwarned custodial statements are excluded from the prosecution's case-in-chief (Miranda). Elstad held that a later warned confession is admissible if knowing and voluntary, even after an earlier voluntary but unwarned admission, rejecting the cat-out-of-the-bag theory. Seibert limits this only for deliberate two-step strategies. Application: Prowse was in custody under an arrest warrant, and the question was interrogation, so the first answer is excluded. The officer's mistake was not a deliberate strategy, the first statement was voluntary, and warnings came later at a different place from a different officer. Elstad controls. Why not the others: A applies fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree reasoning Elstad rejected for Miranda violations. B ignores that the first statement remains excluded. D adopts a presumption Elstad rejected. Policy: Elstad treats Miranda as a prophylactic rule whose exclusionary reach should be limited when officers err in good faith. Seibert prevents police from turning that leniency into a planned evasion of Miranda.

## ccp-08-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Suspect-initiated conversation after invoking counsel
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 18
Prompt: After clearly asking for a lawyer, Gareth Ilves is being moved to the county jail. On the way he asks the transporting officer, "So what's going to happen to me now?" The officer says, "You asked for a lawyer, so you don't have to talk to me, but if you want to, I'll listen." Ilves says he wants to talk. The officer gives fresh warnings, Ilves waives, and he confesses. Is the confession admissible?
A: No, because only a request about the case itself can count as reinitiating communication.
B: Likely yes. A question showing a willingness and desire for a general discussion about the investigation, rather than a routine request incidental to custody, can be initiation by the suspect. Police may then question him if he validly waives, which the reminder of his rights and fresh warnings support.
C: No, because after a request for counsel police may never again question the suspect without counsel present.
D: Yes, because once a suspect reinitiates contact, his waiver is automatically valid.
Answer: B
Authorities: Oregon v. Bradshaw; Edwards v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Edwards bars police-initiated interrogation after a suspect requests counsel unless the suspect himself initiates further communication. The Bradshaw plurality treated "Well, what is going to happen to me now?" as initiation because it showed a desire for a generalized discussion about the investigation, as distinct from routine requests such as for water or a phone call. Even after initiation, the prosecution must show a knowing and voluntary waiver. Application: Ilves's question closely tracks Bradshaw. The officer reminded him of his rights, gave fresh warnings, and got a waiver. Why not the others: A narrows initiation beyond Bradshaw. C misstates Edwards, which allows suspect-initiated conversation. D skips the separate waiver inquiry. Policy: Edwards protects suspects who have said they need a lawyer from being worn down. Allowing suspects to reopen the conversation themselves respects their autonomy without letting police initiate it.

## ccp-08-q19
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Post-indictment photo arrays and the right to counsel
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 19
Prompt: After Linnea Sorrow is indicted for armed robbery, a detective shows the victim a six-photo array over a video call. Sorrow's photo is one of the six. Her lawyer is not told about it and does not attend. The victim picks Sorrow. Sorrow moves to exclude the identification for violating her right to counsel. What result?
A: Exclude it, because every identification procedure after indictment is a critical stage.
B: Exclude it automatically, because uncounseled post-indictment identifications are excluded per se.
C: Exclude it, because the right to counsel attached when she was indicted, so it covers all identification procedures.
D: Deny the motion as to the right to counsel. There is no right to counsel at a photo display, even after indictment, because the defendant is not present and the display can be reconstructed. Any challenge must proceed under due process suggestiveness analysis.
Answer: D
Authorities: United States v. Ash; United States v. Wade; Gilbert v. California; Manson v. Brathwaite
Explanation: Rule: Wade and Gilbert make post-charge corporeal lineups a critical stage requiring counsel, with per se exclusion of uncounseled lineup identifications. Ash held there is no right to counsel at photographic displays, even after indictment, because the accused is not present to be misled or overpowered and counsel can reconstruct the display at trial. Why not the others: A and C overstate attachment; attachment matters only at a critical stage, and photo displays are not one. B applies Gilbert's rule, which is limited to lineups. Policy: Ash reasons that counsel's presence matters where the accused faces the prosecution and cannot reconstruct the event, as at a live lineup. Critics argue that photo arrays are just as susceptible to suggestion, which is why due process screening remains important.

## ccp-08-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Good-faith reliance on a later-invalidated statute
Set: CCP-08
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Saltmarsh Junction
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 5, Question 20
Prompt: A state statute authorizes warrantless inspections of scrap-metal dealers' records and yards. Relying on it, officers inspect Hollin Varga's scrap yard and find stolen copper wire. A year later, a court strikes the statute down as lacking adequate limits on officer discretion. Varga moves to suppress. What result?
A: Deny suppression. Officers who reasonably rely on a statute authorizing warrantless searches act in good faith, unless the statute was clearly unconstitutional when they acted, because excluding the evidence would not deter officers who were following the legislature's direction.
B: Suppress, because evidence from a search authorized only by an unconstitutional statute must always be excluded.
C: Suppress, because the good-faith exception applies only to warrants issued by judges.
D: Suppress, because officers are presumed to know that statutes lacking discretion limits are invalid.
Answer: A
Authorities: Illinois v. Krull; United States v. Leon; New York v. Burger
Explanation: Rule: Krull extended Leon's good-faith exception to officers who act in objectively reasonable reliance on a statute authorizing warrantless administrative searches, later held unconstitutional. The exception does not apply if the legislature wholly abandoned its responsibility to pass constitutional laws, or if a reasonable officer should have known the statute was unconstitutional. Burger supplies the closely regulated industry framework under which such statutes are judged. Application: Scrap dealers are a plausible closely regulated industry, and the defect, insufficient limits on discretion, was not obvious until a court ruled. The officers reasonably relied on the statute. Why not the others: B states the automatic exclusion rule Krull rejected. C ignores that Krull extends good faith beyond warrants. D expects officers to predict judicial rulings. Policy: Exclusion aims to deter police, not legislators. Officers following a statute have done what we ask. Critics argue that applying good faith to statutes removes any check on unconstitutional laws until someone without a criminal case brings a civil challenge.
