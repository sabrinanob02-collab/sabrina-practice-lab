## ccp-06-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Through-the-wall radar and the home
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 1
Prompt: Before serving an arrest warrant, Ironbridge Falls officers stand in the public hallway outside Juniper Vale's apartment and hold a handheld radar unit against the air near the door. It detects breathing and movement through walls and shows that two people are in the back bedroom. The device is sold only to police and the military. Was using it a search?
A: No, because it showed only how many people were present and where, not intimate details.
B: No, because the officers stood in a common hallway where they had a right to be.
C: Only if the device physically touched the door or wall.
D: Yes. Using technology not in general public use to learn details about the inside of a home that otherwise could not be learned without physically entering is a search, whether or not the details are intimate.
Answer: D
Authorities: Kyllo v. United States; United States v. Jones; United States v. Karo
Explanation: Rule: Under Kyllo, using sense-enhancing technology not in general public use to obtain information about the interior of a home that could not otherwise be obtained without physical intrusion is a search. Kyllo rejected any distinction based on whether the details were intimate, because all details of the home are protected. Karo similarly held that monitoring a beeper inside a home is a search. Application: Radar that shows the number and location of people in a bedroom reveals information from inside the home, and it is a police-only device. Why not the others: A relies on the intimate-details distinction Kyllo rejected. B misses the point: lawful presence in the hallway does not authorize using technology to see through the walls. C confuses Jones's trespass test, an additional test, with a requirement; Kyllo found a search with no contact. Policy: Kyllo draws a firm line at the walls of the home so that improving technology does not quietly erode the core of the Fourth Amendment. The cost is that officers must get a warrant before using such tools, even for safety, absent an exigency.

## ccp-06-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Smart-meter data and the third-party doctrine
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 2
Prompt: Ironbridge Falls requires every home to have a city-installed "smart" electric meter that records usage every 15 minutes. Without a warrant, investigators obtain eight months of readings for Thaddeus Corr's house from the city utility. Spikes at night suggest grow lights. On a motion to suppress, which fact most helps Corr argue that this was a search despite the third-party doctrine?
A: The data was held by a utility rather than by Corr.
B: The meter was mandatory, so Corr did not meaningfully choose to share anything, and the fine-grained readings reveal activity inside the home, such as when people sleep, cook, and run appliances.
C: The request for the data was signed by a prosecutor.
D: Corr always paid his electric bills on time.
Answer: B
Authorities: Smith v. Maryland; Carpenter v. United States; Kyllo v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Smith treats information voluntarily conveyed to a third party as unprotected. Carpenter refused to extend the doctrine where the data was revealing, comprehensive, and not truly voluntarily shared, because carrying a phone is effectively required for modern life. Kyllo protects information about activity inside the home. Application: The strongest defense facts are involuntariness (a mandatory meter, so no real choice to convey) and revealing in-home detail (15-minute readings infer domestic routines, much as Kyllo's thermal imager did). Lower courts are divided on smart meters. Why not the others: A helps the government; third-party holding is the basis of Smith. C is irrelevant to whether the acquisition was a search. D has nothing to do with privacy. Policy: The third-party doctrine rests on assumption of risk, which makes sense only when sharing is a real choice. Treating mandatory, home-revealing data as unprotected would let technology defeat Kyllo's line at the home. The government responds that utility records are routine business records and that a warrant requirement would hamper investigations.

## ccp-06-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Open fields vs. a separate dwelling on the property
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 3
Prompt: Hollis Wren rents a converted barn 300 yards from his parents' farmhouse, across a pasture posted "No Trespassing." He sleeps, cooks, and keeps his belongings there and pays his parents rent. Without a warrant, officers climb the pasture fence, walk to the barn, open the barn's front door, and go inside, where they find stolen tools. Which statement is correct?
A: Both crossing the pasture and entering the barn were searches, because the land was posted and fenced.
B: Neither was a search, because the barn sits far outside the curtilage of the main farmhouse.
C: Crossing the pasture was not a search, because open fields are unprotected even when posted. Entering the barn was a search, because it is Wren's own home, and the inside of a dwelling is protected no matter how far it is from someone else's house.
D: Neither was a search, because Wren lacks standing on his parents' land.
Answer: C
Authorities: Oliver v. United States; United States v. Dunn; Payton v. New York; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Open fields beyond the curtilage are not protected, even if fenced and posted (Oliver). Curtilage is judged by the Dunn factors in relation to a home. A dwelling itself gets the Fourth Amendment's strongest protection (Payton). Standing depends on the defendant's own legitimate expectation of privacy (Rakas). Application: The pasture is open fields, so walking across it is not a search. But the barn is Wren's home: he lives, sleeps, and keeps his belongings there and pays rent. Its distance from the farmhouse is irrelevant, because the question is not whether the barn is the farmhouse's curtilage but whether it is itself a home. Entering it is a search. Why not the others: A contradicts Oliver. B misframes the barn as an outbuilding of the farmhouse, as in Dunn, instead of a separate residence. D ignores that Wren, as a paying occupant, has his own expectation of privacy. Policy: Open fields reflects the idea that activities in fields are not the intimate ones the Fourth Amendment protects. Courts protect the home by its function, wherever it is, so rural renters and people in nontraditional housing receive the same core protection.

## ccp-06-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reasonable mistakes in executing a warrant: multi-unit discovery mid-search
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 4
Prompt: After a reasonable investigation, officers believe the top floor of 40 Ferry Road is one apartment, and they get a warrant for "the third-floor apartment." Inside, they search the first bedroom and find nothing. Then they notice two separate mailboxes and two doorbells on the landing and realize the floor is divided into two units, one belonging to a stranger, Rosalind Teague. They keep searching Teague's unit anyway and find stolen jewelry there. Is the jewelry admissible?
A: No. The warrant and the initial entry were valid because the officers' mistake was objectively reasonable, but once they knew or should have known the floor held two units, they had to confine the search to the unit the warrant was meant to cover.
B: Yes, because a warrant valid when issued authorizes everything done under it.
C: No, because the warrant described the wrong place, so it was void from the start.
D: Yes, because the officers continued to act in subjective good faith.
Answer: A
Authorities: Maryland v. Garrison; United States v. Leon
Explanation: Rule: Garrison holds that a warrant's validity is judged by the information officers disclosed, or had a duty to discover, when they sought it, and that execution is judged by whether officers' factual mistakes were objectively reasonable. Officers who reasonably but mistakenly believe a floor holds one unit may search, but once they are on notice of the error they must stop searching the area not covered. Application: The first entry and the first bedroom search were reasonable mistakes. After seeing two mailboxes and two doorbells, the officers knew the truth. Searching Teague's unit after that was not a reasonable mistake. Why not the others: B ignores that execution must stay reasonable. C ignores Garrison's rule that a warrant based on a reasonable, though mistaken, description is valid. D substitutes subjective good faith for the objective test; Leon's good faith concerns reliance on a warrant, not continuing after known error. Policy: Garrison balances practical policing (officers cannot know everything) against the core rule that warrants must describe particular places. Requiring officers to stop once they learn the truth keeps reasonable mistakes from licensing knowing intrusions.

## ccp-06-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Searching a visitor who arrives during warrant execution
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 5
Prompt: While officers execute a warrant to search a tavern for illegal gambling records, Percival Ash walks in to buy a sandwich. Officers know nothing about him. One officer pats him down "since gambling places can be dangerous," feels a pill bottle, and removes it. It holds unprescribed opioids. Is the bottle admissible?
A: Yes, because a warrant to search premises authorizes searching everyone present.
B: No. A premises warrant does not authorize searching or frisking people merely because they are present. A frisk requires individualized reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous, and nothing pointed to Ash. Even a lawful frisk would not have allowed removing a pill bottle that was not a weapon.
C: Yes, because officers may detain and search anyone who enters during a warrant execution.
D: Yes, because some locations are so dangerous that frisking everyone present is always reasonable.
Answer: B
Authorities: Ybarra v. Illinois; Terry v. Ohio; Minnesota v. Dickerson; Michigan v. Summers
Explanation: Rule: A warrant to search premises does not authorize searching people found there merely because of their presence. Each search or frisk of a person needs individualized justification (Ybarra). A Terry frisk requires a reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous, and it is limited to weapons (Terry; Dickerson). Summers allows detaining occupants, not searching them. Application: Ash was a random customer with no connection to the gambling. A general sense that a place is dangerous is not individualized suspicion, and a pill bottle is not a weapon. Why not the others: A and C extend the warrant beyond its terms and beyond Summers. D creates a location-based rule Ybarra rejects. Policy: Individualized suspicion is central to the Fourth Amendment's protection against dragnet searches. Without it, anyone who happens to be in a business when police arrive could be searched, which would discourage ordinary public life.

## ccp-06-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Odor of marijuana as probable cause after legalization
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 6
Prompt: In a state where adults may lawfully possess up to two ounces of marijuana, an officer making a traffic stop smells burnt marijuana coming from Calla Bryn's car. He sees nothing else suspicious. On the odor alone, he searches the trunk and finds five pounds of marijuana packaged in vacuum-sealed bags. Was there probable cause for the search?
A: Yes. Odor of marijuana always establishes probable cause, whatever state law says.
B: No. Odor can never contribute to probable cause once some possession is legal.
C: No, because probable cause to search a car requires an officer to see contraband.
D: This is unsettled, and courts are split. Because lawful possession is now possible, odor alone is a weaker indicator that a crime has occurred. Most analyses treat odor as one factor in the totality, so odor alone of burnt marijuana may not establish a fair probability that evidence of a crime, such as an illegal quantity, is in the trunk.
Answer: D
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Carroll v. United States; California v. Acevedo
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause is a fair probability, on the totality of the circumstances, that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in the place searched (Gates). Under the automobile exception, probable cause defines where officers may search (Carroll; Acevedo). Application: Where small amounts are legal, the smell of burnt marijuana fits lawful conduct as easily as unlawful conduct, and it says even less about the trunk, since burnt marijuana suggests recent smoking in the cabin. Courts have divided over whether odor alone still suffices. The best answer recognizes odor as one factor whose weight has diminished. Why not the others: A ignores that probable cause depends on what the law makes criminal. B goes too far the other way, because odor remains relevant. C invents an observation requirement. Policy: Treating odor as automatic probable cause after legalization would let a lawful activity justify full searches and invite pretextual stops. Courts cautious about changing the rule stress officer safety and the difficulty of determining quantity from odor.

## ccp-06-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Plain view: immediately apparent incriminating character
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 7
Prompt: Officers execute a warrant to search Garrick Holm's house for stolen rifles. On his kitchen counter they see eleven prescription opioid bottles. Without touching or moving them, they can read that the labels bear eight different patients' names, several of which match names on a list of victims from recent pharmacy burglaries that the officers have with them. They seize the bottles. Is the seizure valid?
A: Yes. The officers were lawfully present under the warrant, could read the labels without moving anything, and the many names matching the burglary victims gave probable cause at a glance that the bottles were stolen.
B: No, because the officers expected they might find stolen drugs, so the discovery was not inadvertent.
C: No, because officers may seize only items named in the warrant.
D: No, because only contraband that is illegal to possess under any circumstances can be seized in plain view.
Answer: A
Authorities: Horton v. California; Arizona v. Hicks
Explanation: Rule: Plain view permits a warrantless seizure if the officer is lawfully at the vantage point, has lawful access to the object, and its incriminating character is immediately apparent, meaning there is probable cause without further search (Horton; Hicks). Application: The warrant put officers in the kitchen. Reading visible labels is not a search, unlike moving equipment to reveal serial numbers in Hicks. Eight different patients' names, matched to burglary victims, gives probable cause that the bottles are stolen. Why not the others: B relies on the inadvertence requirement Horton eliminated. C ignores plain view, which exists to permit seizing unnamed items. D is too narrow; plain view reaches evidence and stolen property, not just per se contraband. Policy: Plain view avoids the absurd result of officers who are lawfully present ignoring obvious evidence. The probable-cause and no-manipulation limits keep it from becoming a general search.

## ccp-06-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Booking DNA swabs for minor offenses
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 8
Prompt: An Ironbridge Falls ordinance requires a cheek swab for DNA from every arrestee, including those booked for misdemeanors. Briar Kettle is arrested for shoplifting $30 of groceries and swabbed at booking. Her profile matches DNA from an unsolved burglary. Which statement best describes the constitutional analysis?
A: The swab was plainly valid, because the Supreme Court has held that DNA may be taken from any arrestee for any offense.
B: The swab was invalid, because collecting DNA always requires a warrant and probable cause.
C: This is an extension question. The Court upheld swabbing arrestees for serious offenses as a reasonable booking procedure for identification. Kettle's strongest argument is that the identification rationale and the balance of interests do not carry over to petty offenses, which makes the program look like a search for evidence of unrelated crimes.
D: The swab was not a search at all, because a cheek swab is so brief and painless.
Answer: C
Authorities: Maryland v. King; Illinois v. Lafayette; Florence v. Board of Chosen Freeholders
Explanation: Rule: King upheld DNA swabs of people arrested for serious offenses and held for them, treating the swab as a search but a reasonable booking procedure serving identification, comparable to fingerprinting. Booking searches generally are justified by institutional needs (Lafayette inventory; Florence jail searches). Application: King's holding is limited to serious offenses, and the dissent argued its real purpose was solving cold cases. Applying it to a $30 shoplifting arrest pushes the identification rationale to its limit. Kettle's best argument is that the balance changes and the purpose looks like ordinary crime-solving without suspicion. Why not the others: A overstates King's holding. B ignores that King upheld swabs without a warrant. D contradicts King, which treated the swab as a search, though a reasonable one. Policy: Supporters see DNA as modern fingerprinting that can also exonerate the innocent. Critics warn that collecting DNA from minor arrests builds a genetic database through arrests that often fall disproportionately on some communities.

## ccp-06-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Inventory searches: standardized criteria and discretion
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 9
Prompt: After lawfully impounding Fennimore Pratt's car for unpaid tickets, an officer inventories it. The department's written policy says only, "Officers may inventory vehicles as they see fit." The officer pries open a locked briefcase in the trunk and finds forged checks. Is the evidence admissible under the inventory exception?
A: Yes, because an inventory may always include opening any closed container.
B: No, because inventory searches require probable cause.
C: No. An inventory search must follow standardized criteria that limit officer discretion, including how closed containers are treated. A policy leaving everything to the officer's judgment cannot support prying open a locked briefcase.
D: Yes, because the officer said he wanted to protect Pratt's property.
Answer: C
Authorities: Colorado v. Bertine; Illinois v. Lafayette
Explanation: Rule: Inventory searches are reasonable without a warrant or probable cause because they protect the owner's property, protect police from claims, and protect against danger. They must follow standardized criteria or established routine that limits officer discretion, and they may not be a pretext for an evidence search (Bertine, which upheld opening containers under standardized procedures). Application: "As they see fit" provides no standard at all and leaves opening containers to the officer's choice, which is the discretion Bertine forbids. Prying open a locked case looks investigative. Why not the others: A is wrong because container searches are allowed only under standardized policies. B imposes a probable-cause requirement inventory searches do not have. D relies on subjective explanation; the test is the policy's objective constraint on discretion. Policy: Standardized procedures are what make suspicionless inventories legitimate. Without them, impoundment becomes a tool for exploratory searches of anyone police choose to tow.

## ccp-06-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Closely regulated businesses vs. short-term home rentals
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 10
Prompt: An Ironbridge Falls ordinance requires owners who rent out their homes by the night to register, and it authorizes police officers to inspect any registered home "at any time, for any reason related to compliance," without a warrant. Officers enter Marisol Thane's home under the ordinance while she is staying there between rentals and find unlicensed firearms. Is the warrantless inspection valid?
A: Probably not. Short-term home rentals are not a long-established, pervasively regulated industry. The ordinance places no limits on time, scope, or frequency, and it reaches the inside of a home. When consent is refused, administrative inspections generally require an administrative warrant.
B: Yes, because any business that must be licensed is a closely regulated industry.
C: Yes, because administrative searches never require a warrant.
D: No, because administrative inspections require individualized probable cause that a specific law has been violated.
Answer: A
Authorities: Camara v. Municipal Court; New York v. Burger
Explanation: Rule: Administrative inspections generally require a warrant if consent is refused, though administrative probable cause (reasonable legislative or administrative standards) is enough, without individualized suspicion (Camara). Warrantless inspections are permitted only in closely regulated industries, and only if the scheme serves a substantial government interest, inspections are necessary to further it, and the statute provides a constitutionally adequate substitute for a warrant by limiting time, place, and scope (Burger). Application: Home-sharing is newly regulated, not historically pervasive like junkyards or liquor sales. "At any time, for any reason" provides no real limits, and the target is a home where the owner lives. Why not the others: B equates licensing with pervasive regulation. C contradicts Camara. D overstates the standard; administrative warrants need not rest on individualized suspicion. Policy: The closely regulated industry exception rests on reduced expectations in businesses that have long accepted inspection. Extending it to homes through new licensing rules would let cities sidestep Camara whenever they impose a registration requirement.

## ccp-06-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Co-occupant objection after the objector leaves voluntarily
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 11
Prompt: Officers investigating a stolen-goods ring come to the home of Leopold and Adair Fenwick. Leopold stands in the doorway and says, "You are not searching my house." The officers leave. The next morning, after Leopold has gone to work, they return, and Adair, who shares the home equally, consents. They find stolen electronics in the shared living room. Is the search valid?
A: No, because Leopold's objection binds the household until he withdraws it.
B: No, because the officers knew Leopold objected and deliberately came back when he was gone.
C: Valid only if Adair holds title to the home.
D: Yes. A co-occupant's objection overrides another occupant's consent only when the objector is physically present and expressly objecting. Leopold left voluntarily and was not removed to prevent an objection, so Adair's consent controls.
Answer: D
Authorities: Georgia v. Randolph; Fernandez v. California; United States v. Matlock
Explanation: Rule: A physically present co-occupant's express refusal overrides another occupant's consent (Randolph). The objection does not survive once the objector is absent, whether from lawful removal (Fernandez) or ordinary departure. Police may not remove someone for the purpose of preventing an objection. A co-occupant with common authority may consent to the search of shared areas (Matlock). Application: Leopold went to work on his own. Adair has common authority over the shared living room. Fernandez rejected the argument that an earlier objection continues to bind after the objector leaves. Why not the others: A contradicts Fernandez. B describes timing that Fernandez treated as acceptable when the absence was not engineered by police. C confuses common authority, which turns on use and access, with property title. Policy: Randolph rests on the social expectation that a visitor would not enter over a present resident's objection. Limiting it to present objectors gives a clear rule and respects the consenting co-occupant's own authority, which matters especially when the consenting resident may be a domestic-violence victim.

## ccp-06-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Imminent destruction of evidence
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 12
Prompt: Officers have probable cause that Delphine Rusk sells fentanyl from her apartment, and they go there to arrest her on the street when she comes out. While they wait in the hallway, they hear a voice inside shout, "Cops in the hall, dump it all!" followed by repeated toilet flushing. They force entry, secure the bathroom, and seize fentanyl from the toilet tank. After Rusk is handcuffed, they spend an hour searching her bedroom closets and find cash. Which ruling is correct?
A: All evidence must be suppressed, because officers may never enter a home without a warrant.
B: The entry and the seizure from the bathroom were justified by an objectively reasonable belief that evidence was being destroyed right then, with probable cause in place. The hour-long closet search went beyond that exigency.
C: The entry was unlawful because the officers never saw the drugs before going in.
D: The entry was lawful, and once inside the officers could search the whole apartment for any evidence.
Answer: B
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Warden v. Hayden; Payton v. New York
Explanation: Rule: A warrantless home entry is permitted when exigent circumstances, including imminent destruction of evidence, make getting a warrant impracticable. Probable cause is still required, the scope is limited to the exigency, and the search must end when the exigency does. Reasonableness is judged objectively (Fisher). Application: The officers already had probable cause. The shout and the flushing made destruction imminent, so entering and securing the bathroom fit the exigency. Once the evidence was secured and Rusk was in cuffs, the exigency was over. An hour in the closets needed a warrant. Why not the others: A ignores the exigency exception to Payton. C invents a sight requirement. D ignores scope limits. Policy: The destruction-of-evidence exigency keeps suspects from defeating the warrant process by flushing evidence while police wait. Strict scope limits stop an emergency from becoming a general search.

## ccp-06-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Protective search of a car's passenger compartment during a stop
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 13
Prompt: At 11 p.m., an officer stops Thorne Galloway for speeding on a dark road. As the officer walks up, Galloway leans far down and reaches under the driver's seat. Galloway is ordered out and stands by the trunk, not handcuffed, and will be allowed back into the car once he gets his ticket. The officer checks under the driver's seat and in the unlocked console, finding a loaded pistol, and then opens the locked trunk and finds stolen jewelry. Which evidence is admissible?
A: Neither, because a car may never be searched without probable cause.
B: The pistol only. With reasonable belief that the driver is dangerous and could get immediate access to a weapon, an officer may conduct a protective search of the passenger compartment, limited to places where a weapon could be placed or hidden. A locked trunk is not such a place.
C: Both, because a protective search may cover the entire vehicle, including the trunk.
D: Neither, because a protective search of a car is allowed only after an arrest.
Answer: B
Authorities: Michigan v. Long; Terry v. Ohio
Explanation: Rule: Long extended Terry to cars. If an officer reasonably believes, based on specific and articulable facts, that the suspect is dangerous and may gain immediate control of weapons, the officer may search the passenger compartment, limited to areas where a weapon may be placed or hidden. Application: A furtive reach under the seat at night supports a reasonable belief of danger, and Galloway would be returning to the car. Under the seat and the unlocked console are within the protective scope. A locked trunk is not accessible to him and holds no immediate weapon threat, so searching it required probable cause. Why not the others: A ignores Long. C exceeds Long's scope. D confuses a protective search with search incident to arrest; Long applies precisely because the suspect will return to the car. Policy: Long recognizes that roadside encounters are especially dangerous to officers. Limiting the search to where a weapon can be reached keeps a safety measure from becoming an evidence search.

## ccp-06-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Stop-and-identify statutes demanding documents
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 14
Prompt: An Ironbridge Falls ordinance makes it a crime for a person lawfully stopped on reasonable suspicion to refuse to "produce a government-issued photo identification card." During a valid stop about a reported car break-in, Wilder Strand tells the officer his name truthfully but says he has no ID with him. He is arrested under the ordinance. Which statement best describes the law?
A: The arrest is valid, because a stopped person must answer every question an officer asks.
B: The arrest is invalid, because a person can never be required to identify himself during a stop.
C: Requiring a stopped person to disclose his name during a valid stop has been upheld. Requiring production of a physical document goes beyond that holding, and Strand has a strong argument that punishing him for not carrying a card when he gave his true name exceeds what the stop-and-identify principle supports.
D: The arrest is valid even if the stop lacked reasonable suspicion, because identification requirements apply to every police encounter.
Answer: C
Authorities: Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court; Terry v. Ohio
Explanation: Rule: Hiibel upheld a state law requiring a person detained on reasonable suspicion to disclose his name. The Court reasoned that the request was tied to the purpose of the stop, did not change its duration or location, and that the Fifth Amendment did not apply absent a real danger of incrimination. The opinion emphasized that the statute required stating a name, not producing documents. Application: Strand complied with what Hiibel endorsed. Making it a crime not to carry and produce a card goes further and is not resolved by Hiibel. Why not the others: A overstates Hiibel; it did not require answers to all questions. B contradicts Hiibel. D ignores that Hiibel depends on a lawful Terry stop. Policy: Knowing who a person is helps officers assess danger and check warrants. A document-production rule would effectively require everyone to carry ID in public at all times, which threatens anonymity and freedom of movement.

## ccp-06-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Due process voluntariness under the totality of the circumstances
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 15
Prompt: Ingram Shaw, 19, has an eighth-grade education. He is questioned in relays for 19 hours, kept awake, given no food, and told repeatedly that "it will go much worse for your mother" if he does not confess to an arson. He received warnings at the start and signed a waiver form. He confesses at hour 19, and the confession is later corroborated by physical evidence. Is the confession admissible?
A: No. Under the totality of the circumstances, the prolonged relay questioning, deprivation of sleep and food, threats aimed at his family, and his youth and limited education show coercive police conduct that overbore his will.
B: Yes, because he was warned and signed a waiver, so the confession is voluntary.
C: Yes, because the confession was corroborated, which shows it was voluntary.
D: Yes, because police used no physical force.
Answer: A
Authorities: Brown v. Mississippi; Miranda v. Arizona; Colorado v. Connelly
Explanation: Rule: Due process forbids using a confession that was involuntary because coercive police conduct overbore the suspect's will, judged by the totality of the circumstances, including the length and conditions of questioning, threats or promises, and the suspect's characteristics (Brown; Connelly requires police coercion). Miranda compliance does not guarantee voluntariness. Application: Nineteen hours of relay questioning, sleep and food deprivation, and threats against his mother are coercive police conduct, and Shaw's youth and limited education make him more vulnerable. Why not the others: B treats a waiver form as conclusive; coercion can make a confession involuntary even after warnings. C confuses truth with voluntariness; the test concerns how the confession was obtained. D overstates Brown; psychological coercion can make a confession involuntary without physical force. Policy: The voluntariness requirement reflects the accusatorial system: the state must prove guilt through independent evidence, not by breaking the accused. It also protects against false confessions and deters abusive tactics.

Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 18
Prompt: After warnings, Saoirse Kendrick says clearly, "I don't want to talk about the car theft." The detective leaves. Fifteen minutes later he returns and says, "Let me just read you your rights again," then resumes questioning about the same car theft. Kendrick confesses. Is the confession admissible?

B: No, because once a suspect invokes any Miranda right, police may never question her again about anything.

B: No, because counsel had to be present.
C: No, because showing a suspect in handcuffs automatically requires exclusion.
D: Likely yes. ## ccp-06-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Attenuation: preexisting warrants and systemic suspicionless stops
Set: CCP-06
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Ironbridge Falls
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 3, Question 20
Prompt: Ironbridge Falls' "Warrant Sweep" program directs officers to stop pedestrians in one neighborhood without suspicion and run their names for outstanding warrants. Under the program, an officer stops Corentin Marsh, finds an old warrant for an unpaid parking fine, arrests him, and finds a stolen handgun in a search incident to the arrest. Marsh moves to suppress. What is the best analysis?
A: The gun is admissible, because discovering a valid preexisting warrant always breaks the causal chain.
B: The valid warrant is an intervening circumstance, but the stop was part of a deliberate program of suspicionless stops designed to find warrants. That purposeful, systemic misconduct weighs heavily toward suppression, unlike an isolated, negligent mistake.
C: The gun must be suppressed, because no attenuation is possible when evidence is found within minutes of an illegal stop.
D: The gun is admissible, because Marsh would have received warnings after his arrest.
Answer: B
Authorities: Utah v. Strieff; Brown v. Illinois; Wong Sun v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under the attenuation doctrine, evidence derived from an illegality is admissible if the connection has become so attenuated that the taint dissipates. Brown's factors are temporal proximity, intervening circumstances, and especially the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct. Strieff found attenuation where an officer's unlawful stop was an isolated, good-faith mistake and a valid preexisting warrant intervened, noting that systemic or recurring police misconduct could change the analysis. Application: Close timing favors Marsh, the warrant favors the government, and flagrancy decides the case. A program of deliberate suspicionless stops to find warrants is exactly the systemic, purposeful misconduct Strieff distinguished. Why not the others: A treats the warrant as dispositive, which Strieff did not. C ignores that close timing can be outweighed. D repeats the argument Brown rejected; warnings alone do not attenuate a Fourth Amendment violation. Policy: The flagrancy factor focuses exclusion where it deters most. Admitting evidence from systematic suspicionless stops would reward a program that targets a neighborhood, a concern the Strieff dissents emphasized.
