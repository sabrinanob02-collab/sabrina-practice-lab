## tm-02-q01
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Suggestive versus descriptive marks
Set: TM-02
Coverage: Assigned Beebe printed pp. 1-67, 71-108, and 131-146
Theme: The Lantern District
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: A mobile application that organizes amateur astronomy outings uses STARGAZER. The service does not sell telescopes or literally gaze at stars; purchasers must connect the word to planning nights when they themselves will observe the sky. Competing scheduling applications can describe their functions without the term. The best classification is:
A: Generic.
B: Descriptive.
C: Suggestive.
D: Arbitrary.
Answer: C
Authorities: Abercrombie; Zatarains imagination and competitor-need tests
Explanation: STARGAZER requires a mental step from the person who observes stars to an application that organizes observation outings, while competitor need is low. Those facts favor suggestiveness. It does not name the service genus or immediately describe a scheduling function, and its evocative relationship makes arbitrary treatment less apt.

Title: Question 2
Prompt: An avocado cooperative uses COSTA VERDE for fruit grown and packed in the recognized Costa Verde agricultural region. Relevant English- and Spanish-speaking buyers understand the phrase as "Green Coast" and make an accurate goods-place association. The best initial treatment is:
A: Arbitrary because non-English geographic wording cannot describe goods.
B: Generic for every avocado.
C: Primarily geographically deceptively misdescriptive because all place names deceive.
D: Primarily geographically descriptive, absent acquired distinctiveness.
Answer: D
Authorities: Geographic marks and doctrine of foreign equivalents
Explanation: The term's primary significance is a place, the goods originate there, and purchasers are likely to make an accurate association. That supports geographic descriptiveness rather than deception. A ignores consumer understanding, B confuses place with genus, and C contradicts the accurate origin facts.

Title: Question 3
Prompt: A dishwasher maker uses QUIET-RUN. Competitors use similar wording for low-noise machines. The maker has strong sales after eight years, but its advertising discusses decibel performance without presenting QUIET-RUN as a source cue, and no consumer evidence links the phrase to one producer. The best conclusion is:
A: The designation is suggestive because purchasers cannot hear written words.
B: The designation is descriptive, and sales plus product-focused advertising alone do not establish acquired distinctiveness.
C: Every phrase describing a useful feature is automatically generic.
D: The designation is arbitrary because "quiet-run" is hyphenated.
Answer: B
Authorities: Zatarains; Innovation Ventures; secondary-meaning factors
Explanation: QUIET-RUN immediately describes low-noise operation, and competitor use supports descriptiveness. Sales and advertising volume are indirect when the advertising does not teach source significance. A mistakes literal embodiment for imagination, C collapses descriptive and generic matter, and D makes punctuation dispositive.

Title: Question 4
Prompt: Hikers and retailers use TRAIL BARS as the common name for compact nutrition bars marketed for hiking. Dictionaries and competing listings use the phrase that way. One seller pioneered the product, leads the market, and produces a survey showing that many buyers also associate TRAIL BARS with it. Which analysis is best?
A: Because each word can be generic, the combined phrase is automatically generic without considering the whole.
B: Strong association with the market leader necessarily converts the product name into a mark.
C: The phrase must be evaluated as a whole, but evidence that consumers primarily use it as the product name likely establishes genericness despite secondary association.
D: First use prevents generic treatment.
Answer: C
Authorities: Marvin Ginn; Booking.com; Kellogg; Snyder's-Lance
Explanation: The whole phrase and relevant public perception control. The evidence indicates that TRAIL BARS names the genus, while association with the dominant seller is secondary. A improperly stops at the components, and B and D confuse brand recognition or priority with non-genericness.

Title: Question 5
Prompt: A civic nonprofit prints BALTIMORE BUILDS TOGETHER across the broad face of tote bags. Community groups and local vendors widely use the same words as a civic message. The nonprofit uses a separate lighthouse logo on labels to identify itself, and its campaign materials call the phrase a rallying slogan. The strongest conclusion is:
A: One year of sales automatically makes the phrase a descriptive mark.
B: The phrase is fanciful because the nonprofit cannot identify an earlier local user.
C: All wording on tote bags is generic.
D: The phrase may fail to function as a mark because purchasers are likely to perceive a widely used message rather than a source identifier.
Answer: D
Authorities: Failure-to-function doctrine; assigned In re Lizzo excerpt
Explanation: Failure to function asks whether the matter actually identifies source in context. Prominent slogan placement, widespread message use, the organization's own campaign framing, and a separate source logo support nontrademark perception. A and B misuse duration and priority, while C incorrectly labels all ornamental wording generic.

## tm-02-q06

Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 6
Prompt: A new bookstore uses a distinctive combination of tilted entry arches, floor-to-ceiling map murals, copper reading pods, staff aprons, and a spiral checkout path. A rival copies the combination. The arrangement immediately signals the store, no element is essential to selling books, and many practical alternatives exist. The bookstore lacks secondary-meaning evidence. The best conclusion is:
A: No protection is possible because individual bookstores commonly use murals, aprons, and checkout paths.
B: The overall nonfunctional image may be inherently distinctive and therefore may receive protection without proof of secondary meaning.
C: Trade dress can be protected only by a design patent.
D: Any useful component makes the entire combination functional.
Answer: B
Authorities: Two Pesos
Explanation: Inherently distinctive, nonfunctional trade dress may be protected without secondary meaning, and the claimed total image is considered as a combination. A improperly dissects the combination, C ignores trademark protection, and D makes any useful component dispositive rather than examining the asserted whole.

Title: Question 7
Prompt: A tax-document service uses the same lavender color on all client folders. A survey of relevant accounting firms shows strong single-source association, and advertising repeatedly calls the folders "the lavender file." Other colors are equally visible, cost the same, and perform identically. The best conclusion is:
A: A single color can never serve as a mark.
B: The color is arbitrary and therefore needs no consumer-association evidence.
C: The color may identify source because it has acquired distinctiveness and the facts show no significant nontrademark function.
D: Any customer preference for lavender makes it functional.
Answer: C
Authorities: Qualitex
Explanation: A color can serve as a mark after acquiring distinctiveness if it is nonfunctional. The survey and feature-focused advertising support source meaning, while equal alternatives and performance negate utilitarian function. A states a categorical bar, B misuses word-mark categories, and D confuses preference with legal functionality.

## tm-02-q08
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Product configuration
Set: TM-02
Coverage: Assigned Beebe printed pp. 1-67, 71-108, and 131-146
Theme: The Lantern District
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 8
Prompt: A kettle maker claims rights in an "iconic silhouette" consisting of a flattened oval body, offset loop handle, and three vertical vents. Its advertising always places the registered house name across the body. Its survey shows the complete named kettle and asks respondents who made it. Which analysis is best?
A: Product configuration may be inherently distinctive, so no consumer-source evidence is needed.
B: The claimant must define the asserted design precisely and prove acquired distinctiveness in those features; the visible house name gives respondents an alternative source cue.
C: Product design can never receive trademark protection.
D: Any favorable survey percentage conclusively proves acquired distinctiveness regardless of what respondents saw.
Answer: B
Authorities: Assigned product-configuration preview; TBL Licensing claim-specific proof
Explanation: Product configuration requires acquired distinctiveness, a reasonably precise claim, and proof linking source perception to the claimed features. Showing a prominent word mark contaminates that inference. A erases the packaging-configuration distinction, C is too absolute, and D ignores survey design and claim specificity.
