## ccp-01-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Trespass theory of a search
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: Without a warrant or consent, investigators affix a small locating unit to the underside of a contractor's van and track its whereabouts on public streets for several weeks. The van's routes are visible to anyone on the street. Which analysis best explains why this conduct can implicate the Fourth Amendment even though the routes themselves were publicly visible?
A: Physically attaching the device to a constitutionally protected effect to gather information can itself supply a search, independent of any privacy-expectation analysis.
B: Any monitoring lasting more than a few days automatically becomes a search regardless of how information was gathered.
C: The privacy-expectation test fully replaced any property-based theory, so only societal expectations can matter now.
D: Because the van's movements occurred entirely on public roads, no constitutional interest is implicated under any theory.
Answer: A
Authorities: Trespass/property-based search theory
Explanation: A physical intrusion onto a constitutionally protected effect to gather information can independently establish a search, without needing to run the privacy-expectation analysis at all. That theory sits alongside the privacy test rather than being swallowed by it, so C overstates the privacy test's reach. B converts a durational rule of thumb into a categorical trigger, and D ignores that the trespass theory does not depend on whether the resulting information was itself public.

## ccp-01-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Curtilage
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 2
Prompt: A homeowner keeps a locked toolshed twelve feet from the back door, inside the same wooden fence that encloses the yard, and uses it to store gardening equipment for household use. Officers climb the fence and search the shed without a warrant. Which combination of facts most strongly supports treating the shed as curtilage?
A: The shed's distance from any public road, regardless of its relationship to the house.
B: Its proximity to the home, its placement within the yard's enclosure, its purely domestic use, and the fence itself as a step to shield it from passersby.
C: The shed being visible from a neighboring window.
D: The homeowner's subjective belief that the shed was private, without more.
Answer: B
Authorities: Curtilage four-factor analysis
Explanation: Proximity to the home, enclosure within the same boundary as the house, domestic rather than commercial use, and steps taken to shield the area from outside observation together support a curtilage finding; no single factor is dispositive, but B gathers the factors the analysis actually asks about. A isolates one irrelevant fact, C describes something that could just as easily cut against protection, and D supplies only the subjective half of a test that also requires an objectively reasonable basis.

## ccp-01-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Implied license onto curtilage
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 3
Prompt: An officer walks up a homeowner's front path to the porch and knocks, exactly as a delivery driver or trick-or-treater would. While waiting, the officer brings a trained narcotics dog onto the porch specifically to sniff at the base of the front door for contraband odor. Which statement best captures why this differs from an ordinary knock-and-talk?
A: Any officer presence on a porch is automatically permitted because porches are not curtilage.
B: Because the dog itself never entered the house, no constitutionally protected area was implicated.
C: The implied license lets a visitor approach, knock, and briefly wait to be received, but does not extend to using the porch to conduct an investigative sniff not customarily invited.
D: The license question is irrelevant once the officer is standing on a public sidewalk-adjacent path.
Answer: C
Authorities: Implied license and curtilage-based search
Explanation: Ordinary social custom licenses an approach, a knock, and a brief wait — it does not license bringing an investigative tool onto the porch to detect what is inside, so exceeding that customary purpose can itself supply a search on the curtilage. A treats the porch as unprotected outright, which ignores that curtilage protection is exactly what makes the license question matter, B mistakes the shed/house boundary for the relevant line when the porch itself is the protected area here, and D loses the license issue by focusing on the path rather than the officer's conduct once arrived.

## ccp-01-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 4
Prompt: A tenant speaks in a hushed voice inside a closed phone booth-style kiosk at a library, believing the enclosure keeps the conversation private. An officer outside places a sensitive listening device against the kiosk's exterior wall without penetrating it and records the call. The tenant later argues a search occurred. Which two elements must the tenant establish?
A: That the officer trespassed on the tenant's own property, and that the device was expensive.
B: That the tenant subjectively intended to keep the call private, and that any listener physically could have overheard it anyway.
C: That the kiosk was fully enclosed on all sides, and that no other patrons were present in the library.
D: That the tenant exhibited an actual, subjective expectation of privacy, and that the expectation is one society is prepared to recognize as reasonable.
Answer: D
Authorities: Reasonable-expectation-of-privacy two-prong test
Explanation: The privacy-expectation inquiry asks whether the person actually intended privacy and whether that expectation is one society accepts as reasonable; physical penetration of the enclosure is expressly unnecessary. A wrongly imports a trespass requirement into what is meant to be an alternative theory, B inverts the second prong, and C substitutes physical-enclosure facts for the objective-reasonableness inquiry the test actually asks about.

## ccp-01-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Third-party doctrine
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 5
Prompt: A municipal water utility keeps ordinary account records showing the volume each household uses monthly, generated because customers submit usage information to be billed. Investigators obtain a household's records from the utility without a warrant. Which principle most directly supports the government's position that no search occurred?
A: A person who voluntarily conveys information to a third party for the third party's ordinary business purposes generally assumes the risk that the information will be disclosed, and so lacks a reasonable expectation of privacy in it.
B: A person retains a reasonable expectation of privacy in any information a private company keeps about them.
C: Utility records are always protected because they reveal details about activity inside the home.
D: Because the household did not know the specific employee who would view the records, no meaningful disclosure occurred.
Answer: A
Authorities: Third-party doctrine
Explanation: Information knowingly and voluntarily handed to a business for its own recordkeeping is treated as conveyed at the risk of further disclosure, defeating a reasonable-expectation claim in the records themselves. B states the opposite of the doctrine, C assumes a conclusion the doctrine does not automatically reach for ordinary business records, and D confuses anonymity as to a specific viewer with the voluntariness question the doctrine actually turns on.

## ccp-01-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Overnight guests and mere presence
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 6
Prompt: Dana stops by an acquaintance's apartment for the first time solely to complete a quick cash transaction and leaves within minutes, having no other relationship with the tenant. Separately, Priya has stayed overnight at a close friend's apartment several times and is asleep on the couch when officers enter. Which conclusion about their respective claims is best supported?
A: Neither has any claim, because only the actual leaseholder can ever assert Fourth Amendment protection in a home.
B: Priya's overnight, socially recognized presence supports a reasonable expectation of privacy, while Dana's brief, transactional, first-time visit does not.
C: Both have equally strong claims, because presence inside a private residence is sufficient by itself.
D: Dana has the stronger claim, because her visit was shorter and therefore less likely to be observed.
Answer: B
Authorities: Overnight-guest doctrine
Explanation: Overnight guests are treated as sharing in the host's expectation of privacy because the arrangement reflects a recognized social custom, while a brief, purely transactional visitor with no prior relationship to the host is not. A overstates the rule by limiting protection to leaseholders alone, C erases the distinction the doctrine actually draws, and D treats visit length as cutting the wrong direction.

## ccp-01-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Tracking devices inside and outside dwellings
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 7
Prompt: With the consent of the original seller, investigators place a small locator inside a crate before it is delivered to a buyer. They use it only to confirm the crate's route along public highways to a warehouse. In a second, unrelated case, investigators place a similar locator inside a package without anyone's consent before delivery, then continue monitoring after the package is carried inside a private residence, confirming the package is still there. Which statement best distinguishes the two scenarios?
A: Both are searches, because any concealed locator inside an item is inherently a search regardless of what it reveals.
B: Neither is a search, because locators merely augment senses that could otherwise observe the same public movement.
C: The first is not a search because it reveals only movement along public routes with the original possessor's consent; the second is a search because it reveals a fact about the interior of a private residence that could not otherwise have been learned without a warrant.
D: The second is not a search because the residence was never physically entered by any officer.
Answer: C
Authorities: Beeper-monitoring distinction (public-route augmentation versus revealing facts about a dwelling's interior)
Explanation: Monitoring that merely confirms movement along public roads, with the original possessor's consent to the device's presence, does not exceed what visual surveillance could already reveal; monitoring that discloses a fact about the inside of a home is different in kind, because that fact could not have been obtained without a warrant. A treats the technology as automatically dispositive, B ignores that augmentation can still cross into the home, and D wrongly assumes that only physical entry by an officer, rather than confirmation of an item's presence inside, can create a search.

## ccp-01-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Sense-enhancing technology and aerial observation
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 8
Prompt: From a small aircraft flying at an altitude open to the public, an officer uses an ordinary handheld camera to photograph a fenced backyard garden. In a separate incident, an officer stationed on the public sidewalk aims a specialized imaging device, not sold to consumers or generally available, at the exterior wall of a house to detect heat patterns consistent with indoor equipment. Which conclusion is best supported?
A: Both are searches, because any device used for surveillance purposes triggers Fourth Amendment scrutiny.
B: Neither is a search, because both observations were made from a lawful public vantage point.
C: The heat-imaging use is not a search so long as the officer stayed on the public sidewalk the entire time.
D: The aerial photograph is not a search because any member of the flying public could have observed the same thing with an ordinary camera; the heat-imaging use is a search because it obtains information about the home's interior using technology not in general public use.
Answer: D
Authorities: Aerial-observation and sense-enhancing-technology principles
Explanation: Naked-eye or ordinary-camera observation from a lawful, publicly accessible vantage point does not offend privacy expectations society is prepared to honor, but using technology not available to the general public to learn what is happening inside a home is treated differently, because the home is where privacy expectations are strongest. A collapses a meaningful distinction, B ignores that lawful vantage point is necessary but not sufficient, and C wrongly assumes that staying off the property defeats a claim that turns on the technology's capability, not the officer's location.

Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)

Title: Question 9
Prompt: A resident places sealed trash bags at the curb for the scheduled municipal collection, where passersby, animals, and collectors can freely access them. Officers search the bags without a warrant before the truck arrives. Which fact matters most to whether this was a search?
A: Whether the bags were placed somewhere readily accessible to the public and handed over for the purpose of conveying them to a third party who could allow others to go through them.
B: Whether the resident subjectively hoped no one would look inside the bags.
C: Whether the bags were opaque rather than clear.
D: Whether the collection truck was already running late that day.
Answer: A
Authorities: Curbside-exposure principle
Explanation: Placing trash in a location readily accessible to the public and handing it off for collection defeats any reasonable expectation of privacy in its contents, regardless of the resident's private hope that no one would look. B isolates the subjective half of the inquiry without the objective component, C and D describe details that do not bear on the exposure analysis the doctrine actually applies.

## ccp-01-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Physical manipulation versus canine sniff
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 10
Prompt: On a crowded train, an officer walks a trained narcotics dog past a row of overhead bags, and the dog alerts near one bag without touching it. Separately, a different officer squeezes a passenger's soft duffel bag in an unusually probing way, feeling and identifying a hard, brick-shaped object inside. Which conclusion best fits these two encounters?
A: Both are searches, because any officer contact with a passenger's belongings is a search.
B: The dog sniff is not a search, because it discloses only the presence or absence of contraband and nothing else; the exploratory squeeze is a search, because it exceeds the handling passengers expect from other travelers or staff.
C: Neither is a search, because passengers assume the risk that any bag stored in a shared space might be handled.
D: The exploratory squeeze is not a search, so long as the bag is never opened.
Answer: B
Authorities: Canine-sniff and physical-manipulation principles
Explanation: A trained sniff is treated as revealing only a narrow, binary fact, so it does not intrude on any legitimate privacy interest, while manipulating a bag in an exploratory way exceeds what a passenger expects from ordinary incidental handling and can itself be a search even without opening the bag. A and C both erase a real distinction the doctrine draws, and D wrongly assumes that leaving a bag closed forecloses a manipulation-based search.

## ccp-01-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Search incident to arrest and digital data
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 1-3 (Aug. 17-24, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Question 11
Prompt: Following a lawful custodial arrest, officers seize a folded paper note from the arrestee's pocket and read it on the spot without a warrant. They also seize the arrestee's smartphone and, without a warrant, begin reading through years of stored messages, photos, and location history. Which statement best distinguishes the two searches?
A: Both are permitted without a warrant, because any item found on an arrestee's person may be searched incident to arrest.
B: Neither is permitted without a warrant, because search-incident-to-arrest never extends to reading content.
C: The note may be searched incident to the arrest, but the volume and sensitivity of data typically stored on a phone means that searching it usually requires its own warrant despite the lawful arrest.
D: The phone search is permitted because the arrestee's diminished expectation of privacy while in custody eliminates any distinction between physical and digital items.
Answer: C
Authorities: Search-incident-to-arrest limits on digital devices
Explanation: The search-incident-to-arrest doctrine reaches items on the arrestee's person, including a note, but the exceptional breadth and sensitivity of data typically stored on a phone means that doctrine's traditional justifications do not automatically extend to a full digital search, so a warrant is ordinarily still required for the phone. A treats a phone the same as any physical item despite that difference, B goes too far by denying the doctrine any application to content at all, and D overstates how far custody alone diminishes privacy interests in stored digital data.

## ccp-01-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Knock-and-announce during warrant execution
Set: CCP-01
Coverage: Classes 4-5 (Aug. 26-31, 2026)
Theme: Ashgrove Township
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 12
Prompt: Before executing a validly issued search warrant at a residence, officers want to forgo knocking and announcing entirely, relying only on the general premise that warrants for this category of offense often involve quickly disposable evidence. Which statement best reflects the governing standard?
A: A categorical exception for this offense category is permitted, because a magistrate need not evaluate individual case facts once probable cause for the underlying warrant is established.
B: Knocking and announcing may never be excused for any offense category, regardless of the facts known to officers.
C: Because the executing officers subjectively believed evidence disposal was likely, no further justification is required.
D: Officers must instead point to case-specific reasonable suspicion that knocking and announcing would be dangerous, futile, or would allow destruction of evidence in this particular case; a blanket category-wide exception is not permitted.
Answer: D
Authorities: Case-by-case standard for excusing knock-and-announce
Explanation: The knock-and-announce requirement can yield only on a case-specific showing of reasonable suspicion tied to danger, futility, or evidence destruction in that particular execution, not a categorical rule tied to an entire offense type. A and B both convert a case-specific inquiry into an absolute rule in opposite directions, and C substitutes an unarticulated subjective belief for the reasonable-suspicion showing the standard actually requires.
