## ccp-12-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Physical manipulation of luggage on a train
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 1
Prompt: On a Copperline commuter train, an agent walks down the aisle squeezing and kneading soft duffel bags in the overhead racks to feel for brick-shaped objects. He firmly kneads Aurelio Penn's bag several times, feels a hard brick, and later finds cocaine inside after Penn consents to a search. Was the kneading a search?
A: No, because people who put bags in public overhead racks expect others to touch them.
B: No, because the agent never opened the bag.
C: Yes. Passengers expect their bags to be handled in ordinary ways, but not to be felt in an exploratory manner by someone trying to discover what is inside. That kind of physical manipulation violates a reasonable expectation of privacy.
D: No, because a train is a public place, so nothing on it is protected.
Answer: C
Authorities: Bond v. United States; Katz v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Bond held that a border patrol agent's physical manipulation of a bus passenger's soft-sided bag in the overhead rack was a search. A passenger expects that others may handle the bag, but not that they will feel it in an exploratory manner to discover its contents. Application: The agent's firm, repeated kneading for bricks is exactly the exploratory manipulation Bond condemned. Whether the later consent is fruit of the search is a separate question. Why not the others: A states the argument Bond rejected; ordinary handling differs from exploratory squeezing. B is wrong because a search need not involve opening anything. D contradicts Katz's principle that the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places. Policy: Bond recognizes that travelers keep private belongings in bags they cannot keep in their laps. Protecting against exploratory handling preserves that privacy without stopping incidental contact by other passengers.

## ccp-12-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Geofence warrants: breadth and officer discretion
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 2
Prompt: After a bank robbery, Copperline detectives get a warrant ordering a tech company to identify every account whose location history placed it within 150 meters of the bank during a one-hour window. The warrant then lets the detectives, without returning to the magistrate, pick which anonymized accounts to "unmask" and get more location data on. Among roughly 1,500 people, detectives choose to unmask Ines Dorrell. Which argument most strongly supports her challenge?
A: The warrant authorized sweeping in location data of many people without individualized probable cause as to any of them, and it left the critical choice of whom to unmask to the officers' discretion rather than to a neutral magistrate. That is the particularity and discretion problem the warrant clause was designed to prevent.
B: Location data can never be obtained by warrant.
C: The company is a private actor, so the Fourth Amendment does not apply.
D: Dorrell lacks any expectation of privacy in location history, so the only question is whether the detectives acted reasonably.
Answer: A
Authorities: Chatrie v. United States; Carpenter v. United States; Particularity requirement; Katz v. United States
Explanation: Rule: The warrant clause requires probable cause and particularity to prevent general searches and to place a neutral magistrate between police and citizens. Carpenter recognized that location data can reveal the privacies of life. Chatrie, the assigned excerpt, explores how these principles apply to geofence warrants. Application: The strongest challenge targets the warrant's structure: a dragnet with no individualized probable cause, plus officer discretion over who gets unmasked, which leaves the magistrate out of the most important step. The government will respond that the geographic and time limits supply particularity and that location history is shared with a third party. Why not the others: B overstates the law; warrants can authorize obtaining location data. C misunderstands the issue; the warrant compelled the company to act for the government. D assumes away the threshold question, which is contested after Carpenter. Policy: Geofence warrants can solve crimes with no other leads, but they turn every bystander's phone into a potential witness against them. Courts have looked for narrow geographic and time limits and judicial control at each step.

## ccp-12-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Implied license and the back door
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 3
Prompt: Officers want to "knock and talk" with Sorrel Banning. Her front door is visible from the street and plainly in use, with a doormat, bell, and lit porch. Instead, officers open the latched gate to her fenced backyard, walk past her patio furniture to the back door, and knock. While there, they see marijuana plants on the patio. Did they conduct a search?
A: No, because officers may knock at any door of a house.
B: Likely yes. The implied license lets visitors approach the home by the front path that ordinary visitors use, knock, and leave. Opening a latched gate and crossing the fenced backyard to a back door, when the front entrance was obviously the one for visitors, went beyond the license and intruded on the curtilage.
C: No, because the plants were in plain view once the officers were at the back door.
D: No, because the patio was an open field.
Answer: B
Authorities: Florida v. Jardines; United States v. Jones; United States v. Dunn; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Jardines recognizes an implied license, the same one any visitor has, to approach the front door, knock, wait briefly, and leave. Physically entering the curtilage beyond that license to gather information is a search (Jones). Plain view requires that officers be lawfully at their vantage point (Horton). Application: The front door was the obvious public entrance. Opening a latched gate and crossing a fenced patio to the back door goes beyond what the license covers. Courts sometimes allow approaching a back door if it is the one visitors normally use, but not on these facts. Why not the others: A ignores the license's limits. C assumes the officers were lawfully at the back door, which is the question. D misapplies open fields to a fenced patio next to the house. Policy: The implied license matches social norms: visitors come to the front door, not through a latched gate into the backyard. Tying police authority to those norms protects the home while preserving ordinary investigative contact.

## ccp-12-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: An immaterial falsehood in an affidavit
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 4
Prompt: Detective Wren Halley's affidavit to search Orsolya Teague's storage unit for stolen instruments includes: (1) video showing Teague unloading instruments matching the stolen ones into the unit; (2) a pawn-shop receipt in Teague's name for one stolen violin; and (3) a false statement that a neighbor saw Teague with a stolen cello, which Halley made up. Teague proves the fabrication. What happens?
A: The warrant is void, because any deliberate falsehood voids a warrant regardless of the rest.
B: The detective's fabrication requires automatic suppression as a sanction.
C: The fabrication is irrelevant, because only the informant's statements matter.
D: The court sets the fabricated statement aside and asks whether the rest of the affidavit establishes probable cause. Because the video and the receipt alone show a fair probability that stolen instruments are in the unit, the warrant stands.
Answer: D
Authorities: Franks v. Delaware; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: Under Franks, when a defendant proves a deliberate or reckless falsehood, the court sets it aside. If the remaining content still establishes probable cause, the warrant stands and suppression is not required. Probable cause is a fair probability under the totality of the circumstances (Gates). Application: Without the fabricated neighbor statement, the video and receipt still strongly link Teague and the unit to stolen instruments. Why not the others: A and B ignore Franks's materiality requirement. C misstates Franks, which addresses the affiant's falsehoods. Policy: Franks's materiality requirement avoids suppressing evidence when the lie did not affect the probable-cause finding. Critics argue that a detective who fabricates evidence should face consequences regardless, which may come through discipline or prosecution rather than exclusion.

## ccp-12-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Scope of a search limited by the object sought
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 5
Prompt: A warrant authorizes searching Cassius Morne's house for "a stolen 65-inch television and its large shipping box." Officers find the TV and box in the living room within five minutes. They then open Morne's bedside drawer, a small jewelry box, and an envelope in his desk, finding drugs in the envelope. Are the drugs admissible?
A: No. A warrant's scope is limited by the object of the search: officers may look only where the described item could be. A 65-inch TV cannot fit in a drawer, jewelry box, or envelope, and once the TV was found the warrant's purpose was fulfilled.
B: Yes, because a valid warrant authorizes searching the entire house.
C: Yes, because officers may search anywhere on the chance there is a second stolen TV.
D: Yes, because the drugs were in plain view.
Answer: A
Authorities: Particularity requirement; United States v. Ross; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: The scope of a warrant search is defined by the object of the search and the places where it may be found. A warrant for a large item does not authorize searching small containers (the principle Ross applied to vehicles applies to warrants generally). Plain view requires that officers be lawfully where they see the item. Application: A 65-inch TV cannot be inside a drawer, jewelry box, or envelope. Once the item was found, the search authority was exhausted. Opening those small containers exceeded the warrant, and the drugs were found in the course of that unlawful search. Why not the others: B ignores scope limits. C invents a speculative second object not in the warrant. D fails because the officers were not lawfully inside the envelope. Policy: Linking scope to the object keeps warrants from becoming general licenses and gives officers a concrete, easy-to-apply limit.

## ccp-12-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Probable cause at a crowded party
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 6
Prompt: Responding to a noise complaint, officers enter a house party with the host's consent and see a bag of cocaine on a coffee table in a living room where about 25 guests are mingling. No one claims the bag, and no one is seen using or handling it. Officers arrest all 25 guests for possession. Did they have probable cause to arrest every guest?
A: Yes, because drugs within reach of everyone in a room establish probable cause to arrest all of them.
B: Yes, because refusing to claim the drugs is evidence of joint possession.
C: No. Probable cause must be particularized to each person. The inference of a common enterprise that can justify arresting all occupants of a small car does not carry over to a large, open party with dozens of guests, where mere presence in the room does not show that any particular guest possessed the drugs.
D: No, because officers can never arrest anyone when drugs are found in a shared space.
Answer: C
Authorities: Maryland v. Pringle; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause requires a fair probability that the particular person committed a crime, judged by the totality of the circumstances (Gates). Pringle allowed arresting all three occupants of a small car where cocaine was accessible to all, reasoning that a dealer would be unlikely to admit an innocent person to such a small space with evidence of the crime. Application: A party with 25 guests is the opposite of Pringle's small, close setting. Many guests would not know the drugs were there or have anything to do with them. Without more, such as handling the drugs, ownership indications, or the host's role, presence is not enough. Why not the others: A extends Pringle mechanically. B treats silence as evidence. D overstates; Pringle shows shared-space arrests can be justified on the right facts. Policy: Particularized probable cause prevents mass arrests based on proximity. Pringle's narrow inference meets real drug-enforcement needs without making everyone in a room responsible for whatever is on the table.

## ccp-12-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: No inadvertence requirement for plain view
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 7
Prompt: Detective Faro Lund suspects that Marek Osborne both stole jewelry and used a distinctive brass-knuckle weapon in the robbery. His warrant lists only the jewelry. Executing it, he searches the bedroom closet, a proper place to look for jewelry, and finds the brass knuckles, as he had hoped he would. Is the seizure of the brass knuckles valid?
A: No, because the officer expected to find the weapon, so it was not discovered inadvertently.
B: No, because officers may seize only items named in the warrant.
C: No, because the officer should have listed the weapon in the warrant.
D: Yes. Plain view does not require that the discovery be inadvertent. Lund was lawfully searching the closet for jewelry, the knuckles' incriminating character was immediately apparent, and he had lawful access to them.
Answer: D
Authorities: Horton v. California; Coolidge v. New Hampshire
Explanation: Rule: Horton held that inadvertence is not a necessary condition of plain view, rejecting the Coolidge plurality's requirement. Plain view requires only that the officer be lawfully in the place of observation, that the incriminating character be immediately apparent, and that the officer have lawful access to the object. Horton relied on the objective scope of the warrant to protect privacy, rather than probing an officer's expectations. Application: The facts track Horton: the officer suspected both kinds of items, listed only one, and found the other while lawfully searching. Why not the others: A and C restate the inadvertence rule Horton rejected. B ignores plain view. Policy: Horton avoids subjective inquiries into officers' hopes and relies on the warrant's objective limits. Critics worried that it invites officers to omit items from applications, but Horton reasoned that the scope limit makes that strategy pointless.

## ccp-12-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Strip searches at jail intake for minor offenses
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 8
Prompt: Juniper Ash is arrested on a warrant for an unpaid traffic fine and taken to the Copperline county jail to be held overnight in the general population. Under a uniform policy applied to everyone entering the general population, she must undress, be visually inspected, and shower, without any individualized suspicion. She sues. Is the policy constitutional?
A: No, because strip searches always require reasonable suspicion that the person hides contraband.
B: Likely yes. Jails have a substantial interest in keeping contraband and disease out of the general population, and officials' judgment receives deference. A uniform visual-inspection policy for everyone entering the general population is reasonable even for minor offenses, without individualized suspicion.
C: No, because people arrested for minor offenses may never be searched at intake.
D: Yes, because people in custody have no Fourth Amendment rights at all.
Answer: B
Authorities: Florence v. Board of Chosen Freeholders; Illinois v. Lafayette; Maryland v. King
Explanation: Rule: Florence upheld jail policies requiring all arrestees committed to the general population to undergo close visual inspection while undressed, without individualized suspicion and regardless of the offense, because of the jail's serious security concerns and the difficulty of classifying inmates at intake. Booking procedures generally serve institutional needs (Lafayette; King). Application: Ash is entering the general population under a uniform policy. Florence controls, though the Court left open whether detainees held apart from the general population, or searches involving touching, raise different questions. Why not the others: A states the rule Florence rejected for general-population intake. C has no basis. D overstates the effect of custody. Policy: Florence prioritizes jail security and uniform rules. The dissent stressed the serious humiliation of strip searching people arrested for minor offenses, who pose little contraband risk.

## ccp-12-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Containers belonging to a passenger in a car search
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 9
Prompt: Officers have probable cause that stolen gemstones are somewhere in a car driven by Lorcan Fay. During the automobile-exception search, they open a purse on the back seat belonging to passenger Ivy Dunmore, whom they have no particular reason to suspect, and find the gemstones. Was opening her purse within the scope of the search?
A: No, because officers may never search a passenger's belongings without probable cause specific to the passenger.
B: No, because purses get the same protection as homes.
C: Likely yes. When officers have probable cause to search a car, the search may extend to every part of the vehicle and any container in it that could hold the object of the search. A purse on the back seat could hold gemstones, whoever owns it.
D: Yes, because passengers have no Fourth Amendment rights in their belongings.
Answer: C
Authorities: United States v. Ross; California v. Acevedo; Carroll v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under Ross, probable cause to search a vehicle justifies searching every part of it and its contents that may conceal the object of the search. Acevedo applies one rule to containers. The scope is defined by the object and where it may be found. Application: Gemstones are small and could be in a purse on the back seat. Under Ross's object-based scope rule, ownership by a passenger does not take the container outside the search. Courts have generally followed this approach. Why not the others: A requires separate probable cause for each owner, which Ross's scope rule does not. B exaggerates purse privacy. D overstates the conclusion; Dunmore has an expectation of privacy in her purse, but it is overcome by probable cause to search the car. Policy: An object-based scope rule keeps people from defeating vehicle searches by claiming items belong to someone else. Critics worry about the intrusion on innocent passengers.

## ccp-12-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: School strip search for a vape pen
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 10
Prompt: A Copperline middle-school assistant principal receives a reliable report that 13-year-old Nell Garrow has a nicotine vape pen. She searches Nell's backpack and pockets, finds nothing, and then has a nurse direct Nell to pull her bra and underwear away from her body. Nothing is found. Is the second search reasonable?
A: Yes, because the report was reliable, so school officials may search wherever the item might be.
B: No. A school search must be reasonable in scope given the student's age and the nature of the infraction. A search reaching intimate areas needs a reason to suspect both danger and concealment there. A nicotine vape, with no indication it was hidden in her underwear, does not justify that level of intrusion.
C: Yes, because school officials have the same authority as parents.
D: No, because school officials may never search students for anything.
Answer: B
Authorities: Safford Unified School District v. Redding; New Jersey v. T.L.O.
Explanation: Rule: T.L.O. requires a school search to be justified at its inception and reasonable in scope, not excessively intrusive in light of the student's age and sex and the nature of the infraction. Safford held that a search exposing intimate areas requires suspicion of danger, or a reason to believe the items are hidden in underwear, that justifies the categorically greater intrusion. Ibuprofen did not suffice. Application: The tip justified searching the backpack and pockets. Moving to intimate areas for a nicotine device, with nothing suggesting danger or concealment there, mirrors Safford's problem. Why not the others: A ignores Safford's scope limit. C misstates T.L.O., which rejected in loco parentis as the basis for school searches. D ignores T.L.O. Policy: Safford recognizes the serious emotional harm strip searches cause adolescents. Requiring heightened justification protects students while leaving schools able to respond to genuine dangers.

## ccp-12-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Withdrawing consent during a search
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 11
Prompt: Gemma Strick voluntarily lets officers search her apartment for stolen bicycles. After ten minutes in the living room, as an officer heads to the bedroom, she says firmly, "Stop. I want you to leave now." The officers keep searching for another 20 minutes and find stolen bikes in the bedroom closet. Is the bedroom evidence admissible on a consent theory?
A: Yes, because once she consented, she could not take it back.
B: Yes, because the officers had not yet finished the search she agreed to.
C: Yes, because officers may finish any search once it has begun.
D: No. Consent can be limited or withdrawn. Once Strick clearly revoked it, the officers needed another justification to continue, and evidence found after the revocation cannot rest on her earlier consent.
Answer: D
Authorities: Schneckloth v. Bustamonte; United States v. Matlock
Explanation: Rule: A consent search is valid only within the scope of the consent and only while it lasts. Because consent is voluntary (Schneckloth), the person who gives it controls its extent and may revoke it, and searching beyond a clear withdrawal requires a separate justification. Application: Strick revoked her consent clearly before the bedroom search. Nothing else, such as probable cause plus exigency, justified continuing. Why not the others: A, B, and C treat consent as irrevocable or as authorizing completion, which is inconsistent with consent being voluntary. Policy: Consent rests on the person's control over the intrusion. Allowing withdrawal preserves that control, and it encourages cooperation by assuring people they can change their minds.

## ccp-12-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: No exigency when a warrant can be obtained quickly
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 12
Prompt: Officers have probable cause that stolen guns are in Felix Ormond's garage. Ormond is in custody, the house is empty, and four officers surround it. Copperline's electronic warrant system usually produces a signed warrant in about 20 minutes. Rather than wait, officers enter the garage without a warrant, saying they "didn't want to waste time." Is the entry justified by exigency?
A: No. Exigency requires an emergency that makes getting a warrant impracticable. With Ormond in custody, the house secured, no one inside to destroy evidence, and a warrant available in minutes, there was no exigency, only convenience.
B: Yes, because probable cause plus the inconvenience of a wait is enough.
C: Yes, because guns are dangerous, so a warrant is never required for them.
D: Yes, because officers may enter whenever the suspect is in custody.
Answer: A
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Payton v. New York; Warden v. Hayden
Explanation: Rule: Exigent circumstances exist only when emergency conditions make getting a warrant impracticable. Probable cause alone does not justify entering a home (Payton), and the exception exists for genuine emergencies (Fisher; Hayden). Application: Every reason for an exigency is absent: no one inside to destroy evidence or pose a danger, the perimeter secured, and a quick warrant process available. The officers' explanation, avoiding waste of time, is the opposite of impracticability. Why not the others: B treats inconvenience as an exigency. C invents a category exception. D reverses the logic; the suspect's custody removed any urgency. Policy: Fast electronic warrants reduce the need for exigency exceptions. Requiring officers to use them preserves the neutral magistrate's role at little cost.

## ccp-12-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Walking away from a consensual encounter is not reasonable suspicion
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 13
Prompt: On a quiet residential street at 4 p.m., an officer who has a "feeling" about Lyle Durrant approaches and says, "Hey, can I talk to you?" Durrant says, "No thanks," and keeps walking at a normal pace. The officer then orders him to stop, detains him, and pats him down, finding a stolen phone. Was the detention lawful?
A: Yes, because refusing to talk to police gives reasonable suspicion.
B: No. A stop requires specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity. A hunch, plus a person's lawful decision to decline a consensual encounter and keep walking at a normal pace, is not reasonable suspicion.
C: Yes, because officers may always briefly detain people they are curious about.
D: No, but the phone is admissible because the frisk was for officer safety.
Answer: B
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; United States v. Mendenhall; Florida v. Royer
Explanation: Rule: A Terry stop requires reasonable suspicion based on specific and articulable facts, not an inchoate hunch (Terry). In a consensual encounter, a person may decline to listen and go on his way, and that refusal does not by itself supply the minimal suspicion needed for a detention (Royer; Mendenhall). Application: The officer had only a feeling. Durrant exercised his right to decline and walked away normally. Nothing else suggested a crime. Why not the others: A penalizes exercising a right. C eliminates the reasonable-suspicion requirement. D gets the frisk wrong; a frisk depends on a lawful stop and a reasonable belief that the person is armed, neither of which existed. Policy: If declining a police encounter could justify a stop, consensual encounters would become disguised detentions and people would lose the freedom to go about their business.

## ccp-12-q14

Prompt: Copperline has a law requiring people detained on reasonable suspicion to state their names. An officer stops Rosalind Kee on the sidewalk without any suspicion, just to "see who's out tonight," and asks her name. She refuses and is arrested under the law. Is the arrest valid?
A: Yes, because the identification law applies to everyone officers approach.
B: Yes, because giving one's name can never be incriminating.
C: Yes, because the privilege against self-incrimination never applies to identity.

## ccp-12-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Juvenile confession, false promises, and voluntariness
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 15
Prompt: Fifteen-year-old Tobin Ashe, who has an intellectual disability and reads at a third-grade level, is questioned alone for six hours about a fire. His parents' requests to see him are refused. Detectives repeatedly promise, falsely, that if he says he started the fire "by accident" he "can go home tonight and nothing will happen." He finally says he started it. Is the confession voluntary under due process?
A: Likely not. Under the totality of the circumstances, his youth, intellectual disability, the long questioning without a parent, the refusal to let his parents see him, and the repeated false promise of release show coercive police conduct that overbore his will.
B: Yes, because police may make any promise to get a confession.
C: Yes, because the confession was accurate.
D: Yes, because a juvenile has the same capacity as an adult to resist police pressure.
Answer: A
Authorities: Brown v. Mississippi; Colorado v. Connelly; J.D.B. v. North Carolina
Explanation: Rule: Due process forbids confessions obtained by coercive police conduct that overbears the suspect's will under the totality of the circumstances (Brown; Connelly). The suspect's characteristics, including youth and intellectual capacity, are central to that inquiry. J.D.B. recognized that children are more vulnerable to pressure. Application: The police conduct is coercive: six hours, isolation from parents, and false promises of release. Tobin is especially vulnerable. Together, these show his will was overborne. Why not the others: B ignores that promises can make a confession involuntary. C confuses accuracy with voluntariness. D contradicts J.D.B.'s recognition of children's vulnerability. Policy: Protecting vulnerable suspects from coercion guards against false confessions, which are especially common among juveniles and people with intellectual disabilities, and preserves public confidence in confession evidence.

## ccp-12-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Custody during an in-home warrant search
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 16
Prompt: At 6 a.m., eight armed officers execute a search warrant at Philippa Rowe's house. They gather the family in the living room, and an officer stays by Rowe at all times, even escorting her to the bathroom. A detective takes her to a back bedroom, closes the door, tells her she is "not under arrest," and questions her for 75 minutes about fraud without warnings. Was she in custody?
A: No, because questioning in one's own home is never custodial.
B: No, because she was told she was not under arrest.
C: Likely yes. Although home questioning is usually less coercive, the overwhelming police presence, constant monitoring even to the bathroom, isolation in a closed room, and long accusatory questioning would lead a reasonable person to believe her freedom was restrained to the degree of formal arrest, despite the "not under arrest" statement.
D: Yes, because any questioning during a search warrant execution is custodial.
Answer: C
Authorities: Miranda v. Arizona; Stansbury v. California; Howes v. Fields; Michigan v. Summers
Explanation: Rule: Custody is judged objectively by whether a reasonable person would have felt free to end the questioning and leave, considering all the circumstances, including the location, number of officers, restraints, duration, and statements made (Stansbury; Howes). Telling someone she is not under arrest is relevant but not decisive. Summers authorizes detention during a search, which can contribute to custody for Miranda purposes. Application: Home questioning is often non-custodial, but here the house was dominated by eight armed officers, Rowe was watched constantly, isolated, and questioned at length. A reasonable person would not feel free to leave. Why not the others: A and D state categorical rules the cases reject. B treats one statement as decisive. Policy: The custody inquiry looks at the real pressures on the suspect. A police-dominated home can be as coercive as a station house, and labels should not decide whether warnings are required.

## ccp-12-q17

Topic: Undercover questioning after an invocation of counsel
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 17
Prompt: Arrested for a burglary but not yet charged, Gideon Vane asks for a lawyer, and police interrogation stops. The next day, police put an undercover officer posing as an inmate in his holding cell, who asks Vane about the burglary. Vane, unaware he is talking to police, brags about it. Which statement best fits the assigned cases?
A: The statements are barred by the Sixth Amendment, because Vane had already asked for a lawyer.
B: The statements are barred by Miranda, because Vane was in custody and was being questioned.
C: The statements must be excluded, because Vane's request for counsel prohibits any government contact.
D: Under the reasoning that Miranda addresses the coercive pressure of interrogation by known police, an undercover conversation the suspect does not know is with police does not violate Miranda, and many courts apply that reasoning even after an invocation. Answer: D
Authorities: Illinois v. Perkins; Edwards v. Arizona; Massiah v. United States; Kirby v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Perkins held that Miranda warnings are not required when the suspect does not know he is speaking with police, because the coercive atmosphere Miranda addresses is missing. Edwards bars police-initiated interrogation after a request for counsel. The Sixth Amendment attaches only at formal charging (Kirby; Massiah). Application: Whether Perkins applies after an Edwards invocation is not directly resolved by the Court, but the logic carries over: Vane felt no police pressure. Uncharged, he had no Sixth Amendment right yet. Why not the others: A misplaces attachment. B ignores Perkins. C overstates Edwards, which concerns police interrogation the suspect knows about, not all government contact. Policy: Perkins reflects Miranda's focus on coercion, not deception. ## ccp-12-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Waiver by an intoxicated suspect
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 18
Prompt: Ronan Kade is arrested at a bar after a fight. He has been drinking, but he answers the booking officer's questions clearly, correctly states the date and his address, and, when warned, says, "Yeah, I know my rights, I watch TV. Ask away." Police do nothing to exploit his condition. He confesses. He later argues his intoxication made the waiver invalid. What result?
A: The waiver is invalid, because any intoxication makes a waiver involuntary.
B: The waiver is invalid, because police must wait until a suspect is fully sober before questioning.
C: The waiver is likely valid. A waiver must be voluntary, meaning free of police coercion, and knowing and intelligent, meaning made with understanding of the rights. Absent police exploitation, intoxication matters only if it prevented him from understanding, and his coherent answers and statement that he understood show he did understand.
D: The waiver is valid, because intoxication is never relevant.
Answer: C
Authorities: Moran v. Burbine; Colorado v. Connelly; Berghuis v. Thompkins
Explanation: Rule: A Miranda waiver must be voluntary (the product of free choice, not police intimidation or deception) and knowing and intelligent (made with awareness of the rights and the consequences of giving them up) (Moran). Connelly held that voluntariness, for both waivers and confessions, requires coercive police activity. Berghuis confirms waiver may be shown by conduct after understanding. Application: No police exploitation, so voluntariness is satisfied. His coherent answers and acknowledgment show he understood. Severe intoxication can defeat the knowing-and-intelligent requirement, but not on these facts. Why not the others: A and B impose per se rules. D goes too far, since intoxication can bear on understanding. Policy: Focusing on comprehension and police conduct avoids excluding confessions from people who understood their rights, while still protecting those too impaired to make a real choice.

Title: Exam 9, Question 19
Prompt: Two hours after his warrantless arrest for a purse snatching, and before any charge, initial appearance, or other proceeding, Cyrus Lamb is placed in a lineup without a lawyer. ## ccp-12-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Inevitable discovery through a lawful decryption already underway
Set: CCP-12
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Copperline
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 9, Question 20
Prompt: Under a valid warrant, Copperline's forensic lab begins a brute-force decryption of Mira Hollis's seized laptop, a process the lab reliably completes in three to five days. On day two, an impatient detective unlawfully pressures Hollis's roommate into revealing the password, opens the laptop, and finds fraud records. The lab's process was still running and would have finished on day four. Are the records admissible?
A: No, because the records were found through an unlawful act, so they must be excluded.
B: Likely yes, under inevitable discovery, if the prosecution proves by a preponderance that the lawful decryption already underway under the warrant would have opened the laptop and revealed the same records.
C: Yes, under attenuation, because two days passed.
D: No, because inevitable discovery applies only to physical evidence such as bodies or weapons.
Answer: B
Authorities: Nix v. Williams; Segura v. United States; Murray v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Nix admits evidence the prosecution proves by a preponderance would ultimately have been discovered by lawful means. In Nix, an organized search party was already underway and would have found the body. The doctrine puts the government in the same, not a worse, position than it would have occupied without the violation. Application: A lawful process authorized by warrant was already running and reliably finishes within days. That parallels the active search party in Nix. Unlike a "we would have gotten a warrant" claim, this lawful path actually existed and was in progress. Why not the others: A ignores Nix. C misapplies attenuation, whose factors do not fit; the unlawful act directly produced the evidence. D invents a limitation; the doctrine applies to any evidence. Policy: Inevitable discovery avoids giving defendants a windfall when lawful processes would have produced the evidence anyway. Requiring an actual lawful path in progress keeps it from erasing the warrant requirement.
