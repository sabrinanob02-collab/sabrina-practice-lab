## ccp-13-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Tracker placed with the seller's consent, then monitored inside a home
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 1
Prompt: With the seller's permission, Osprey Landing detectives hide a tracking chip inside a drum of industrial chemicals before Kestrel Vance buys it. Vance does not know about the chip. Detectives monitor its signal as Vance drives home on public roads, and then for three weeks while the drum sits inside his house, using the data to confirm when he moves it between rooms. Which monitoring was a search?
A: Neither, because the chip was placed with the owner's consent before the sale.
B: Only the monitoring inside the house. Placing the chip with the then-owner's consent was not a search, and following it on public roads revealed only what was exposed to the public. But monitoring it inside a private home revealed facts about the inside of the home that could not have been observed from outside.
C: Both, because any tracking device in a person's property makes all monitoring a search.
D: Only the public-road monitoring, because tracking on roads lasted longer than a single trip.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Karo; United States v. Knotts; United States v. Jones
Explanation: Rule: Karo held that installing a beeper in a container with the then-owner's consent, before transfer to the suspect, was not a search, but that monitoring it inside a private residence, revealing information that could not be obtained by visual surveillance, was a search. Knotts held that monitoring a beeper on public roads is not a search. Jones adds a trespass test, but placement here occurred with the owner's consent before Vance owned the drum. Application: Placing the chip and tracking it on roads fit Karo and Knotts. Three weeks of monitoring inside the home is Karo's search. Why not the others: A ignores Karo's holding on in-home monitoring. C ignores the consent-based placement and Knotts. D reverses the analysis; a single drive home is classic Knotts territory. Policy: Karo protects the home from technology that reports on activity behind its walls, while allowing police to track contraband in public as any observer could.

## ccp-13-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Months of rideshare trip history
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 2
Prompt: Without a warrant, investigators get three months of Delia Rowe's rideshare history from the app company: 240 trips with exact pickup and drop-off points and times, including visits to a fertility clinic, a mosque, a criminal-defense lawyer's office, and the scene of an arson. Which statement best describes the analysis?
A: Clearly not a search, because Rowe voluntarily told the company each pickup and destination.
B: Clearly a search, because any record held by a technology company now requires a warrant.
C: The question is open. The government relies on the voluntary sharing of each trip, as with dialed numbers. Rowe's strong argument draws on the location-records case: three months of precise trips create a detailed, retrospective map of her associations and private life, and using a rideshare app is a common part of modern life. The trip records are less continuous than cell-site data, which helps the government.
D: Not a search, because Rowe lacks standing in records the company created.
Answer: C
Authorities: Carpenter v. United States; Smith v. Maryland; United States v. Knotts
Explanation: Rule: Smith treats information voluntarily conveyed to a third party as generally unprotected, and Knotts holds that individual trips on public roads are exposed. Carpenter refused to apply those rules to comprehensive historical cell-site data, emphasizing its detail, its retrospective reach, its revealing nature, and the lack of meaningful voluntary choice. Application: Rideshare records involve affirmative choices for each trip, which favors the government, and they are episodic rather than continuous. But 240 precise trips over three months reveal medical, religious, and legal visits, the very privacies Carpenter protected. Courts have not resolved this. Why not the others: A treats Smith as automatically controlling despite Carpenter. B overreads Carpenter. D misframes standing; the question is whether Rowe had a protected expectation in the records. Policy: The government says business records of voluntary transactions are fair game and essential to investigations. The defense says aggregated trip data recreates the pervasive location tracking Carpenter warned against.

## ccp-13-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Standing in a storage unit rented in someone else's name
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 3
Prompt: Ezra Pell's sister rents a storage unit in her own name and, with her permission, gives Ezra the only key and the gate code. For a year, Ezra alone uses the unit to store his tools and furniture, locks it with his own padlock, and has kept everyone else out. Officers cut the lock without a warrant and find stolen generators. Can Ezra challenge the search?
A: No, because his name is not on the rental agreement.
B: No, because storage units are commercial spaces, where no one has a legitimate expectation of privacy.
C: No, because only owners of property may challenge searches of it.
D: Likely yes. Legitimate expectations of privacy do not depend on formal title or contract. Ezra had permission, the only key and code, his own lock, exclusive use for a year, and the ability to exclude others, which together give him a legitimate expectation of privacy in the unit.
Answer: D
Authorities: Rakas v. Illinois; Minnesota v. Olson
Explanation: Rule: Rakas held that standing depends on a legitimate expectation of privacy, not property law or formal title. Relevant factors include possession of a key, the ability to exclude others, the frequency and nature of use, and permission from the person in control. Olson shows that people without title can have legitimate expectations based on social understandings. Application: Ezra had exclusive, long-term, permitted use, his own lock, and the power to keep others out. Those facts give him a legitimate expectation of privacy whatever the contract says. Why not the others: A and C make property or contractual title dispositive, which Rakas rejected. B is wrong because commercial spaces can be private, as rented storage units and hotel rooms are. Policy: Linking standing to actual control and use reflects how people really arrange their lives, including informal family arrangements, rather than technicalities that would leave many people unprotected.

## ccp-13-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reasonable reliance on a judge's assurance about a defective form
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 4
Prompt: On a Sunday, Detective Rhea Lorne can find only a warrant form meant for drug cases to use in a homicide investigation. She points out the problem to the judge, who says he will make the needed changes. The judge edits some parts but forgets to change the line that still describes "controlled substances" as the items to be seized. He signs it and tells Lorne it is sufficient. Officers search for and seize only the homicide evidence listed in her affidavit. Should the evidence be suppressed?
A: No. Officers who reasonably rely on a judge's assurance that a warrant is sufficient act in objective good faith even if the judge failed to correct a defect. Suppressing evidence to punish a judge's clerical error would not deter police misconduct.
B: Yes, because a warrant describing the wrong items is void, and nothing can save evidence seized under it.
C: Yes, because officers must always proofread warrants themselves, and any defect they miss requires suppression.
D: No, because particularity is never required for homicide warrants.
Answer: A
Authorities: Massachusetts v. Sheppard; United States v. Leon; Groh v. Ramirez
Explanation: Rule: Sheppard, decided with Leon, held that the exclusionary rule does not apply when officers reasonably relied on a judge's assurance that a warrant form had been corrected and authorized the search, even though the judge failed to fix the description of items to be seized. Groh contrasts: there the officer prepared a facially deficient warrant and could not reasonably rely on it. Application: Lorne flagged the problem, the judge promised to fix it and said the warrant was sufficient, and officers searched only for the items described in the affidavit. That is Sheppard. Why not the others: B ignores the good-faith exception. C overstates officers' duties; Sheppard said officers need not disbelieve a judge who assures them a warrant is valid. D is wrong because particularity applies to every warrant. Policy: Exclusion targets police misconduct. When a judge's error caused the defect and officers acted reasonably, suppression would punish no one responsible and would only let reliable evidence go unused.

## ccp-13-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Remedies for a knock-and-announce violation
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 5
Prompt: Officers executing a warrant to search Corbin Ashe's house for stolen tools burst in without knocking, with no reason to suspect danger or destruction of evidence. They terrify his elderly mother, who falls and breaks her wrist. They find the tools. Ashe moves to suppress, and his mother wants a remedy. Which statement is correct?
A: The tools must be suppressed, because a violation this serious requires exclusion.
B: There is no remedy at all, because knock-and-announce is not a constitutional requirement.
C: The entry violated the Fourth Amendment's knock-and-announce requirement, but suppression is unavailable for such violations. The remedies are a civil damages suit against the officers or the department and internal police discipline.
D: The tools must be suppressed, because the mother was injured.
Answer: C
Authorities: Hudson v. Michigan; Wilson v. Arkansas; Richards v. Wisconsin
Explanation: Rule: Knock-and-announce is part of the Fourth Amendment reasonableness inquiry (Wilson), and it may be skipped only on reasonable suspicion of danger, futility, or destruction of evidence (Richards). Hudson held that the exclusionary rule does not apply to knock-and-announce violations, pointing to civil suits and improvements in police training and discipline as alternative deterrents. Application: The entry was unconstitutional, but under Hudson the tools are admissible. The mother's injury supports a civil claim, not suppression in Ashe's case. Why not the others: A and D ignore Hudson. B contradicts Wilson. Policy: Hudson reasons that the interests knock-and-announce protects, such as dignity, property, and safety, are unrelated to seizing evidence, so suppression would be a disproportionate remedy. The dissent argued that civil suits rarely succeed, leaving the rule without effective enforcement.

## ccp-13-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Probable cause must account for exculpatory facts
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 6
Prompt: A witness tells officers that "a tall man named Tobias" stole a kayak from the Osprey Landing marina at 3 p.m. Officers identify Tobias Fenn, who is tall and lives nearby. Before arresting him, they see time-stamped video, which they have no reason to doubt, showing Fenn at a dental appointment across town from 2:30 to 4 p.m. They arrest him anyway. Did they have probable cause?
A: Yes, because once a witness names a suspect, officers have probable cause to arrest.
B: No. Probable cause is judged by the totality of the circumstances known to the officers, including facts that point away from guilt. Reliable video placing Fenn across town at the time of the theft defeats any fair probability that he committed it.
C: Yes, because officers need not consider alibi evidence before an arrest.
D: Yes, because probable cause is a low standard, and a name and height match is always enough.
Answer: B
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Maryland v. Pringle
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause exists when the facts and circumstances within the officers' knowledge would lead a reasonable person to believe there is a fair probability the suspect committed the crime, assessed on the totality of the circumstances (Gates; Pringle). Officers may not ignore facts they know that undermine that belief. Application: A first name and height match create a weak link. Reliable, time-stamped video placing Fenn elsewhere during the theft eliminates any fair probability. Why not the others: A treats any naming as automatically sufficient. C misstates the totality test; officers need not investigate every possible alibi, but they cannot disregard a reliable one already in hand. D overstates how low the standard is. Policy: Probable cause protects against arresting people the evidence shows are innocent. Requiring officers to consider what they already know prevents arrests that serve no legitimate purpose and cause real harm.

## ccp-13-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Arrest warrant entry without reason to believe the suspect is home
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 7
Prompt: Officers hold an arrest warrant for Marisol Penn, who works full time as a nurse on weekday day shifts. At 2 p.m. on a Tuesday, with her car gone from the driveway, the lights off, and no sign anyone is home, they open her door with a locksmith, enter, and look through every room. She is not there, but they see stolen jewelry on her dresser. Was the entry lawful?
A: Likely not. An arrest warrant carries limited authority to enter the suspect's own home only when there is reason to believe she is inside. With her car gone, the lights off, and a weekday when she normally works, officers had no reason to believe she was home.
B: Yes, because an arrest warrant authorizes entering the suspect's home at any time.
C: Yes, because officers may enter a home whenever they have an arrest warrant and probable cause that evidence is inside.
D: Yes, because the jewelry was in plain view.
Answer: A
Authorities: Payton v. New York; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Payton held that an arrest warrant implicitly carries limited authority to enter the suspect's home when there is reason to believe the suspect is within. The authority is tied to finding and arresting the suspect. Plain view requires that officers be lawfully where they see an item (Horton). Application: Every visible fact suggested Penn was at work. Without reason to believe she was inside, the warrant did not authorize the entry, and plain view cannot rest on an unlawful entry. Why not the others: B drops Payton's reason-to-believe limit. C mixes up arrest and search authority; an arrest warrant does not authorize searching for evidence. D assumes the lawful presence plain view requires. Policy: The reason-to-believe limit keeps arrest warrants from becoming general licenses to enter homes when the person is plainly absent, protecting the home while still letting officers act on real information.

## ccp-13-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A flip phone's call log after arrest
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 8
Prompt: After arresting Ronan Coyle for a drug sale, an officer takes a basic flip phone from his pocket. The phone shows "My House" calling repeatedly. Without a warrant, the officer opens the phone, scrolls the call log, and uses it to find Coyle's home address. Was the call-log search valid as a search incident to arrest?
A: Yes, because a flip phone holds so little information that the warrant rule for smartphones does not apply.
B: Yes, because call logs are like phone numbers dialed through a phone company, which are unprotected.
C: Yes, because the arrestee's phone was found on his person.
D: No. The rule that police generally must get a warrant before searching data on a phone seized incident to arrest applies to all cell phones, including basic flip phones. The call log is digital data stored on the phone, and nothing suggests an exigency.
Answer: D
Authorities: Riley v. California; Smith v. Maryland; United States v. Robinson
Explanation: Rule: Riley, decided with a companion case involving a flip phone whose call log police searched, held that officers must generally get a warrant before searching digital information on a cell phone seized incident to arrest. The Court declined to distinguish flip phones from smartphones. Robinson's rule for physical items does not extend to digital data. Smith concerns records held by the phone company, not data stored on the phone itself. Application: The officer scrolled stored data on the phone without a warrant or exigency. Riley controls. Why not the others: A creates an exception Riley rejected. B confuses records obtained from the phone company with data stored on the device. C applies Robinson's physical-items rule. Policy: A single rule for all phones is easy for officers to follow and avoids case-by-case judgments about which devices hold enough private data.

## ccp-13-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reading documents in detail during an inventory
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 9
Prompt: Osprey Landing's inventory policy requires officers to list all containers and items of value in impounded vehicles. Inventorying Lark Orsini's impounded car, an officer finds a folder of papers. Rather than listing "folder of papers," he reads each page closely for 20 minutes and finds a detailed plan to commit insurance fraud. Is the evidence admissible under the inventory exception?
A: Likely not. An inventory may list a vehicle's contents to protect property and guard against claims, but reading documents page by page goes beyond what an inventory requires and looks like an investigative search, especially when the policy calls only for listing items.
B: Yes, because an inventory permits examining all contents of a car in any detail.
C: Yes, because the papers were in plain view once the folder was opened.
D: Yes, because the officer was following an inventory policy.
Answer: A
Authorities: Colorado v. Bertine; Illinois v. Lafayette; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Inventory searches are justified by caretaking purposes and must follow standardized procedures, without being a pretext for investigation (Bertine). Their scope is limited to what those purposes require, such as identifying and listing items. Plain view requires that the incriminating character be immediately apparent without further search (Horton). Application: Listing a folder of papers serves the inventory's purpose. Reading every page for 20 minutes serves only investigation and goes beyond the policy, which called for listing. The plan's incriminating character became apparent only through that reading. Why not the others: B ignores the inventory's purpose-based limits. C fails because the content was not apparent without reading. D ignores that the officer went beyond what the policy required. Policy: Inventories are tolerated because they are not investigative. Using them to read private documents would convert every impoundment into an opportunity to examine a person's papers, which the Fourth Amendment expressly protects.

## ccp-13-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Warrantless inspection of an auto-dismantling business
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 10
Prompt: A state statute requires auto dismantlers to be licensed, keep records of vehicles and parts, and allow inspections during regular business hours of their records and the vehicles and parts on the premises. During business hours, officers inspect Cassius Rowan's dismantling yard under the statute, without a warrant, and find stolen car parts. Rowan argues the inspection was really aimed at finding evidence of crimes. Is it valid?
A: No, because officers needed a warrant for any inspection of a business.
B: No, because an inspection that may uncover evidence of crime is a criminal search requiring probable cause.
C: No, because police officers, rather than civil inspectors, carried out the inspection.
D: Yes. Auto dismantling is a closely regulated industry, the state has a substantial interest in deterring car theft, warrantless inspections are necessary to make the scheme work, and the statute limits the time, place, and scope of inspections. That the scheme also serves to uncover crime does not invalidate it.
Answer: D
Authorities: New York v. Burger; Camara v. Municipal Court
Explanation: Rule: Burger upheld warrantless inspections of auto dismantlers under a statute satisfying three requirements: a substantial government interest, inspections necessary to further the regulatory scheme, and a statute that provides a constitutionally adequate substitute for a warrant by limiting time, place, and scope. The Court held that a regulatory scheme may share a goal with criminal law and may be enforced by police. Camara governs non-closely-regulated inspections. Application: The facts track Burger: a closely regulated industry, a statute limited to business hours with defined scope, and an interest in stopping car theft. Why not the others: A ignores the closely regulated industry exception. B and C restate arguments Burger rejected. Policy: Burger recognizes that businesses that knowingly enter heavily regulated fields accept inspection, and it lets the state police markets for stolen goods. Critics argue it lets the administrative exception become a tool for criminal investigation without probable cause.

## ccp-13-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A present co-occupant who does not object
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 11
Prompt: Officers ask Gemma Lorne for permission to search the house she shares with her husband Dorian for evidence of a stolen-check scheme. She agrees. Dorian is sitting in the kitchen a few feet away, hears the whole exchange, and says nothing while officers search. They find stolen checks in a shared desk. Dorian argues that, as a present co-occupant, his rights were violated. Is the search valid?
A: No, because a present co-occupant's rights override another's consent whenever he is in the room.
B: Yes. A co-occupant's refusal overrides another's consent only when he is physically present and expressly objects. Dorian was present but said nothing, so Gemma's consent, given with common authority over the shared desk, controls.
C: No, because officers must ask every present occupant for consent.
D: Yes, but only because Gemma owns the house.
Answer: B
Authorities: Georgia v. Randolph; United States v. Matlock; Fernandez v. California
Explanation: Rule: Randolph held that a physically present co-occupant's express refusal overrides another's consent. The rule is limited: officers need not seek out other occupants, and a present occupant who does not object cannot invalidate the consent (Randolph; Fernandez). A co-occupant with common authority may consent to the search of shared areas (Matlock). Application: Dorian was present but silent. Gemma had common authority over the shared desk. Why not the others: A drops the express-objection requirement. C imposes a duty Randolph disclaimed. D treats property ownership as decisive, while common authority turns on use and access. Policy: Randolph's narrow rule reflects social norms, since a visitor would leave if a present resident told him to, and gives officers a clear line without requiring them to poll everyone in a house.

## ccp-13-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Emergency entry to find a missing child
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 12
Prompt: Nine-year-old Wren Tallis has been missing for four hours after a stranger was seen talking to her at a bus stop. Her phone's location places it inside a house on Gull Street. Officers knock and get no answer, though they hear a television. They enter without a warrant, search rooms large enough to hold a child, find her unharmed in a back bedroom with a man, and see child-exploitation images on a monitor in the same room. Was the entry lawful?
A: No, because the phone's location could be wrong, so officers needed a warrant.
B: No, because emergency aid requires that officers see or hear someone in distress.
C: Likely yes. The facts give an objectively reasonable basis to believe a missing child was inside and in immediate danger. Entering and searching places a child could be was within the emergency, and the images on the monitor in the room where she was found were in plain view.
D: Yes, and once inside the officers could search the computer for other evidence.
Answer: C
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe that someone inside needs immediate aid or protection from imminent harm. The scope is limited to the emergency (Fisher). Items seen in plain view during the lawful entry may be seized (Horton). Application: A young child missing for hours after contact with a stranger, her phone inside the house, and no response all give an objectively reasonable belief that she was in danger inside. Searching places a child could be fits the emergency, and the images were in plain view where she was found. Why not the others: A demands certainty the objective test does not require. B demands a specific kind of sign. D exceeds the emergency's scope; searching the computer requires a warrant. Policy: Emergency aid lets officers protect vulnerable people, especially children, when delay could be catastrophic. Strict scope limits keep the rescue from becoming a full search.

## ccp-13-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A vague description is not reasonable suspicion
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 13
Prompt: Dispatch reports a robbery by "a young man in a dark hoodie" somewhere in Osprey Landing's downtown, a 20-block area, 45 minutes earlier. On a cold evening, an officer stops Jonah Ferris, a young man in a dark hoodie walking calmly ten blocks from the robbery, among dozens of others dressed similarly. Was the stop supported by reasonable suspicion?
A: Yes, because he matched the description.
B: Yes, because any match to a description of a violent crime allows a stop.
C: Yes, because officers may stop people in an area where a crime recently occurred.
D: No. Reasonable suspicion must be particularized to the person stopped. A generic description that fits many people in a large area, nearly an hour later, with nothing about Ferris's behavior suggesting involvement, does not give specific, articulable facts pointing to him.
Answer: D
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Illinois v. Gates
Explanation: Rule: A Terry stop requires reasonable suspicion based on specific and articulable facts particularized to the person stopped, judged on the totality of the circumstances (Terry). A description's value depends on how distinctive it is and how closely the person matches in time, place, and conduct. Application: "Young man in a dark hoodie" describes dozens of people on a cold downtown evening. The time gap and distance are significant, and Ferris did nothing suspicious. Nothing distinguishes him from other people dressed the same way. Why not the others: A treats a generic match as particularized suspicion. B makes the seriousness of the crime decisive. C allows stops based on location alone. Policy: Particularized suspicion protects people, often young men of color, from being stopped simply because they resemble a vague description. Police can still act on distinctive descriptions close in time and place.

## ccp-13-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Suspicionless look into a closet next to the arrest
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 14
Prompt: Executing an arrest warrant, officers handcuff Saoirse Lane in her bedroom. Without any specific reason to suspect anyone else is there, an officer opens the walk-in closet that opens directly off the bedroom, steps in, and looks around. He sees a stolen rifle leaning against the wall. Was looking into the closet lawful?
A: No, because a protective sweep always requires articulable facts that a dangerous person is present.
B: No, because officers may never look anywhere other than the arrestee's person after an arrest.
C: Yes. Incident to an in-home arrest, officers may, as a precaution and without any suspicion, look in closets and other spaces immediately next to the place of arrest from which an attack could be launched. The rifle was in plain view during that lawful look.
D: Yes, because officers may search the entire home incident to any arrest.
Answer: C
Authorities: Maryland v. Buie; Horton v. California; Chimel v. California
Explanation: Rule: Buie recognized two levels of protective sweep incident to an in-home arrest. First, as a precaution and without probable cause or reasonable suspicion, officers may look in closets and other spaces immediately next to the place of arrest from which an attack could immediately be launched. Second, sweeps beyond those spaces require articulable facts suggesting a dangerous person is present. Plain view lets officers seize items they see while lawfully looking (Horton). Application: A walk-in closet opening directly off the bedroom is an adjoining space under Buie's first level. The look was cursory and for people. The rifle was in plain view. Why not the others: A ignores Buie's first, suspicionless level. B ignores Buie and Chimel's grab-area rule. D states the whole-house rule Chimel rejected. Policy: Buie's first level reflects the danger of an ambush from spaces right next to an arrest, while its limits keep the sweep a narrow safety measure.

## ccp-13-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A paid jailhouse informant as a government agent
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 15
Prompt: Lazlo Venn, indicted for armed robbery, is jailed with Pike Morrow, a paid, registered informant. Prosecutors had told Morrow to "be alert to anything" Venn says about his case, without telling him to ask questions. Over two weeks, Morrow repeatedly asks Venn about the robbery, sympathizes, and urges him to "get it off your chest." Venn describes the robbery, and Morrow reports it. Are the statements admissible at Venn's robbery trial?
A: No. Morrow was a government agent, paid and acting under an arrangement with prosecutors, and he deliberately elicited statements about the charged offense by questioning and encouraging Venn. Instructions to only listen do not help the government when the agent actively draws out the statements.
B: Yes, because the government told Morrow only to be alert, not to ask questions.
C: Yes, because Venn was not in custody in the Miranda sense.
D: Yes, because Venn made the statements voluntarily.
Answer: A
Authorities: Massiah v. United States; Kuhlmann v. Wilson; Illinois v. Perkins
Explanation: Rule: After the right to counsel attaches, Massiah forbids the government from deliberately eliciting incriminating statements, including through informants acting as its agents. Kuhlmann allows purely passive listening. The question is what the informant actually did, not just what he was told. Perkins addresses Miranda, not the Sixth Amendment. Application: Morrow was paid and had an arrangement with prosecutors, which makes him an agent. Rather than listening passively, he questioned and encouraged Venn over two weeks, which is deliberate elicitation. Why not the others: B relies on instructions while ignoring the informant's conduct. C confuses Miranda custody with the Sixth Amendment, which does not require custody. D is wrong because voluntariness does not resolve a Massiah claim. Policy: The government cannot evade the right to counsel by using paid agents to do what officers could not do directly. Close attention to the agent's actual conduct prevents nominal "just listen" instructions from becoming a loophole.

## ccp-13-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Police allowing a spouse's visit that they record
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 16
Prompt: After Hollis Ward invokes his right to counsel and questioning stops, his wife, on her own initiative, insists on seeing him. Officers reluctantly agree, tell both of them that an officer will be present, and place a visible recorder on the table. During the visit, his wife asks what happened, and Ward makes incriminating statements. Was this interrogation?
A: Yes, because any conversation recorded by police in custody is interrogation.
B: Likely not. Police did not plan the visit to elicit statements; they allowed a meeting the wife demanded, told both that an officer would be present, and recorded openly. Officers who simply permit such a conversation are not engaging in express questioning or its functional equivalent, even if they know incriminating statements are possible.
C: Yes, because the wife was acting as an agent of the police.
D: Yes, because police knew Ward might say something incriminating.
Answer: B
Authorities: Rhode Island v. Innis; Edwards v. Arizona; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Interrogation is express questioning or its functional equivalent, meaning words or actions police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response (Innis). After invocation of counsel, police may not initiate interrogation (Edwards). Allowing a requested visit, with open recording and no police prompting, is not police interrogation, even if officers know incriminating statements might result. Application: The wife initiated the visit on her own, officers did not ask questions or direct her, and the recording was disclosed. The questioning came from the wife, not the police. Why not the others: A treats recording as interrogation. C has no facts to support agency. D overstates Innis, which asks whether police words or actions are reasonably likely to elicit a response, not whether incriminating statements are merely possible. Policy: The interrogation definition targets police tactics designed to overcome the suspect's will. Permitting family visits, an important humane practice, would become impossible if every resulting statement were treated as interrogation.

## ccp-13-q17
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Public safety exception after the danger is over
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 17
Prompt: Officers arrest Bryn Alder in her own apartment for a shooting the previous week. She is alone, handcuffed, and secured. Officers spend 40 minutes thoroughly searching the apartment under a warrant and find nothing. Then, before any warnings, a detective asks, "Where did you put the gun from last week?" She says she threw it in the river. Is the answer admissible under the public safety exception?
A: Likely not. The exception covers questions prompted by an objectively reasonable need to protect officers or the public from an immediate danger. With Alder secured, the apartment searched, no one else present, and the gun a week old, there was no immediate danger. The question was ordinary investigation.
B: Yes, because questions about guns always fall within the exception.
C: Yes, because the officers might have felt uneasy about an unfound gun.
D: No, because the public safety exception applies only to questions asked in public places.
Answer: A
Authorities: New York v. Quarles; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Quarles applies when officers ask questions necessary to address an immediate threat to public or officer safety, such as a gun just discarded in a supermarket. The test is objective and limited to the exigency. Application: There was no immediate danger. Alder was secured, the apartment had been thoroughly searched, no one else was there, and the gun had been gone for a week. Asking where it went is evidence-gathering. Why not the others: B treats the subject matter as decisive. C relies on vague, subjective unease, while Quarles requires an objective, immediate danger. D invents a location limit; the exception turns on danger, not on whether the place is public. Policy: The public safety exception is a narrow necessity exception. Applying it to routine questions about weapons would let it consume Miranda in every gun case.

## ccp-13-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Resuming questioning about a different crime after silence
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 18
Prompt: Arrested for a string of car break-ins, Damian Ruel is warned and says, "I don't want to talk about any break-ins," and questioning stops immediately. Three hours later, a detective from the homicide unit, who has not spoken with Ruel before, gives full fresh warnings and asks only about an unrelated killing. Ruel waives and makes incriminating statements about the killing. Are they admissible?
A: No, because once a suspect invokes the right to silence, police may never question him again about anything.
B: Likely yes. Police scrupulously honored his invocation: they stopped immediately, waited a significant time, gave fresh warnings, and used a different officer on a different crime.
C: No, because police must wait 14 days after any invocation before questioning again.
D: No, because the second detective had to obtain a lawyer for Ruel first.
Answer: B
Authorities: Michigan v. Mosley; Edwards v. Arizona; Maryland v. Shatzer
Explanation: Rule: Mosley held that police may resume questioning after a suspect invokes the right to silence if the right to cut off questioning was scrupulously honored, considering whether questioning stopped immediately, whether a significant time passed, whether fresh warnings were given, and whether the later questioning concerned a different crime. Edwards's stricter rule applies to counsel invocations, and Shatzer's 14-day break addresses Edwards. Application: These facts closely track Mosley. Every factor favors admission. Why not the others: A states a permanent bar Mosley rejected. C misapplies Shatzer, which concerns counsel invocations. D imposes a counsel requirement Ruel never triggered, since he invoked silence, not counsel. Policy: Mosley balances the suspect's control over questioning against legitimate law enforcement needs. A permanent bar would keep police from investigating other crimes, and honoring the invocation prevents them from simply wearing the suspect down.

## ccp-13-q19
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A lineup where only the suspect fits the description
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 19
Prompt: A witness tells police a burglar had a full red beard and a shaved head. In the lineup, Ansel Brook is the only one of six men with a red beard and a shaved head; the other five are clean-shaven with full heads of hair. The witness, who saw the burglar for about five seconds through a rain-streaked window at night, picks Brook, saying, "I think so." Is the identification admissible?
A: Yes, because a lineup with six people is always fair.
B: Yes, because the witness ultimately picked the suspect, which shows reliability.
C: Likely not. Making the suspect the only participant who matches the key features of the description is unnecessarily suggestive. Under the totality of the circumstances, the brief, obstructed nighttime view and the tentative certainty give little independent reliability to outweigh that suggestion.
D: No, because counsel was not present, regardless of when the lineup occurred.
Answer: C
Authorities: Manson v. Brathwaite; Neil v. Biggers; Foster v. California; Kirby v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: An identification procedure is unnecessarily suggestive when police arrange it so the suspect stands out, as in Foster. The identification is then admissible only if reliable under the totality of the circumstances, weighing the opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of the description, certainty, and time elapsed (Biggers; Manson). The right to counsel at a lineup depends on whether formal charges have begun (Kirby). Application: Making Brook the only one with the described features effectively told the witness whom to pick. Five seconds through a rain-streaked window at night and "I think so" show weak reliability. Why not the others: A treats the number of participants as decisive, rather than composition. B uses the result of a suggestive procedure as proof of reliability. D ignores that the counsel question depends on timing. Policy: Fair lineup composition is one of the most effective protections against mistaken identification. Exclusion here encourages police to choose fillers who match the description.

## ccp-13-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: No good faith when the magistrate abandons the neutral role
Set: CCP-13
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Osprey Landing
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 10, Question 20
Prompt: An Osprey Landing magistrate signs a warrant to search an adult bookstore for "obscene materials" and then accompanies officers to the store. During the search, he decides on the spot which items they may seize, adding them to the warrant as they go. The prosecution argues good faith. What result?
A: The good-faith exception applies, because officers may always rely on a magistrate's decisions.
B: The good-faith exception applies, because the magistrate personally saw each item.
C: The evidence is admissible because the store had a reduced expectation of privacy.
D: The good-faith exception does not apply. Officers cannot reasonably rely on a warrant when the magistrate wholly abandoned his neutral and detached role by joining the search team and making seizure decisions as a participant.
Answer: D
Authorities: United States v. Leon; Lo-Ji Sales v. New York
Explanation: Rule: Leon's good-faith exception does not apply when the issuing magistrate wholly abandoned the judicial role, giving the example of Lo-Ji Sales, where a town justice joined the search of a bookstore and decided on the spot what to seize. The warrant requirement depends on a neutral and detached magistrate. Application: The magistrate acted as part of the search team rather than as a neutral judge. No reasonable officer could believe that process met the warrant requirement. Why not the others: A ignores Leon's exceptions. B misunderstands the problem; the magistrate's presence made him a participant, not a neutral reviewer. C misstates the law; businesses retain Fourth Amendment protection. Policy: The neutral magistrate is the core of the warrant process. When the magistrate joins the police team, there is no independent check left, and the reason for relying on warrants disappears.
