## ccp-11-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Thermal imaging once the technology becomes consumer-grade
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 1
Prompt: A $200 thermal-camera attachment for smartphones is now sold at hardware stores, and millions of people own one to find drafts and leaks. A Glenmara officer stands on the public street and points one at Dorian Vael's house, detecting unusual heat from the attic consistent with grow lamps. The heat reading supports a warrant. Was the scan a search?
A: This is unsettled. The thermal-imaging case turned partly on the device not being in general public use, which favors the government now that such devices are common. Vael's strongest argument is that the case's deeper rationale protects all information about the inside of the home obtained by technology that could not otherwise be had without entering, and that the line at the home's walls should not shrink as gadgets become cheaper.
B: It was clearly a search, because any heat reading of a home is a search under any circumstances.
C: It was clearly not a search, because once a technology is sold to the public, using it on homes can never be a search.
D: It was not a search, because heat escaping a house is abandoned, like curbside trash.
Answer: A
Authorities: Kyllo v. United States; California v. Greenwood; Katz v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Kyllo held that using a device not in general public use to explore details of the home that would previously have been unknowable without physical intrusion is a search. The "general public use" qualifier has been criticized as making privacy shrink as technology spreads. Application: The facts test exactly that weakness. The government relies on the qualifier. Vael relies on Kyllo's emphasis on the home's interior and its stated goal of preserving the privacy that existed when the Fourth Amendment was adopted. The Court has not revisited the question. Why not the others: B ignores the general-public-use qualifier entirely. C treats the qualifier as decisive and ignores the home-focused rationale. D misapplies Greenwood; heat is not an item deliberately exposed to the public, and Kyllo rejected a similar off-the-wall argument. Policy: A rule tied to the home's walls gives stable protection. A rule tied to general public use adapts to social expectations but risks letting privacy erode with every consumer gadget.

## ccp-11-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Cryptocurrency exchange records and the third-party doctrine
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 2
Prompt: Investigators tracing ransom payments use an administrative subpoena, without a warrant, to obtain from a cryptocurrency exchange Thea Morrow's account records: identity documents, deposit and withdrawal amounts, and the public wallet addresses she transacted with. She argues the location-records case requires a warrant. What is the likely result?
A: She wins, because all digital financial data now requires a warrant.
B: She likely loses. She voluntarily gave the exchange these transaction records to use its services, and they reveal financial transactions rather than a comprehensive, continuous record of her physical movements. The third-party doctrine likely applies, especially since the transactions are also recorded on a public ledger.
C: She wins, because cryptocurrency is designed to be private.
D: She loses, because she has no Fourth Amendment rights in anything stored online.
Answer: B
Authorities: Smith v. Maryland; Carpenter v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Smith treats records voluntarily conveyed to a third party in the course of business as unprotected. Carpenter refused to extend that rule to historical cell-site data because of its comprehensive, involuntary, and revealing nature, but described its decision as narrow and left conventional business records, including financial records, untouched. Application: Exchange records are business records of transactions Morrow voluntarily made, and the blockchain itself is public. Courts have generally applied the third-party doctrine to such records. Why not the others: A overreads Carpenter. C confuses a technology's marketing with a legal expectation of privacy. D goes too far; content stored online, such as email text, can be protected. Policy: The third-party doctrine lets investigators follow the money in financial crimes. Critics argue that it gives the government easy access to detailed financial lives as more of life moves through intermediaries.

## ccp-11-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Standing of a social, daytime dinner guest
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 3
Prompt: Olivia Marten invites her close friend Bastien Reyes to her house for a four-hour Sunday dinner. He brings dessert, hangs his coat in her front closet, and plans to leave by 9 p.m. Officers enter without a warrant or exception and search his coat pocket in the closet, finding stolen jewelry. Can Reyes challenge the search?
A: No, because only overnight guests can ever have a legitimate expectation of privacy in another person's home.
B: No, because he was present for a short time, which alone defeats standing.
C: He has a strong argument that he can. The case denying protection to brief visitors emphasized their purely commercial purpose and lack of connection to the home. A close friend's social visit, with his belongings stored in the home, looks more like the protected overnight guest than a business visitor. He can at least challenge the search of his own coat.
D: Yes, because anyone lawfully in a home may challenge any search of it.
Answer: C
Authorities: Minnesota v. Olson; Minnesota v. Carter; Rakas v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: Olson protects overnight guests. Carter denied protection to visitors present for a few hours to bag cocaine, emphasizing the commercial nature of the visit, its short duration, and the lack of any prior connection. Justice Kennedy's concurrence suggested almost all social guests have a legitimate expectation of privacy. Rights are personal (Rakas). Application: Reyes is a close friend on a social visit, which is the opposite of Carter's facts. His coat is his own effect placed in his host's closet. His argument is strong, though the Court has not squarely decided daytime social guests. Why not the others: A treats Olson as the minimum. B treats duration as decisive. D restates the presence test Rakas rejected. Policy: Society recognizes that guests in a friend's home expect privacy. Limiting standing to overnight stays would leave ordinary social life unprotected.

## ccp-11-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Errors in a warrant's description of the place
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 4
Prompt: A warrant authorizes searching "1412 Thistle Lane, a yellow two-story house with green shutters and a detached red garage, the residence of Corwin Hale." Hale actually lives at 1421 Thistle Lane, the only yellow house with green shutters and a red garage on the street. The executing officer had personally conducted surveillance there. Officers search 1421 and find stolen goods. Is the search valid?
A: No, because the address was wrong, so the warrant described a different place.
B: No, because the warrant must be reissued whenever there is any mistake.
C: Yes, because officers may search any place they believe is the suspect's home.
D: Yes. A description is sufficient if it lets officers identify the intended place with reasonable effort and there is no reasonable probability another place will be mistakenly searched. The detailed physical description, Hale's name, and the officer's personal knowledge removed any real risk of searching the wrong house.
Answer: D
Authorities: Particularity requirement; U.S. v. Brobst; Maryland v. Garrison
Explanation: Rule: Particularity requires a description that enables the executing officer, with reasonable effort, to identify the place intended and avoids a reasonable probability of searching the wrong premises. Technical errors, such as a transposed address, do not invalidate a warrant when other details identify the place unambiguously (the Brobst line of description-error cases in the outline). Garrison judges the reasonableness of officers' factual mistakes. Application: Transposed digits aside, the color, shutters, garage, owner's name, and the officer's surveillance all point to one house. Why not the others: A treats any address error as fatal. B invents a reissuance rule. C eliminates the warrant's constraint. Policy: Particularity protects against searching the wrong home. Courts look to practical accuracy rather than perfection, so clerical errors do not free defendants when no one's privacy was actually at risk of being mistakenly invaded.

## ccp-11-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Knock-and-announce: refusal inferred from flight
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 5
Prompt: Executing a warrant for counterfeit currency, officers knock and announce "Police, search warrant!" at Ines Corvo's front door. Through the glass door they see Corvo look straight at them, turn, and sprint toward the back of the house. Five seconds after announcing, they force the door. Was the entry reasonable?
A: No, because officers must always wait at least 15 to 20 seconds.
B: Yes. The wait required depends on the circumstances. Seeing the occupant recognize the police and run away is effectively a refusal of admittance and raises a risk of flight or destruction of evidence, so a very short wait was reasonable.
C: No, because officers may never enter in less than a minute.
D: Yes, because officers need not wait at all once they have a warrant.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Banks; Wilson v. Arkansas; Richards v. Wisconsin
Explanation: Rule: Knock-and-announce requires officers to announce their presence and purpose and wait a reasonable time before forcing entry (Wilson). What is reasonable depends on the totality of the circumstances, viewed from the officers' perspective (Banks, upholding a 15-to-20-second wait for drugs). Officers may also act on reasonable suspicion of danger, futility, or destruction of evidence (Richards). Application: Corvo saw the officers and ran. That amounts to a refusal of entry and creates a risk that she will flee or destroy counterfeit bills. Five seconds was reasonable. Why not the others: A and C treat Banks's facts as a fixed minimum. D eliminates the requirement altogether. Policy: Knock-and-announce gives occupants a chance to comply peacefully, but that purpose disappears once an occupant shows she will not.

## ccp-11-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Strong basis of knowledge offsetting unknown reliability
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 6
Prompt: A first-time informant of unknown reliability tells officers that this morning, inside Pell Morrow's basement at 7 Quarry Road, she saw about a kilogram of white powder wrapped in green cellophane on a workbench under a red lamp, next to a digital scale and stacks of small baggies, and that Morrow told her it was "pure" cocaine "ready to move." The affidavit recites only her statement. Which approach supports issuing the warrant?
A: The totality-of-the-circumstances approach. A detailed, firsthand, very recent account, with specific details and an admission from the suspect, can make up for the informant's unknown track record. That is enough for a magistrate to find a substantial basis, though it is a close case. Under the older two-pronged test, the unproven veracity alone would have defeated the warrant.
B: The older two-pronged test, because a strong basis of knowledge satisfies both prongs at once.
C: Neither approach, because a first-time informant can never support a warrant.
D: Both approaches equally, because they always reach the same result.
Answer: A
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Aguilar v. Texas; Spinelli v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under Aguilar-Spinelli, the affidavit had to establish both the informant's basis of knowledge and her veracity independently. Gates replaced that test with the totality of the circumstances, holding that a strong showing on one factor, such as an explicit and detailed firsthand description, can make up for a deficiency in the other. Application: The informant's account is firsthand, recent, highly specific, and includes Morrow's own admission. Under Gates, that detail can carry the affidavit despite unknown reliability. Under Aguilar-Spinelli, the veracity prong would fail. Why not the others: B misdescribes the older test, which required both prongs independently. C is wrong under Gates. D ignores that the tests differ precisely in cases like this. Policy: Gates's flexibility lets magistrates credit detailed accounts from new sources. Critics argue that detailed lies are easy to fabricate and that dropping the veracity requirement weakens protection against false accusations.

## ccp-11-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consensual entry followed by an in-home arrest
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 7
Prompt: Detectives with probable cause, but no warrant, to arrest Sabine Croft for a felony theft knock on her door. When she answers, they ask, "Can we come in and talk?" She says, "Sure, come in," and steps aside. Inside the living room they arrest her. Was the arrest unlawful under the warrant requirement for home arrests?
A: Yes, because police may never arrest someone inside her home without an arrest warrant.
B: Yes, because asking to come in was a trick to avoid getting a warrant.
C: No, because the warrant requirement for home arrests applies only to misdemeanors.
D: No. The warrant requirement forbids warrantless, nonconsensual entry into a home to make a routine felony arrest. Croft voluntarily let the detectives in, and once they were lawfully inside with probable cause, they could arrest her.
Answer: D
Authorities: Payton v. New York; Schneckloth v. Bustamonte; United States v. Watson
Explanation: Rule: Payton prohibits warrantless, nonconsensual entry into a home to make a routine felony arrest. Consent is a well-established exception to the warrant requirement, if voluntary (Schneckloth). Officers lawfully present with probable cause may arrest without a warrant (Watson). Application: Croft voluntarily invited the officers in. Nothing suggests coercion. The arrest followed lawful entry and was supported by probable cause. Why not the others: A ignores consent. B treats an honest request to enter as a trick; officers are not required to announce their plans, and nothing suggests the consent was involuntary. C reverses the rule; Payton concerned felony arrests. Policy: Payton protects the home against unconsented police entry. When a resident freely invites officers in, the core concern is absent, and requiring a warrant would serve little purpose.

## ccp-11-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Grab area during an in-home arrest
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 8
Prompt: Officers arrest Hollis Varn in his bedroom with an arrest warrant. Before handcuffing him, while he stands unrestrained next to his dresser, an officer opens the top dresser drawer within his reach and finds a pistol. Another officer then goes to the attic two floors up and finds stolen goods. Which evidence is admissible as a search incident to arrest?
A: Both, because search incident to arrest covers the entire home in which the arrest occurs.
B: Neither, because officers may never search anywhere in a home without a search warrant.
C: The pistol only. Search incident to arrest covers the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control, meaning where he could grab a weapon or destroy evidence. The open dresser drawer within his reach qualifies; the attic two floors away does not.
D: The stolen goods only, because they were evidence of a crime.
Answer: C
Authorities: Chimel v. California; Maryland v. Buie
Explanation: Rule: Chimel limits searches incident to arrest to the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control, the area from which he might gain possession of a weapon or destructible evidence. It rejected searching the entire house. Buie separately permits protective sweeps for people, not evidence. Application: Varn was unrestrained next to the dresser, so the drawer was within his grab area. The attic, two floors away, was not, and a search there for evidence is not a protective sweep. Why not the others: A states the rule Chimel overruled. B ignores the search-incident exception. D reverses the analysis; being evidence does not authorize a search outside the grab area. Policy: Chimel ties the exception to its justifications, officer safety and evidence preservation, and prevents arrests from becoming excuses for general searches of the home.

## ccp-11-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Delayed search of an impounded vehicle
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 9
Prompt: Officers stop Ondine Petr's pickup with probable cause that it carries stolen catalytic converters. With traffic dangerous and night falling, they tow it to the police lot. Three days later, without a warrant, detectives search it and find converters under a tarp. Does the delay defeat the automobile exception?
A: Yes, because once a vehicle is impounded, there is no exigency, so a warrant is required.
B: Yes, because a vehicle search must happen within 24 hours of the stop.
C: Yes, because impoundment converts any later search into an inventory search.
D: No. When officers have probable cause to search a vehicle at the scene, they may instead take it to the station and search it later without a warrant, as long as the probable cause continues and the delay is not unreasonable.
Answer: D
Authorities: Carroll v. United States; California v. Acevedo; United States v. Ross
Explanation: Rule: The automobile exception rests on probable cause and the vehicle's ready mobility and reduced expectation of privacy, not on case-specific exigency (Carroll; Acevedo). Courts allow officers who could have searched on the spot to take the vehicle to the station and search it later without a warrant, since that is no more intrusive than an immediate roadside search. Ross defines the scope as whatever the probable cause supports. Application: Probable cause existed at the scene and continued, and towing was reasonable given the conditions. A three-day delay does not, by itself, make the search unreasonable. Why not the others: A imposes a case-specific exigency requirement. B invents a time limit. C confuses the two doctrines; an inventory requires standardized procedures, but this search rests on probable cause. Policy: Allowing delayed searches avoids forcing officers to choose between a dangerous roadside search and losing the exception. Critics argue that once a car is in police custody, getting a warrant is easy and should be required.

## ccp-11-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Border inspection of international mail
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 10
Prompt: At Glenmara's international mail facility, a customs officer, with no suspicion, opens a padded envelope arriving from overseas and addressed to Lisette Marn. It is noticeably heavier than its declared contents. Inside she finds pills. Was the warrantless opening valid?
A: No, because opening mail always requires a warrant.
B: No, because opening international mail requires probable cause.
C: Yes. International mail entering the country is subject to inspection at the border or its functional equivalent, and the government's interest in controlling what enters is at its peak. Opening the envelope to inspect its contents was a routine border search that needed no warrant or suspicion.
D: Yes, because the envelope was heavier than declared, which gave probable cause.
Answer: C
Authorities: Border search exception; United States v. Flores-Montano
Explanation: Rule: Routine searches at the border or its functional equivalent, including inspection of goods and packages entering the country, require no warrant, probable cause, or reasonable suspicion, because the government's interest in preventing the entry of contraband is at its zenith (Flores-Montano). An international mail facility is a functional equivalent of the border. Application: The envelope was entering the country and was inspected at the facility. Opening it is routine. The weight discrepancy adds nothing that the officer needed. Why not the others: A ignores the border exception, which applies to mail as to luggage. B imposes an inapplicable standard. D reaches the right result for the wrong reason; no suspicion was needed. Policy: The border exception lets the government screen contraband at the point of entry. Inspecting packages for physical contraband intrudes less on privacy than reading private correspondence, which has historically been treated with more caution.

## ccp-11-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consent given while in custody
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 11
Prompt: After a lawful arrest on a public street, Wystan Rhee, handcuffed in the back of a patrol car, is asked by an officer in a calm tone, "Mind if we look in your apartment? You can say no." Rhee, a 40-year-old paralegal, says, "Go ahead, there's nothing there." Officers find stolen laptops. Rhee argues his consent was involuntary because he was in custody. What result?
A: The consent is likely valid. Custody is a relevant factor but does not by itself make consent involuntary. Here the calm request, the express statement that he could refuse, his age and education, and the absence of threats or coercion support voluntariness under the totality of the circumstances.
B: The consent is invalid, because consent given while in custody is involuntary as a matter of law.
C: The consent is invalid, because a suspect in custody must receive Miranda warnings before being asked to consent.
D: The consent is valid, because a person in custody loses all expectation of privacy in his home.
Answer: A
Authorities: Schneckloth v. Bustamonte; United States v. Watson
Explanation: Rule: Voluntariness of consent is judged by the totality of the circumstances (Schneckloth). Custody alone does not make consent involuntary (Watson upheld consent given while in custody). Knowledge of the right to refuse is relevant but not required. Application: Rhee was in custody, but officers were calm, told him he could say no, and made no threats. He is an educated adult. These facts support voluntariness. Why not the others: B states a per se rule the Court rejected. C confuses consent to search, which is not a testimonial statement, with Miranda's protection against compelled self-incrimination. D overstates the effect of arrest. Policy: The totality test respects the possibility of genuine cooperation even in custody, while requiring courts to examine whether the pressures of custody coerced the decision.

## ccp-11-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Exigency to prevent a felony suspect's escape
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 12
Prompt: Detectives with probable cause to arrest Torin Ashe for an armed bank robbery, but no warrant, knock on his door to talk. Ashe opens it, sees their badges, slams the door, and they hear running footsteps and a back window being forced open. The detectives kick in the front door, chase him through the house, and catch him climbing out the back window. Was the entry lawful?
A: No, because the detectives created the exigency by knocking.
B: Likely yes. With probable cause for a serious, violent felony, the suspect's flight inside after seeing police, and the sounds of an escape underway created an urgent need to prevent his escape that made getting a warrant impracticable.
C: No, because officers who have time to knock also have time to get a warrant.
D: Yes, because any knock-and-talk gives officers authority to enter if the occupant closes the door.
Answer: B
Authorities: Warden v. Hayden; United States v. Santana; Payton v. New York
Explanation: Rule: Exigent circumstances, including the need to prevent the escape of a fleeing felon, can justify warrantless home entry when they make getting a warrant impracticable and probable cause exists (Hayden; Santana). Payton's warrant requirement yields to genuine exigency. Application: Knocking to talk was lawful conduct any citizen could engage in. Ashe's response, slamming the door, running, and forcing a window, created an urgent risk that an armed robbery suspect would escape. Why not the others: A is wrong because officers did nothing unlawful or threatening to create the exigency; the suspect did. C ignores that the exigency arose after the knock. D overstates the rule; simply closing the door to police is a person's right and does not create an exigency. Policy: The fleeing-felon exigency prevents dangerous suspects from escaping. It requires probable cause and real urgency so that exercising the right not to talk to police does not by itself license entry.

## ccp-11-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Frisk scope: opening a container too small for a weapon
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 13
Prompt: During a valid stop of Ffion Lark, who matches the description of an armed robber, an officer frisks her outer clothing and finds no weapon. She carries a small zippered coin purse about two inches square, which feels soft and flat. The officer opens it "to make sure" and finds three rocks of crack cocaine. Is the cocaine admissible?
A: Yes, because officers may open any container a suspect carries during a frisk.
B: Yes, because the robbery description justified a full search of everything she carried.
C: No. A frisk is limited to a search for weapons. A soft, flat, two-inch purse that could not reasonably hold a weapon falls outside the frisk's scope, so opening it was an unlawful search.
D: No, because officers may never touch items a suspect is carrying during a stop.
Answer: C
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Minnesota v. Dickerson
Explanation: Rule: A Terry frisk is a limited protective search for weapons, justified by officer safety, and confined to what is reasonably necessary to discover weapons (Terry). Dickerson held that going beyond what is needed to determine whether the suspect is armed exceeds Terry's scope. Application: The stop and frisk were justified by the description of an armed robber. A soft, flat, two-inch purse could not reasonably conceal a weapon, so opening it was not a weapons search. Why not the others: A has no limits. B confuses reasonable suspicion for a frisk with probable cause for a full search. D goes too far; officers may check items that could hold weapons or set them out of reach. Policy: Limiting frisks to weapons keeps a protective measure from becoming an evidence-gathering search on reasonable suspicion, which is the bargain at the heart of Terry.

## ccp-11-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Consensual encounter on a sidewalk
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 14
Prompt: A single officer approaches Gideon Ardley on a busy afternoon sidewalk and asks, in a conversational tone, if he has a minute. The officer asks for ID, looks at it for ten seconds, hands it back, and says, "You're free to go, but would you mind telling me where you're headed?" Ardley answers and then agrees to let the officer look in his shopping bag, which holds stolen merchandise. Was Ardley seized before he consented?
A: No. A single officer, a public setting, a conversational tone, prompt return of the ID, and an express statement that he was free to go would not make a reasonable person feel unable to leave. The encounter was consensual.
B: Yes, because any request for ID is a seizure.
C: Yes, because a reasonable person never feels free to walk away from a police officer.
D: Yes, because the officer had no reasonable suspicion when he approached.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Mendenhall; Florida v. Royer; Schneckloth v. Bustamonte
Explanation: Rule: A person is seized only when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave, considering factors such as the number of officers, display of weapons, physical touching, tone of voice, and retention of documents (Mendenhall). Asking questions and requesting ID are not by themselves seizures. Keeping a person's documents can turn an encounter into a seizure (Royer). Application: Every factor points toward a consensual encounter: one officer, a public sidewalk, a conversational tone, the ID quickly returned, and an express statement that he was free to go. Without a seizure, the consent was not the product of an unlawful detention. Why not the others: B contradicts the rule that asking for ID is not a seizure. C rejects the test entirely. D skips the threshold question; consensual encounters need no suspicion. Policy: Allowing consensual encounters lets police talk to the public and seek cooperation, while the free-to-leave test identifies when officers have crossed into detention.

## ccp-11-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Recorded jail calls to a family member after indictment
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 15
Prompt: After Marisol Kade is indicted for embezzlement and jailed, she calls her mother on the jail phone, where a recorded message warns that all calls are monitored. Her mother, acting on her own, asks about the case, and Kade describes how she hid the money. Prosecutors want to use the recording. Does it violate her Sixth Amendment right to counsel?
A: Yes, because any statement an indicted defendant makes outside counsel's presence is barred.
B: No. The right forbids the government from deliberately eliciting statements, directly or through agents. Here a routine recording system captured a call to her mother, who was not acting for the government, so there was no deliberate elicitation.
C: Yes, because recording calls is government action, which is the same as deliberate elicitation.
D: No, because the Sixth Amendment does not attach until trial.
Answer: B
Authorities: Massiah v. United States; Kuhlmann v. Wilson; Kirby v. Illinois
Explanation: Rule: After the right attaches, Massiah bars the government from deliberately eliciting incriminating statements without counsel, including through secret agents. Kuhlmann held that passive collection, without action designed to elicit statements, does not violate the right. Attachment occurs at formal adversary proceedings (Kirby). Application: The right attached at indictment, but the mother acted on her own, not as a government agent, and the jail's routine, announced recording did nothing to prompt the conversation. Why not the others: A overstates Massiah, which targets government elicitation. C equates recording with elicitation, which Kuhlmann rejects. D misplaces attachment. Policy: The Sixth Amendment protects against the state undermining the adversary process. It does not protect defendants from the consequences of their own voluntary choices to talk to family on lines they know are monitored.

## ccp-11-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Reading the arrest warrant aloud: interrogation?
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 16
Prompt: Arresting Leopold Hask at his office, an officer reads the arrest warrant aloud in a neutral tone: "This warrant charges you with theft of $80,000 from the Glenmara Credit Union." The officer says nothing else. Hask blurts out, "It was only $60,000, and I was going to pay it back." No warnings had been given. Is the statement admissible?
A: No, because reading charges to a suspect is always the functional equivalent of interrogation.
B: No, because any statement by a person in custody without warnings is inadmissible.
C: No, because the officer should have warned Hask before telling him anything.
D: Likely yes. Interrogation excludes words or actions normally attendant to arrest and custody. Neutrally reading the arrest warrant to inform the person of the charge is a standard part of arrest, not conduct police should know is reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response, so the blurted statement was volunteered.
Answer: D
Authorities: Rhode Island v. Innis; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: Miranda applies to custodial interrogation. Innis defines interrogation as express questioning or its functional equivalent, but excludes words or actions normally attendant to arrest and custody. Volunteered statements are not barred. Application: Telling an arrestee the charge, neutrally and without elaboration, is part of a normal arrest. Unlike staging evidence or making targeted emotional appeals, it is not designed to elicit a response. Hask's statement was volunteered. Why not the others: A overgeneralizes; Innis expressly excludes ordinary arrest procedures. B ignores that Miranda requires interrogation, not just custody. C misstates the rule; warnings are required before interrogation, not before every communication. Policy: Excluding routine arrest procedures from "interrogation" lets police perform necessary tasks without giving warnings first, while the functional-equivalent test still catches deliberate tactics.

Title: Exam 8, Question 17
Prompt: Officers chase a suspected gunman, Corin Ulle, through a public park next to an elementary school during recess. When they tackle him, his waistband holster is empty. A: Yes. Under the public safety exception, officers may ask questions reasonably prompted by an objective concern for public safety before giving warnings. An unaccounted-for gun in a park next to a school at recess is exactly such a danger.
B: No, because Ulle was in custody and the question was interrogation, and there are no exceptions.
C: The gun is admissible, but the statement is not.
D: Yes, but only because the officer personally feared for the children.

D makes the officer's subjective motive decisive, which Quarles rejected. Policy: The exception recognizes that rigid warnings could cost lives when a weapon is loose in a public place. ## ccp-11-q18
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Pre-custody silence used at trial
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 18
Prompt: Before any arrest or warnings, Dashiell Merrow voluntarily goes to the Glenmara station for an interview about a hit-and-run. He answers every question until a detective asks whether paint from his car would match the victim's bike. He says nothing, looks at the floor, and fidgets. Then the interview continues. At trial, the prosecutor argues that his silence on the paint question shows guilt. Is that permissible?
A: No, because using any silence against a defendant violates the privilege against self-incrimination.
B: No, because the detective should have warned him before asking about the paint.
C: No, because silence during a police interview is always an invocation of the right to remain silent.
D: Likely yes. Under the controlling plurality approach, a witness who wants the privilege's protection outside custody must expressly invoke it. Merrow simply stayed silent on one question during a voluntary, non-custodial interview, so the privilege did not bar the prosecution's argument.
Answer: D
Authorities: Salinas v. Texas; Berghuis v. Thompkins; Miranda v. Arizona
Explanation: Rule: In Salinas, the plurality held that a person who voluntarily answers police questions outside custody, without warnings, must expressly invoke the privilege to rely on it; merely remaining silent on a particular question does not invoke it. Berghuis similarly requires unambiguous invocation in custody. Warnings are required only for custodial interrogation. Application: Merrow was not in custody, came voluntarily, answered other questions, and did not say he was invoking his rights. Salinas permits using his silence. Why not the others: A overstates the privilege; it protects compelled testimony, and outside custody it must be claimed. B misstates Miranda's custody requirement. C contradicts Salinas and Berghuis. Policy: Requiring express invocation gives courts and police clear signals. Critics argue it expects ordinary people to know and use legal language, and it penalizes the natural human response of saying nothing.

## ccp-11-q19
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: In-court identification after an uncounseled lineup
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 19
Prompt: After Rhiannon Vale is indicted for armed robbery, police hold a lineup without notifying her lawyer, and the victim picks her. The court excludes the lineup identification. At trial, the prosecution asks the victim to identify Vale in court. The victim had faced the robber in good light for three minutes during the robbery and gave an accurate description, including a distinctive scar. Can the in-court identification come in?
A: No, because once a lineup is tainted, the witness may never identify the defendant at trial.
B: Yes, if the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence that the in-court identification rests on the victim's own observations during the robbery, independent of the uncounseled lineup. The long, well-lit view and accurate description, including the scar, support an independent origin.
C: Yes, automatically, because in-court identifications are never affected by pretrial procedures.
D: No, because the defense can never cross-examine an in-court identification.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Wade; Gilbert v. California
Explanation: Rule: Testimony about an uncounseled post-indictment lineup identification is excluded per se (Gilbert). An in-court identification is admissible only if the prosecution establishes by clear and convincing evidence that it has an independent source in the witness's observations at the time of the crime (Wade). Courts consider the prior opportunity to observe, any discrepancy between the description and the defendant, any earlier misidentification, and the time elapsed. Application: Three minutes of face-to-face observation in good light and an accurate description including a distinctive scar strongly suggest an independent memory. Why not the others: A and D overstate the effect of the taint. C ignores Wade's independent-source requirement. Policy: The independent-source test excludes identifications that are products of the uncounseled lineup while preserving reliable memories formed during the crime, so the victim's own memory is not discarded because of police error.

## ccp-11-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Attenuation: release and voluntary return to confess
Set: CCP-11
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Glenmara
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 8, Question 20
Prompt: Police arrest Ewan Thorne without probable cause and question him for an hour about a warehouse burglary. He says nothing incriminating and is released. Six days later, after consulting a lawyer, Thorne walks into the station on his own, receives warnings, waives, and confesses. Is the confession suppressible as fruit of the illegal arrest?
A: Yes, because a confession that follows an illegal arrest is always fruit of the poisonous tree.
B: Yes, because the warnings did not cure the illegal arrest.
C: No. The connection between the illegal arrest and the confession has become so attenuated that the taint has dissipated. Six days passed, he was released and consulted counsel in between, and he returned voluntarily, all of which break the causal chain.
D: No, because the confession was voluntary under due process, and voluntariness alone ends the inquiry.
Answer: C
Authorities: Wong Sun v. United States; Brown v. Illinois; Utah v. Strieff
Explanation: Rule: Evidence derived from an illegality is admissible if the connection has become so attenuated as to dissipate the taint. Brown's factors are temporal proximity, intervening circumstances, and the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct, and warnings alone are not enough. In Wong Sun, a defendant who was released, then returned voluntarily several days later to make a statement, was found attenuated. Application: Six days, release, consultation with counsel, and a voluntary return are strong intervening circumstances, as in Wong Sun. Why not the others: A states a per se rule Wong Sun rejected. B is true as far as it goes, but the confession here does not rely on the warnings alone. D conflates due process voluntariness with Fourth Amendment attenuation; Brown requires both. Policy: Attenuation recognizes that at some point a person's own free choice, not the police illegality, is the real cause of the evidence. Exclusion past that point deters little and costs much.
