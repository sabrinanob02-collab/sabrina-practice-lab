## tm-04-q01
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Copying evidence and acquired distinctiveness
Set: TM-04

Theme: Old Line Design Cooperative
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: A competitor deliberately copies the ribbed surface of a popular water bottle because testing shows buyers prefer the grip. The seller offers no survey, customer testimony, or advertising that teaches buyers to treat the ribs as a source cue. What is the strongest assessment of the copying evidence?
A: Copying for the feature's appeal or utility does not by itself establish acquired distinctiveness; intent to deceive consumers about source would be more probative.
B: Any intentional copying conclusively proves that consumers identify the feature with one source.
C: Copying is irrelevant to acquired distinctiveness under all circumstances.
D: Copying automatically makes the feature fanciful.
Answer: A
Authorities: TBL Licensing copying-intent analysis
Explanation: Rule. Copying can support acquired distinctiveness when it reflects an intent to deceive as to source, but copying an attractive or useful feature does not alone show consumer source association. Application. The competitor copied the ribs for grip preference, and the record lacks feature-focused consumer evidence. Why the other choices fail. B makes ambiguous evidence conclusive, C ignores potentially probative deceptive intent, and D misapplies a verbal-mark category to product design.

## tm-04-q02
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition

Theme: Old Line Design Cooperative
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 2
Prompt: Eighty percent of relevant buyers say the embossed compass symbol on a tool comes from the same company each time, but only twelve percent can name that company. The record otherwise shows consistent source association. Which conclusion is best?
A: The symbol cannot identify source unless a majority can state the producer's legal name.
B: The symbol is generic because many buyers cannot name the producer.
C: The symbol can identify source only if the producer manufactures every unit itself.
D: Buyers may understand a designation as pointing to a single, even anonymous, source without knowing that source's name.
Answer: D
Authorities: Anonymous-source principle; TBL Licensing and Cartier
Explanation: Rule. Trademark significance requires association with a single source, not the consumer's ability to name that source; licensed or otherwise controlled production can still preserve source significance. Application. The strong same-source response is probative even though most buyers cannot identify the company by name. Why the other choices fail. A imposes a naming requirement, B confuses anonymity with genericness, and C equates source with a single physical manufacturer.

## tm-04-q03
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Trade-dress claim specificity
Set: TM-04
Coverage: Assigned Beebe printed pp. 1-67, 71-108, and 131-146
Theme: Old Line Design Cooperative
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 3
Prompt: An audio-equipment company claims rights in the "overall appearance" of a wall speaker but alternately points to the grille pattern, corner brackets, indicator lights, frame color, mounting plate, and packaging. Its survey displays the complete speaker beside the company name. What is the most serious proof problem?
A: The company has not consistently defined the claimed combination or shown that consumers associate those particular features, rather than the house name or product as a whole, with one source.
B: A claimant may protect only one physical feature at a time.
C: Consumer evidence is never relevant to product appearance.
D: The use of a company name necessarily destroys all rights in accompanying design features.
Answer: A
Authorities: TBL Licensing claim-definition and claim-specific acquired-distinctiveness analysis
Explanation: Rule. A trade-dress claimant must articulate the specific claimed combination and prove source significance in those features, not merely recognition of the complete branded product. Application. The shifting definition and name-visible survey prevent the proof from matching the asserted claim. Why the other choices fail. B wrongly forbids combination claims, C rejects relevant consumer evidence, and D turns possible survey contamination into a categorical loss of rights.
