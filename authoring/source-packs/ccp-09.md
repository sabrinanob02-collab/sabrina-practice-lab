## ccp-09-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Drug-dog sniff at an apartment door in a shared hallway
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 1
Prompt: Kestrel Heights officers walk a trained drug-detection dog down the locked interior hallway of a 40-unit apartment building, which they entered when a resident held the door. The dog sniffs along the seam of Delphine Marr's door and alerts. Officers use the alert to get a warrant. Marr argues the sniff was a search. Which statement is most accurate?
A: It was clearly not a search, because a dog sniff reveals only contraband, and that rule applies everywhere.
B: The question is unsettled. A sniff of luggage in a public place is not a search because it reveals only contraband, and bringing a dog onto a house's front porch to sniff is a search. Marr's argument draws on the home's special protection and on the claim that the hallway right outside her door works like her porch. The government relies on her lack of a right to exclude other residents from the hallway.
C: It was clearly a search, because any investigative use of a dog inside a residential building is a search.
D: It was not a search, because renters have no Fourth Amendment interest in their apartment doors.
Answer: B
Authorities: United States v. Place; Florida v. Jardines; Kyllo v. United States; United States v. Dunn
Explanation: Rule: Place held that a canine sniff of luggage in a public place is not a search because it reveals only the presence or absence of contraband. Jardines held that bringing a drug dog onto the curtilage of a house (the front porch) to investigate exceeds the implied license and is a search. Kyllo protects information about the interior of the home. Application: An apartment hallway falls between these cases. Marr cannot exclude other residents, which favors the government. But the area right outside her door is closely tied to her home, and the sniff detects what is inside it, which favors her. Lower courts are split. Why not the others: A ignores Jardines. C is overbroad. D contradicts the rule that a rented home receives full protection. Policy: The government argues that apartment hallways are shared spaces and that dogs detect only contraband. The defense argues that renters, who are disproportionately less wealthy, should not receive less protection for their homes than owners of houses with porches.

## ccp-09-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Content vs. addressing information held by a provider
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 2
Prompt: Without a warrant, investigators get two things from Rafferty Lowe's email provider: (1) a list of the addresses he emailed over a month, with dates and times, and (2) the full text of those emails. Which statement best reflects the assigned doctrine?
A: The addressing list resembles the numbers a caller conveys to the phone company and is likely unprotected. The content of the messages is more like the private conversations protected in the phone-booth case, so Lowe has a strong argument that getting it without a warrant was a search, even though a third party stored it.
B: Both are unprotected, because Lowe voluntarily gave the provider everything.
C: Both are protected, because the location-records case requires a warrant for all digital data.
D: Neither can be challenged, because the provider, not Lowe, owns its servers.
Answer: A
Authorities: Katz v. United States; Smith v. Maryland; Carpenter v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Katz protected the content of a phone call made from a booth. Smith held there is no legitimate expectation of privacy in dialed phone numbers, which are conveyed to the phone company to route calls and reveal no content. Carpenter cautioned against mechanically extending the third-party doctrine to new technology. Application: Email addresses and timestamps work like dialed numbers: routing information given to a provider. The text of the messages is the modern equivalent of the conversation in Katz. Lower courts have widely held that email content is protected even when stored by a provider, though the Supreme Court has not ruled directly. Why not the others: B applies Smith to content, which the Court has not done. C overreads Carpenter's narrow holding. D confuses property in servers with privacy in communications. Policy: Protecting content preserves private communication, which is central to free expression and association, while allowing routing information to remain available keeps investigative tools that do not reveal what people say.

## ccp-09-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: A passenger's standing in his own closed bag
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 3
Prompt: Ilan Brisco is a passenger in a friend's car. His zipped duffel bag, holding his clothes, sits in the trunk. Officers unlawfully search the car, opening the glove box, where they find a gun, and Ilan's duffel, where they find stolen watches. Ilan, who has no ownership or possessory interest in the car, moves to suppress both items. What result?
A: He can challenge both, because anyone in a car can challenge a search of it.
B: He can challenge neither, because a passenger never has standing in a car he does not own.
C: He can challenge both, because the search was aimed at him.
D: He likely cannot challenge the glove box search, in which he had no interest, but he can challenge the search of his own closed duffel bag. A person keeps a legitimate expectation of privacy in his own closed container even when it is inside someone else's car.
Answer: D
Authorities: Rakas v. Illinois; Rawlings v. Kentucky
Explanation: Rule: Rakas held that passengers with no property or possessory interest in a car, and no interest in the items seized, could not challenge a search of the glove box and the area under the seat. Fourth Amendment rights are personal and depend on the defendant's own legitimate expectation of privacy. Rawlings shows that owning an item does not create privacy in someone else's container, but the reverse case, a person's own closed container, carries its own expectation. Application: Ilan had no interest in the glove box, so the gun falls under Rakas. His duffel, holding his clothes, is his own closed container, and nothing suggests he gave up privacy in it by placing it in a friend's trunk. Why not the others: A and C restate theories Rakas rejected (presence-based and target standing). B is too categorical; a passenger can have an interest in his own belongings. Policy: Tying standing to personal interests limits exclusion to people whose own privacy was invaded, while ensuring that riding in someone else's car does not strip people of protection for their own belongings.

## ccp-09-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Stale information and probable cause
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 4
Prompt: A proven informant tells police that six weeks ago she saw Corwin Pell sell one gram of cocaine from his apartment. Nothing more recent is known, and no investigation has suggested ongoing dealing. Police get a warrant to search the apartment for cocaine. Was there probable cause when the warrant issued?
A: Yes, because a reliable informant's firsthand observation always supports probable cause.
B: Yes, because probable cause, once established, lasts until the evidence is found.
C: Probably not. Probable cause to search requires a fair probability that evidence will be found in the place now. A single small sale six weeks earlier, with no sign of ongoing activity, makes it unlikely the cocaine is still there. Evidence of a continuing operation would change the analysis.
D: No, because an informant's information becomes unusable after one week.
Answer: C
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Probable cause nexus requirement
Explanation: Rule: Probable cause to search requires a fair probability that evidence will be found in the place to be searched, and the nexus must exist when the warrant issues (Gates). Timing matters: information can go stale depending on the nature of the crime, the item sought, and whether the activity is continuing. Application: Small amounts of cocaine are sold or used quickly. A single sale six weeks ago, with no sign of ongoing dealing, does not support a fair probability of drugs there now. A continuing operation, such as repeated sales or a stash location, would keep older information fresh. Why not the others: A ignores timing. B is wrong because probable cause can dissipate. D invents a fixed time limit; staleness is assessed case by case. Policy: Staleness keeps warrants tied to present reality, preventing searches based on old suspicion. A flexible rule allows long-running operations to be investigated without losing evidence because of an arbitrary deadline.

## ccp-09-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Questioning detained occupants during a warrant search
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 5
Prompt: During a lawful two-hour search of a house for stolen firearms, officers detain the occupant, Saffi Orlen, in handcuffs in the garage. While she is held, and without extending the detention, an officer asks for her name, date of birth, and immigration status. She has no connection to the guns, and officers have no suspicion about her immigration status. Did the questioning make the detention unreasonable?
A: Yes, because questioning about unrelated matters during a detention requires independent reasonable suspicion.
B: Yes, because handcuffing someone for two hours during a search is always unreasonable.
C: Yes, because officers may never ask questions of people detained during a warrant search.
D: No. Officers may detain occupants during a warrant search, using reasonable force including handcuffs where safety warrants. Asking questions is not a separate seizure and needs no independent suspicion, as long as it does not extend the detention.
Answer: D
Authorities: Muehler v. Mena; Michigan v. Summers
Explanation: Rule: Summers authorizes detaining occupants during a warrant search. Muehler upheld handcuffing an occupant for the duration of a two-to-three-hour search for weapons connected to a gang, and held that questioning her about immigration status required no independent suspicion because mere police questioning is not a seizure and did not prolong the detention. Application: The search was for firearms, so handcuffs were justified for safety. The questions did not extend the detention. Muehler controls. Why not the others: A states a rule Muehler rejected. B ignores Muehler's approval of handcuffing during a lengthy weapons search. C has no basis. Policy: Muehler lets police manage dangerous searches and use the time efficiently. Critics argue that it allows officers to use detentions tied to one investigation to explore unrelated matters, such as immigration status, without any suspicion.

## ccp-09-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Corroborating a predictive anonymous tip
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 6
Prompt: An anonymous message to a police tip line says that at 4 p.m. on Thursday, Vesna Hollow will leave 12 Linden Court in a white panel van, drive to the Kestrel Storage facility, unload two black suitcases into unit 214, and return with them, and that the suitcases will hold stolen jewelry. Officers watch every detail happen exactly as predicted, then stop the van as it leaves the facility and search the suitcases. Was there probable cause?
A: No, because anonymous tips can never establish probable cause.
B: Yes. Although the tipster's reliability was unknown, accurate predictions of Hollow's future behavior, verified in detail by officers, give reason to believe the tipster had inside knowledge. Under the totality of the circumstances, that creates a fair probability the suitcases hold contraband.
C: No, because the officers corroborated only innocent conduct.
D: Yes, because any tip is enough once police see the suspect do anything at all.
Answer: B
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Florida v. J.L.; Carroll v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under Gates, an anonymous tip can establish probable cause when independent police work corroborates significant details, especially predictions of future behavior not easily known to outsiders, which shows the tipster's inside knowledge. J.L. found a bare tip without predictive detail insufficient for even reasonable suspicion. The automobile exception permits searching containers in a car when there is probable cause (Carroll). Application: The tipster predicted the time, vehicle, destination, unit number, and specific actions, all of which officers verified. Like the Gates letter, the accuracy about the future supports trusting the claim about the contents. Why not the others: A contradicts Gates. C misreads Gates, which held that corroborating innocent but predictive details can establish probable cause. D ignores the need for detailed, predictive corroboration. Policy: Gates lets police act on credible information from sources who cannot be named, while corroboration requirements guard against acting on rumors or harassment.

## ccp-09-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Plain view does not authorize warrantless entry
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 7
Prompt: Walking on a public sidewalk at noon, an officer looks through Hamish Corrigan's uncovered front window and clearly sees a dozen tall marijuana plants under grow lights in his living room, far more than the state's legal limit. No one appears to be home, and nothing suggests the plants will be moved or destroyed. The officer opens the unlocked front door, walks in, and seizes the plants. Is the seizure valid under plain view?
A: Yes, because the officer saw contraband in plain view from a lawful vantage point.
B: Yes, because illegal plants are contraband, which may be seized anywhere it is seen.
C: No. Plain view requires lawful access to the object, not just a lawful vantage point. Seeing contraband through a window provides probable cause for a warrant but does not, without an exigency, justify entering a home to seize it.
D: No, because looking through the window was itself a search.
Answer: C
Authorities: Horton v. California; Payton v. New York; California v. Ciraolo
Explanation: Rule: Plain view requires that the officer be lawfully at the vantage point, that the incriminating character be immediately apparent, and that the officer have a lawful right of access to the object itself (Horton). Payton protects the home against warrantless entry absent exigency. Looking from a public vantage point is not a search (Ciraolo). Application: The officer lawfully saw the plants from the sidewalk, and their incriminating nature was apparent. But nothing gave him a right to enter the home: no warrant, no consent, and no exigency. The observation supports a warrant, not an entry. Why not the others: A and B skip the lawful-access requirement. D is wrong because observation from a public sidewalk through an uncovered window is not a search. Policy: Separating observation from entry protects the home's threshold, the line Payton draws, while still letting police use what they see to obtain a warrant quickly.

## ccp-09-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Securing a phone against remote wiping without searching it
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 8
Prompt: After a lawful arrest for an investment-fraud scheme, an officer seizes Ottoline Breck's phone. Breck says her partner "will wipe everything." The officer puts the phone into a signal-blocking bag and, at the station, scrolls through her messages without a warrant, saying he feared remote wiping. Which statement is correct?
A: Seizing the phone and putting it in a signal-blocking bag to prevent remote wiping was proper. But once the phone was secured, the threat of wiping was neutralized, so searching its data still required a warrant.
B: The search was valid, because the risk of remote wiping always justifies a warrantless search of a phone.
C: Seizing the phone itself was unlawful, because officers need a warrant even to take a phone from an arrestee.
D: The search was valid, because the phone was found on the arrestee's person during a lawful arrest.
Answer: A
Authorities: Riley v. California; United States v. Robinson
Explanation: Rule: Riley held that police generally need a warrant to search digital data on a phone seized incident to arrest. Riley allowed officers to seize and secure the phone to prevent destruction of evidence while seeking a warrant, and specifically noted that remote wiping can be countered by disconnecting the phone from the network or using signal-blocking bags. Case-specific exigencies may still justify warrantless searches in genuine emergencies. Application: The officer properly secured the phone. Once it was in the bag, the wiping risk was addressed, so no exigency justified searching it. A warrant was required. Why not the others: B states the general wiping justification Riley rejected. C is wrong because seizing the phone pending a warrant is permitted. D applies Robinson's rule for physical items, which Riley refused to extend to digital data. Policy: Riley reconciles evidence preservation with privacy: officers can freeze the evidence while a neutral magistrate decides whether its contents may be searched.

## ccp-09-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Scope of a car search when there is probable cause for the whole car
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 9
Prompt: Officers have probable cause to believe that stolen gold coins are somewhere in Florian Dusk's SUV, but not in any particular spot. Without a warrant, they search the cabin, the cargo area, and a locked metal toolbox in the back, which they pry open and find the coins. Is the toolbox search valid?
A: Yes. When officers have probable cause to search an entire vehicle, they may search every part of it and every container inside that could hold the object of the search, including a locked toolbox.
B: No, because locked containers in cars always require a warrant.
C: No, because probable cause for the vehicle does not extend to containers within it.
D: Yes, but only because Dusk had been arrested.
Answer: A
Authorities: United States v. Ross; California v. Acevedo; Carroll v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under Ross, when officers have probable cause to search a lawfully stopped vehicle, they may search every part of it and its contents that may conceal the object of the search, as a magistrate could authorize by warrant. Acevedo confirmed one rule for containers in cars. Application: The probable cause covered the whole SUV and concerned coins, which could fit in the toolbox. Being locked does not take the toolbox outside the search; a warrant for the vehicle would also cover it. Why not the others: B states a rule Ross rejected. C contradicts Ross. D adds an arrest requirement the automobile exception does not have. Policy: Ross matches the scope of warrantless vehicle searches to what a warrant could authorize, so the exception neither expands nor shrinks what probable cause supports. Critics argue it gives officers broad power to open closed containers holding private items without judicial review.

## ccp-09-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Area-wide administrative warrants
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 10
Prompt: After fires in several old Kestrel Heights rowhouses, the fire marshal adopts a plan to inspect every rowhouse in a 12-block district built before 1940, because of their age and wiring. Ines Morrow refuses entry. The marshal gets an inspection warrant based solely on the area plan, with no suspicion about Morrow's house in particular. Is the warrant valid?
A: No, because a warrant to enter a home requires probable cause of a violation in that specific home.
B: No, because administrative inspections may never involve homes.
C: Yes, and the marshal did not need a warrant even after Morrow refused.
D: Yes. Administrative warrants may rest on reasonable legislative or administrative standards, such as a building's age, type, and area-wide conditions, rather than individualized suspicion. Because Morrow refused, a warrant was required, and this one rests on such standards.
Answer: D
Authorities: Camara v. Municipal Court
Explanation: Rule: Camara held that administrative inspections of homes are significant intrusions requiring a warrant if consent is refused, but that the probable cause for such a warrant may rest on reasonable legislative or administrative standards, such as the passage of time, the nature of the building, or the condition of the area, without individualized suspicion. Application: The age-based, area-wide plan fits Camara. Morrow's refusal triggered the warrant requirement, and the marshal complied. Why not the others: A demands criminal-style probable cause, which Camara rejected for administrative inspections. B contradicts Camara, which involved a residence. C ignores that refusal requires a warrant. Policy: Camara keeps a magistrate involved in home inspections while recognizing that public-safety inspections cannot depend on knowing about particular violations in advance, since many hazards are invisible from outside.

## ccp-09-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Apparent authority undermined by the consenter's own words
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 11
Prompt: Rosamund Teel brings officers to her ex-boyfriend Desmond Ivers's apartment, saying he has stolen her laptop. She unlocks the door with a key and says, "I lived here until last month, but I moved out and just kept the key." She tells them to go in. Officers enter and find stolen electronics in plain view. Is the entry valid on Teel's consent?
A: Yes, because she had a key and had lived there, which gives her common authority.
B: No. Apparent authority requires that the facts available would lead a reasonable officer to believe the consenter had authority. Teel told them she had moved out, which defeats any reasonable belief in her current authority, and the officers could not rely on the key alone.
C: Yes, because the officers believed in good faith that she could consent.
D: No, because only a leaseholder can ever consent to entry.
Answer: B
Authorities: Illinois v. Rodriguez; United States v. Matlock
Explanation: Rule: A third party with common authority, meaning mutual use and joint access or control, may consent (Matlock). Rodriguez held that entry is valid if officers reasonably, though mistakenly, believe the consenter has authority, judged objectively from the facts available. Officers may not proceed when the facts make authority doubtful. Application: In Rodriguez, the woman called it "our" apartment and gave no hint she had moved out. Here Teel said directly that she had moved out and simply kept a key. No reasonable officer could think she still had authority. Why not the others: A treats a key and past residence as current authority. C substitutes subjective good faith for the objective test. D overstates the rule; non-leaseholders can have common authority. Policy: Apparent authority accepts reasonable mistakes to make consent workable, but the objective standard keeps police from ignoring obvious warning signs, which protects people from consent given by those with no current connection to their homes.

## ccp-09-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Emergency entry for a hazardous chemical lab, and returning later
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 12
Prompt: Neighbors report choking fumes from Lyle Ardent's duplex, and responding officers smell a strong chemical odor consistent with a methamphetamine lab, which can explode. They force entry, evacuate a child, and turn off a heating element under a flask, seeing lab equipment as they do. The next day, with the danger long gone, officers return without a warrant to collect the equipment and search the rest of the house. Which ruling is correct?
A: Both entries were lawful, because once an emergency justifies entry, police may return at any time to finish their investigation.
B: Neither entry was lawful, because officers must always get a warrant before entering a home.
C: The first entry was lawful, because an objectively reasonable belief in imminent danger to people allowed officers to enter and take steps needed to neutralize it, and items seen then were in plain view. The next-day return, after the danger was over, required a warrant.
D: The first entry was lawful only if officers had a warrant within the hour.
Answer: C
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Horton v. California; Payton v. New York
Explanation: Rule: An exigency justifies a warrantless entry only while it lasts. The scope must be limited to the emergency, and once it ends officers must leave unless another exception or a warrant applies (Fisher; Payton). Items in plain view during the lawful emergency entry may be noted and seized (Horton). Application: A volatile lab with a child inside is a clear emergency, and evacuating the child and cutting the heat are within its scope. The lab equipment was seen during that entry. A return the next day after the danger had passed is a new entry with no exigency. Why not the others: A ignores that the exigency ends. B ignores the exigency exception. D invents a timing requirement. Policy: Emergencies justify immediate action because lives are at stake. Requiring a warrant once the emergency is over keeps the exception tied to necessity rather than convenience.

## ccp-09-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Frisking a passenger during a lawful traffic stop
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 13
Prompt: An officer lawfully stops a car for an expired registration. The back-seat passenger, Rourke Vallen, wears a jacket with the colors of a gang known for armed robberies, has a police-scanner app open on his phone, and tells the officer he recently served time for burglary. The officer orders him out and frisks him, finding a revolver. Vallen argues the officer had no suspicion that he had committed any crime. Is the frisk valid?
A: No, because a passenger may be frisked only if the officer suspects him of a crime.
B: No, because the stop concerned only the car's registration, which is not a crime.
C: Yes. During a lawful traffic stop, an officer may order passengers out and may frisk a passenger whom the officer reasonably suspects is armed and dangerous, without separate suspicion that the passenger committed a crime.
D: Yes, because every occupant of a stopped car may be frisked automatically.
Answer: C
Authorities: Arizona v. Johnson; Pennsylvania v. Mimms; Terry v. Ohio
Explanation: Rule: During a lawful traffic stop, officers may order the driver (Mimms) and passengers out of the car. Under Johnson, an officer may frisk a passenger if the officer reasonably suspects the passenger is armed and dangerous. The lawful stop itself supplies the first Terry requirement, so no separate suspicion of a crime by the passenger is needed. Application: The gang affiliation with armed robbery, the scanner app, and the recent burglary record together support reasonable suspicion of being armed and dangerous. Why not the others: A states the argument Johnson rejected. B ignores that a lawful stop for any violation satisfies the first prong. D eliminates the armed-and-dangerous requirement. Policy: Traffic stops can be dangerous, and Johnson gives officers tools to protect themselves while requiring individualized suspicion before frisking anyone, so that riding in a car with someone does not subject a person to a pat-down.

## ccp-09-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Protective sweep during a consensual entry
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 14
Prompt: Juno Halvard invites two detectives into her living room to talk about a neighbor's burglary. Nothing suggests danger: the house is quiet, Juno is calm, and no one else is known to be there. While one detective talks with her, the other walks through every bedroom "for safety" and sees stolen guns in an open closet. Is the sweep valid?
A: Yes, because officers lawfully inside a home may always do a protective sweep.
B: Likely not. Whether protective sweeps are allowed outside the arrest setting has not been decided by the Supreme Court, and many lower courts allow them when officers are lawfully present and have specific facts suggesting danger. But here officers had no articulable facts pointing to a dangerous person, so a sweep of the bedrooms was not justified under any approach.
C: Yes, because Juno's consent to enter the living room covered the whole house.
D: Yes, because the guns were in plain view in an open closet, and finding them there shows the sweep was justified.
Answer: B
Authorities: Maryland v. Buie
Explanation: Rule: Buie allowed a protective sweep incident to an in-home arrest: without suspicion for spaces immediately next to the arrest, and with articulable facts of danger for other areas. The Court has not decided whether sweeps are permitted outside arrests, and lower courts have extended Buie to other lawful presence, such as consent entries, typically requiring articulable facts that someone dangerous is present. Application: Even under the broadest approach, there were no facts suggesting danger. A walk through every bedroom "for safety" has no articulable basis. Why not the others: A ignores the articulable-facts requirement. C misreads the scope of consent, which was to enter the living room for a conversation. D reasons backward. Plain view requires that officers already be lawfully in the place where they see the item, so what a sweep finds cannot justify the sweep. Policy: Sweeps protect officers in dangerous situations. Requiring articulable facts prevents consensual visits from becoming pretexts for walking through a home.

## ccp-09-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Attachment at the first appearance before a magistrate
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 15
Prompt: Quinlan Fosse is arrested without a warrant for aggravated assault, brought before a magistrate the next morning, told of the charge, and given bail. No prosecutor has been involved, and no indictment has been filed. A week later police send an informant to ask Fosse about the assault, and Fosse admits it. Were his Sixth Amendment rights violated?
A: Likely yes. The right to counsel attaches at the first formal proceeding where the accused learns the charge against him and his liberty is subject to restriction, even if no prosecutor took part. After that, deliberate elicitation by a police agent violates the right.
B: No, because the right does not attach until indictment.
C: No, because the informant's questioning was not custodial.
D: No, because the right attaches only once a prosecutor formally files charges.
Answer: A
Authorities: Rothgery v. Gillespie County; Kirby v. Illinois; Massiah v. United States
Explanation: Rule: The Sixth Amendment right attaches at the start of adversary judicial proceedings, through formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information, or arraignment (Kirby). Rothgery held that a criminal defendant's initial appearance before a judicial officer, where he learns the charge and his liberty is subject to restriction, marks attachment, whether or not a prosecutor is aware of it. Once attached, Massiah forbids deliberate elicitation by government agents. Application: Fosse's first appearance triggered attachment. The informant then deliberately elicited statements about the charged offense. Why not the others: B and D set attachment too late. C confuses the Fifth Amendment's custody requirement with the Sixth Amendment's attachment trigger. Policy: Rothgery places attachment where the state has committed itself to prosecution and the accused faces its organized power, so the government cannot delay formal steps to avoid the right to counsel.

## ccp-09-q16
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I

Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights

Explanation: Rule: The Court has never required a precise formulation of the warnings. The question is whether they reasonably convey the rights required by Miranda (Prysock; Duckworth). Explanation: Rule: Due process forbids identification procedures that are unnecessarily suggestive and create a substantial likelihood of misidentification, judged by the totality of the circumstances and the reliability factors (Biggers; Manson). ## ccp-09-q20
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Independent source after an illegal entry
Set: CCP-09
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Kestrel Heights
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 6, Question 20
Prompt: Agents watching a Kestrel Heights warehouse have strong probable cause from wiretaps and surveillance that it holds stolen cigarettes. Before a warrant arrives, they unlawfully enter and see the cartons, then leave and secure the outside. Their warrant application relies only on the wiretap and surveillance evidence and does not mention the entry, and they had begun preparing it before the entry. The warrant issues, and they seize the cartons. Are the cartons admissible?
A: No, because anything seen during an illegal entry must be suppressed forever.
B: No, because the agents would never have sought a warrant if they had not first confirmed the cartons were there.
C: Yes, under the independent source doctrine, if the warrant was supported by information independent of the illegal entry and the agents' decision to seek it was not prompted by what they saw inside.
D: Yes, because of attenuation, since enough time passed between the entry and the warrant.
Answer: C
Authorities: Segura v. United States; Murray v. United States; Nix v. Williams
Explanation: Rule: The independent source doctrine admits evidence discovered through a lawful source independent of the illegality. In Murray, the Court allowed evidence first seen during an illegal entry and later seized under a valid warrant, if the warrant rested on independent information and the decision to seek it was not prompted by the illegal entry. Segura similarly allowed evidence found under a warrant based on independent information after an illegal entry and securing of the premises. Application: The application relied only on the wiretap and surveillance evidence, and preparation began before the entry, which shows the decision to seek the warrant was independent. Both Murray conditions appear to be met. Why not the others: A ignores the doctrine. B would be correct only if the entry prompted the decision, which the facts contradict. D misapplies attenuation, which concerns whether taint has dissipated, not whether there is a separate lawful source. Policy: The independent source doctrine puts police in the same position they would have been in without the violation, not a worse one. Critics worry that it lets officers confirm suspicions illegally before deciding whether to bother with a warrant, which is why the second Murray condition matters.
