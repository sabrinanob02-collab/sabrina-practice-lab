## tm-05-q01
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Competitive silence and genericness
Set: TM-05
Coverage: Class 4 through 2026-08-31; assigned Beebe pp. 89-108 and 131-146
Theme: Patapsco Product Studio
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 1
Prompt: A seller claims STACK TRAYS for modular seedling trays and repeatedly threatens small nurseries that use the phrase to describe stackable trays. Most stop rather than fund litigation. The seller argues that the resulting scarcity of competing uses proves the public treats the phrase as its brand. Which assessment is strongest?
A: The scarcity has limited probative value because aggressive enforcement may explain competitors' silence without showing that consumers understand the term primarily as a source identifier.
B: The scarcity conclusively establishes source significance because competitors' business decisions bind consumers.
C: The scarcity makes the individual meanings of the two words irrelevant to the inquiry.
D: The scarcity automatically converts the designation into a fanciful mark.
Answer: A
Authorities: Snyder's-Lance genericness evidence analysis; assigned Beebe pp. 99, 107-108
Explanation: Rule. Competitive silence may carry little weight when enforcement threats plausibly caused it; the ultimate question remains how the relevant public primarily understands the designation as a whole. Application. The small competitors stopped because litigation was costly, not because they conceded or demonstrated consumer source perception. Why the other choices fail. B makes private settlements conclusive, C discards relevant whole-mark and component evidence, and D treats marketplace suppression as word coinage.

## tm-05-q02

Title: Question 2
Prompt: A company submits 1,200 online references to TRAVEL NESTS for collapsible pet carriers. Most are duplicate press releases, retailer listings for the company's product, financial reports about its sales, or posts repeating its advertising. A smaller set of independent travel guides and comparison articles uses the same words as the ordinary name of a carrier category. How should a court most sensibly evaluate this record?
A: Count every reference equally because the larger numerical total necessarily controls consumer perception.
B: Disregard all media evidence because only dictionaries can establish genericness.
C: Treat retailer listings and sales reports as conclusive proof that the designation is a brand.
D: Weigh the quality and context of the references; commercial-success materials may show association or secondary meaning while independent category uses more directly illuminate generic understanding.
Answer: D
Authorities: Snyder's-Lance media-reference and evidence-weight analysis; assigned Beebe pp. 96-107
Explanation: Rule. Genericness evidence is weighed, not merely counted. Materials showing that one company successfully sells a product may speak more to commercial association than to whether the public primarily understands the term as the product's common name, while contextual independent uses can be more probative. Application. The large pile is dominated by duplicates and seller-driven commerce, whereas the recipes and comparisons use the designation categorically. Why the other choices fail. A substitutes volume for probative value, B wrongly makes dictionaries exclusive, and C conflates market success with non-generic status.

## tm-05-q03
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Acquired distinctiveness of color
Set: TM-05
Coverage: Class 4 through 2026-08-31; assigned Beebe pp. 89-108 and 131-146
Theme: Patapsco Product Studio
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 3
Prompt: A new maker applies one unusual shade of coral to the entire surface of its ceramic desktop cable trays. Many equally practical colors remain available, and the shade does not affect cost, quality, or operation. The maker begins selling the trays this week and offers no evidence that buyers yet associate the color with a single source. What is the strongest conclusion?
A: The color is not barred merely because it is color, and it appears nonfunctional, but color alone requires acquired source significance that the present record does not show.
B: The color is inherently distinctive because competitors have many alternatives.
C: The color is protectable immediately whenever it performs no utilitarian function.
D: Color alone can never identify source, even after buyers learn to rely on it.
Answer: A
Authorities: Qualitex acquired-distinctiveness and functionality analysis; assigned Beebe pp. 139-146
Explanation: Rule. Color alone can serve as a mark, but it does not automatically signal source and therefore requires acquired distinctiveness; it also must be nonfunctional. Application. Alternative colors and the absence of cost, quality, or operational effects support nonfunctionality, but one week of use and no consumer evidence fail to establish source significance. Why the other choices fail. B mistakes nonfunctionality for inherent distinctiveness, C omits the acquired-distinctiveness requirement, and D imposes the categorical prohibition rejected in Qualitex.
