Set: TM-03

Theme: Brightwater Makers District
Difficulty: hard

Title: Question 1
Prompt: A protective-clothing company uses HEAT-SHIELD for a liner that shields industrial workers from heat. Dictionaries connect both words directly to the liner's function, competitors use similar wording, and rivals reasonably need the terms to describe their products. The best classification is:
A: Fanciful, because the hyphen creates a new word.
B: Descriptive, because the phrase immediately conveys function and the competitor-need and third-party-use evidence point the same way.
C: Suggestive, because any two-word phrase necessarily requires imagination.
D: Generic as a matter of law whenever competitors would like to use the wording.
Answer: B
Authorities: Zatarains descriptiveness tests
Explanation: Rule. Descriptiveness may be tested through ordinary meaning, imagination, competitor need, and third-party use; no spelling variation controls. Application. HEAT-SHIELD directly communicates the liner's protective function, and market evidence reinforces that conclusion. Why the other choices fail. A overvalues punctuation, C invents a categorical rule, and D confuses descriptive evidence with the separate genericness inquiry.

## tm-03-q02
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition

Title: Question 2
Prompt: A business uses SIX-HOUR SPARK for a portable battery, but the phrase viewed alone could also evoke a beverage, an exercise program, or an endurance snack. A consumer must make several inferences to identify the goods. Which argument most strongly supports suggestiveness?
A: The phrase is suggestive solely because the owner calls it a brand.
B: The phrase does not immediately identify the product and requires imagination and perception to connect it to a portable battery.
C: Every duration-plus-noun phrase is inherently distinctive.
D: A mark is suggestive only after five years of exclusive use.
Answer: B
Authorities: Innovation Ventures inferential-reasoning analysis
Explanation: Rule. A term is suggestive when imagination or multistep inference is needed to discern the nature of the goods; it is descriptive when it immediately conveys their feature or function. Application. SIX-HOUR SPARK leaves the product category unresolved without additional thought. Why the other choices fail. A and C substitute labels for analysis, while D confuses inherent with acquired distinctiveness.

## tm-03-q03

Title: Question 3
Prompt: A seller claims that a descriptive package design has acquired distinctiveness. It offers high sales and large advertising expenditures, but the ads never draw attention to the claimed design and no survey or customer testimony links that design to one source. Which assessment is strongest?
A: High sales automatically establish secondary meaning.
B: Advertising expenditures establish secondary meaning even when the ads never teach consumers to treat the claimed feature as a mark.
C: The evidence is weak because the inquiry is whether consumers associate the specifically claimed design with a single source; direct survey or testimony and feature-focused advertising are more probative.
D: Descriptive matter can never acquire distinctiveness.
Answer: C
Authorities: Assigned secondary-meaning factors and TBL Licensing
Explanation: Rule. Secondary meaning asks whether the relevant public primarily associates the claimed feature with source; sales and advertising matter only insofar as they support that association, while surveys and direct testimony can be especially probative. Application. The seller proves commercial activity but not consumer perception of the specific design. Why the other choices fail. A and B make indirect evidence conclusive, and D ignores acquired distinctiveness.

Title: Question 4
Prompt: A storage-equipment seller seeks protection for a precise combination of recessed corner grips, staggered side ribs, and a hexagonal latch. Its survey shows the complete bin with the famous house name molded across the lid but never isolates the claimed combination. What is the key defect?
A: Product design is automatically generic.
B: A famous house name automatically transfers distinctiveness to every product feature.
C: Surveys are categorically inadmissible in product-design cases.
D: The evidence measures recognition of the overall branded product rather than whether the specifically claimed features identify source.
Answer: D
Authorities: TBL Licensing claim-specific acquired-distinctiveness analysis
Explanation: Rule. A product-design claimant must prove acquired distinctiveness in the particular features it seeks to protect, not merely recognition of the product or producer as a whole. Application. The molded name and complete bin allow respondents to rely on matter outside the grips, ribs, and latch. Why the other choices fail. A misclassifies design, C rejects a central form of evidence, and B improperly bootstraps brand fame.

Title: Question 5
Prompt: A survey tests secondary meaning for specialized laboratory centrifuges by interviewing a national adult sample dominated by people who never purchase medical equipment. A smaller survey of hospital procurement officers and laboratory managers produces much higher source-association results. Which issue is most important?
A: Whether the respondents represent the relevant consuming population for the goods.
B: Whether every respondent can correctly name the manufacturer, which is always required.
C: Whether the survey was conducted in a completely brand-free vacuum, which is always required.
D: Whether the larger survey produced the lower percentage, because lower results are inherently more reliable.
Answer: A
Authorities: Cartier survey-universe analysis
Explanation: Rule. A secondary-meaning survey should measure the perceptions of the relevant purchasing public; association with one source can be probative even if respondents cannot name it. Application. Procurement officers and laboratory managers better approximate the market for specialized centrifuges than the general public. Why the other choices fail. B demands unnecessary source naming, C imposes an absolute condition, and D confuses size and outcome with representativeness.

Title: Question 6
Prompt: A company claims exclusive rights in GARDEN TILES for thin edible crackers marketed to home gardeners. What must the decision maker determine first?
A: Whether the company spent more on advertising than its competitors.
B: Whether the company was the first seller to use the term.
C: Whether the term has ever appeared in a dictionary.
D: The genus of goods or services and whether the relevant public primarily understands the term to refer to that genus.
Answer: D
Authorities: Marvin Ginn genericness test
Explanation: Rule. The genericness inquiry identifies the relevant genus and then asks whether the relevant public primarily understands the disputed term to name that genus rather than indicate source. Application. The analysis must define the relevant crackers and their purchasing public before weighing perception evidence. Why the other choices fail. A, C, and B may supply context but do not replace the two-part inquiry.

Title: Question 7
Prompt: Both words in CITY BIKES are generic for bicycles supplied for urban transportation. Relevant consumers understand the combined phrase only as the product category and derive no additional source-indicating meaning from the combination. Which conclusion is best?
A: The phrase is protectable because two generic words always become distinctive when combined.
B: The phrase is fanciful because no dictionary lists the exact two-word phrase.
C: The phrase is generic when considered as a whole because the combination adds no source-distinguishing meaning.
D: The phrase is protectable if the seller separates the words with a design element, regardless of consumer perception.
Answer: C
Authorities: Booking.com and Snyder's-Lance whole-mark analysis
Explanation: Rule. A compound of generic elements must be considered as a whole, but it remains generic when the combination yields no additional source-distinguishing meaning. Application. CITY BIKES conveys only the urban-bicycle category. Why the other choices fail. A and B treat combination or dictionary absence as talismans, while D disregards public understanding.

## tm-03-q08
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Genericness and brand association
Set: TM-03
Coverage: Assigned Beebe printed pp. 1-67, 71-108, and 131-146
Theme: Brightwater Makers District
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 8
Prompt: One exercise company dominates sales of a recovery tool that consumers primarily call FOAM ROLLERS as the common product name. Many buyers also associate the term with that company because it pioneered and heavily advertised the tool. Does that association alone make the term protectable?
A: No, because secondary source association cannot rescue a term the public primarily understands as the product name.
B: Yes, if the bakery is the market leader.
C: Yes, because any association with one producer defeats genericness.
D: No, but only because exercise-product names can never be marks.
Answer: A
Authorities: Kellogg primary-significance principle and Snyder's-Lance
Explanation: Rule. Genericness turns on whether the term's primary significance is the product rather than the producer; commercial success or secondary association does not convert a generic product name into a mark. Application. Buyers primarily use FOAM ROLLERS to name the tool even though one company is strongly associated with it. Why the other choices fail. B and C confuse recognition with non-genericness, while D invents a product-specific bar.

## tm-03-q09

Title: Question 9
Prompt: A survey reports that 54% of 210 respondents classify a disputed term for cloud-storage services as a brand, with a plus-or-minus seven-point margin of error. Several control answers suggest that respondents confused familiar service names with legally valid marks. How should the survey be treated?
A: Conclusive proof of trademark status because 54% exceeds 50%.
B: Irrelevant because consumer surveys can never bear on genericness.
C: Conclusive proof of genericness because the sample is fewer than 500 people.
D: Cautiously and potentially as inconclusive, considering margin of error, representativeness, controls, and whether the format measured fame or secondary meaning instead of genericness.
Answer: D
Authorities: Booking.com survey caution and Snyder's-Lance survey analysis
Explanation: Rule. Surveys can illuminate consumer perception but require careful design and interpretation, including sampling error, population fit, controls, and conceptual validity. Application. The result can fall below a majority within the margin of error, and the controls suggest confusion between familiarity and legal classification. Why the other choices fail. A ignores uncertainty, B rejects relevant evidence categorically, and C invents a sample floor.

Title: Question 10
Prompt: An applicant places a short phrase beneath its house logo on reusable water bottles. The record contains a handful of decorative uses of the phrase by others but does not show widespread common-expression use that would defeat source perception. What is the best conclusion?
A: The limited record does not establish failure to function merely from some decorative use; consumer perception in context remains controlling.
B: Any phrase on a reusable bottle necessarily fails to function as a mark.
C: A phrase functions as a mark only if it is fanciful.
D: Failure to function and genericness are identical inquiries.
Answer: A
Authorities: Assigned In re Lizzo excerpt
Explanation: Rule. Failure to function turns on whether the proposed matter actually identifies source; a common-expression refusal requires sufficiently widespread use to defeat source perception. Application. A few decorative uses do not establish that record, particularly when context may support branding. Why the other choices fail. B and C impose categorical rules, and D collapses distinct doctrines.

Title: Question 11
Prompt: A new traveling farmers market uses a distinctive, nonfunctional combination of striped modular stalls, overhead pennants, matching vendor aprons, hand-painted price boards, and a circular checkout station that immediately signals source. A rival market copies the total presentation before acquired distinctiveness can be proved. What is the market's strongest position?
A: Unregistered trade dress is never protectable.
B: Trade dress requires five years of exclusive use in every case.
C: Inherently distinctive, nonfunctional trade dress may be protected without proof of secondary meaning.
D: Each individual element must be independently fanciful.
Answer: C
Authorities: Two Pesos
Explanation: Rule. Section 43(a) does not impose secondary meaning on inherently distinctive trade dress, although the claimed combination must still be source identifying and nonfunctional. Application. The market alleges a distinctive total presentation before acquired distinctiveness developed. Why the other choices fail. A ignores Section 43(a), B adds a temporal rule, and D improperly atomizes the combination.

## tm-03-q12
Course: Trademarks and Unfair Competition
Topic: Color and functionality
Set: TM-03
Coverage: Assigned Beebe printed pp. 1-67, 71-108, and 131-146
Theme: Brightwater Makers District
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Question 12
Prompt: A medical-supply company claims one shade of blue for dose-identification caps. Consumers associate the shade with the company, but hospitals rely on that blue to identify dosage and alternatives would materially increase error risk. Which is the best analysis?
A: The color may be barred as functional because its dose-identification role affects use and exclusive control would impose a significant non-reputation-related competitive disadvantage despite source association.
B: Color can never be a mark.
C: The color is protectable because secondary meaning always defeats functionality.
D: The color is automatically fanciful because no words are involved.
Answer: A
Authorities: Qualitex functionality analysis
Explanation: Rule. Color can serve as a mark if it has acquired distinctiveness and is nonfunctional, but functionality bars features essential to use or purpose, affecting cost or quality, or creating significant non-reputation-related competitive disadvantage. Application. The dosage role is a substantial nontrademark function that source association cannot override. Why the other choices fail. C subordinates functionality to secondary meaning, B contradicts color-mark doctrine, and D misapplies verbal-mark categories.
