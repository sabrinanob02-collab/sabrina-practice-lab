## ccp-04-q01
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Search definition: long-term pole-camera surveillance
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 1
Prompt: Halvorsen Bay detectives suspect Ines Varga of running stolen outboard motors through her rented house. Without a warrant, they mount a camera on a utility pole across the street. For 18 months it records her front yard, driveway, and front door around the clock, can pan and zoom, and stores every frame so detectives can search the archive later by date and time. Anyone standing on the sidewalk could see the same view. A low hedge blocks part of the yard from the street but not from the pole. At a suppression hearing, which statement best describes how a court applying the assigned cases should analyze whether this was a search?
A: It was not a search as a matter of settled law, because no one physically intruded on Varga's property, and without a trespass there can be no search.
B: The question is open. The government will rely on the principle that what a person knowingly exposes to the public from a lawful vantage point is unprotected. Varga's strongest argument is that months of continuous, archived, searchable recording builds a detailed record of home life that no passerby could ever assemble.
C: It was a search, because any camera is sense-enhancing technology, and using sense-enhancing technology to observe a residence is always a search.
D: It was not a search, because Varga rents the house, and a renter has no legitimate expectation of privacy in the yard of a home she does not own.
Answer: B
Authorities: Katz v. United States; California v. Ciraolo; United States v. Knotts; United States v. Jones; Kyllo v. United States; Carpenter v. United States
Explanation: Rule: A search occurs under either the trespass test (Jones) or the reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test (Katz). What a person knowingly exposes to public view from a lawful vantage point is generally unprotected (Ciraolo; Knotts). Carpenter, however, recognized that long-term, comprehensive, retrospective tracking can be a search even though each individual movement was public. Application: No Supreme Court case decides long-term pole cameras, and lower courts are divided. The government's best argument is the public vantage point. Varga's best argument is Carpenter's: 18 months of continuous, searchable footage of the front of a home reveals patterns of visitors, routines, and associations that no single officer on the sidewalk could gather. Why not the others: A misreads Jones; Katz survives, and trespass is an additional test, not the only one. C overstates Kyllo, which turned on technology not in general public use revealing details of the home's interior; ordinary cameras are in general use and saw only the exterior. D is wrong because renters have a legitimate expectation of privacy in their homes and the area around them; ownership is not required. Policy: The defense frames this as preventing cheap, permanent, dragnet surveillance that changes the relationship between citizen and state. The government frames it as keeping police from being forbidden to record what any neighbor could see, and as preserving a clear rule officers can follow.

## ccp-04-q02
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Third-party doctrine and location databases (license-plate readers)
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 2
Prompt: Halvorsen Bay has 400 automated plate-reader cameras on traffic lights and bridges. They log the date, time, and GPS position of every passing car and keep the data for three years. Without a warrant, detectives query Teodor Lindqvist's plate and pull 26 months of location points, roughly 9,000 sightings, showing his trips to a clinic, a church, a union hall, and a marina where a burglary occurred. Lindqvist moves to suppress. Which argument should his lawyer lead with?
A: His plate was displayed to the public and the cameras captured only what any observer could see, so the third-party doctrine makes the data unprotected.
B: Photographing the plate was a physical trespass on his car, an effect, done to obtain information.
C: Plate readers are technology not in general public use, and like the thermal imager they revealed intimate details from inside his home.
D: The query produced a detailed, retrospective record of his movements over more than two years, compiled cheaply and without his doing anything more than driving. That is the kind of comprehensive location record recognized as protected, unlike limited, real-time tracking of a single trip.
Answer: D
Authorities: Carpenter v. United States; United States v. Knotts; Smith v. Maryland; United States v. Jones; Kyllo v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Under Smith, information voluntarily conveyed to third parties is generally unprotected, and under Knotts, movements on public roads are knowingly exposed. Carpenter refused to extend those cases to historical cell-site records because of their depth, breadth, and comprehensive reach, and because they were retrospective and effortless to compile. Application: Whether plate-reader databases are a Carpenter search is unresolved. Lindqvist's best argument tracks Carpenter: 26 months and 9,000 data points reveal the "privacies of life," including medical, religious, and associational visits. Knotts involved a beeper that aided visual surveillance of a single trip. Why not the others: A is the government's argument, not the defense's. B fails because a camera that photographs a plate from a pole makes no physical contact with the car. C misapplies Kyllo; the cameras record public roads, not the interior of a home. Policy: Defense: aggregation turns lawful individual observations into a surveillance tool the Framers would have found intolerable, and it chills association and religious practice. Government: a warrant requirement for routine database queries would cripple investigations of hit-and-runs and abductions, and drivers know their plates are public.

## ccp-04-q03
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Standing: short-term commercial renter vs. business visitor
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 3
Prompt: Through a home-sharing app, Dorian Pell pays $60 to rent a private room in a Halvorsen Bay rowhouse for one afternoon. The app gives him a door code that only he holds for those four hours. He brings no luggage and uses the room to divide pills into packets for buyers who stop by. Officers, acting on a hunch, get the host to open the door without a warrant and find the pills. Does Pell have a legitimate expectation of privacy that lets him challenge the entry?
A: Probably yes. Unlike visitors who are present briefly on a leaseholder's premises for business, Pell himself held the possessory right to the room and the means to exclude others. The commercial purpose and short stay weigh against him but do not erase the interest a paying occupant holds, as with a hotel guest.
B: No, because anyone present on premises for a commercial transaction categorically lacks standing.
C: No, because only an overnight guest can have a legitimate expectation of privacy in a home belonging to someone else.
D: Yes, because anyone legitimately on the premises when a search occurs may challenge it.
Answer: A
Authorities: Rakas v. Illinois; Minnesota v. Olson; Minnesota v. Carter
Explanation: Rule: Fourth Amendment rights are personal (Rakas). A defendant must show his own legitimate expectation of privacy, considering factors such as possession of a key, the ability to exclude others, the nature and duration of the stay, and the relationship to the premises. Olson protects overnight guests. Carter denied protection to visitors who were in the leaseholder's apartment for a few hours for a purely commercial purpose. Application: The open twist is that Pell is not the leaseholder's business visitor; he is the renter. His exclusive door code and right to exclude look like a hotel guest's interest. The business purpose and brief duration (the Carter factors) cut against him, so this is a close call. Choice A states the best analysis. Why not the others: B overstates Carter, which weighed several factors rather than announcing a categorical commercial bar. C misreads Olson as setting a floor; Olson is sufficient, not necessary. D restates the "legitimately on the premises" test that Rakas rejected. Policy: Linking standing to the right to exclude respects property-based intuitions and the growing reality of app-based short-term lodging. Narrow standing limits the reach of the exclusionary rule and the costs of suppression.

## ccp-04-q04
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Particularity and digital-data warrants; severability
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 4
Prompt: An affidavit establishes probable cause that Marisol Okafor defrauded one elderly client of $40,000 through fake invoices emailed in March 2026. The warrant, which does not incorporate or attach the affidavit, authorizes officers to seize "all computers, phones, and storage devices at 18 Gull Lane" and to search "all data contained therein." Okafor moves to suppress. What is the best analysis?
A: The warrant is valid. Just as a drug warrant may authorize a search of an entire house, a fraud warrant may authorize a search of all data on every device.
B: The entire warrant is void, because any particularity flaw in any part of a warrant invalidates the whole warrant and everything seized under it.
C: Seizing the devices may be supportable, but authorizing a search of "all data" without tying it to the fraud, such as a date range, the victim, or invoice records, likely fails particularity. A court may sever the overbroad portion and suppress only what was obtained under it.
D: The warrant is valid, because the affidavit described the March invoice scheme in detail, and an affidavit always cures a deficient warrant.
Answer: C
Authorities: Particularity requirement; Groh v. Ramirez; Messerschmidt v. Millender; United States v. Leon
Explanation: Rule: A warrant must particularly describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized. The specificity required depends on the crime and the facts known. An affidavit can cure a deficient warrant only if it is incorporated and accompanies the warrant (Groh). Courts may sever the valid parts of a warrant from the overbroad parts. Application: The probable cause concerns one victim, one scheme, and one month. "All data" on every device authorizes a general rummage through a person's digital life, unconnected to that showing. The device seizure is closer, because the evidence is electronic and must be stored somewhere. Separately, good faith under Leon and Messerschmidt may save evidence unless the overbreadth was so obvious that no reasonable officer would rely on the warrant. Why not the others: A stretches the drug-house analogy. Contraband can be hidden anywhere in a house, but a fraud warrant can be limited by date, sender, and file type. B ignores severability. D misstates Groh, because the affidavit was neither incorporated nor attached. Policy: Particularity targets the evil of the general warrant that motivated the Fourth Amendment. Digital devices hold more private information than a house. Severance and good faith avoid freeing a defendant over drafting errors.

## ccp-04-q05
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Knock-and-announce: wait time, smart doorbells, and remedy
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 5
Prompt: Officers hold a valid warrant to search Bram Halloway's garage for 30 stolen catalytic converters. At 2 p.m. they press his video doorbell, say "Police, search warrant" into its speaker, wait eight seconds, and break the door. Nothing suggested danger or that anyone was home. They find the converters. Halloway moves to suppress. What result?
A: Suppress the converters. Knock-and-announce is part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness, so evidence found after any violation must be excluded.
B: Deny suppression, because the entry was reasonable. A wait of under 20 seconds has been approved before.
C: The entry was likely unreasonable, because bulky converters cannot be flushed and nothing indicated danger or futility. Even so, suppression is unavailable for a knock-and-announce violation.
D: Suppress the converters, because announcing through a doorbell speaker is a no-knock entry, which is per se unconstitutional without a judicial no-knock authorization.
Answer: C
Authorities: Wilson v. Arkansas; Richards v. Wisconsin; United States v. Banks; Hudson v. Michigan
Explanation: Rule: Knock-and-announce is part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness (Wilson). Officers may dispense with it only on reasonable, case-specific suspicion of danger, futility, or destruction of evidence (Richards). How long officers must wait depends on how quickly the evidence could be destroyed (Banks: 15-20 seconds was enough for easily flushed drugs). Violations do not trigger the exclusionary rule (Hudson). Application: Thirty converters cannot be destroyed in seconds, and nothing suggested danger. Eight seconds at 2 p.m. probably did not give an occupant a reasonable chance to reach the door. But Hudson reasons that the interests protected by knock-and-announce (property, dignity, and the chance to comply) have nothing to do with seizing evidence. Why not the others: A ignores Hudson. B misapplies Banks, whose short wait depended on disposable drugs. D is wrong because police did announce, and Richards rejects blanket categorical rules in either direction. Policy: Hudson shows the cost-benefit approach to exclusion: civil suits and police discipline are thought adequate deterrents, and suppression is a heavy cost. Critics answer that a right without a remedy invites routine violations.

## ccp-04-q06
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Probable cause from a proven informant (totality of the circumstances)
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 6
Prompt: An affidavit says an informant who has given officers accurate information three times in the past year, each leading to seizures, personally saw a large bag of methamphetamine in the rear bedroom of 7 Pilot Street yesterday and saw the occupant weigh portions on a scale. Officers confirm only that the occupant drives the truck the informant described. The magistrate issues a warrant. Was there a substantial basis for probable cause?
A: Yes. Under the totality of the circumstances, the informant's track record shows reliability and her recent firsthand observation shows a strong basis of knowledge. Corroboration of innocent details adds some weight, though it is not required to make up for a weak factor.
B: No, because officers must independently corroborate the criminal activity itself, not just innocent details.
C: No, because an informant must be named to the magistrate before her information can count.
D: Yes, because an officer's sworn statement that an informant gave information is enough on its own, whatever the informant's reliability or basis.
Answer: A
Authorities: Illinois v. Gates; Aguilar v. Texas; Spinelli v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Gates replaced the rigid two-pronged Aguilar-Spinelli test with a totality-of-the-circumstances inquiry. Veracity and basis of knowledge remain highly relevant, and strength in one can offset weakness in another. A reviewing court asks only whether the magistrate had a substantial basis. Application: Both traditional factors are strong here: a proven track record and a firsthand, recent, detailed observation. Corroboration is a plus, not a prerequisite. Why not the others: B imposes a corroboration-of-crime requirement no case demands. C invents a naming requirement; protecting confidential informants is routine. D describes a "bare bones" affidavit that Gates says cannot support probable cause, because the magistrate must have facts to evaluate, not just the officer's conclusion. Policy: The flexible Gates test reflects the view that probable cause is a practical, common-sense judgment made by non-lawyers, often in a hurry. Critics argue the flexibility weakens the magistrate's role as a check and encourages reliance on informants with mixed incentives.

## ccp-04-q07
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Plain view after an in-home arrest; moving an object
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 7
Prompt: Officers enter Nell Strand's apartment with a warrant for her arrest on a forgery charge, reasonably believing she is home. They handcuff her in the kitchen and walk her to the patrol car. Officer Ruiz then returns to the living room and sees a laptop on the couch. Suspecting it might be one of several laptops reported stolen in town, though nothing about it says so, she flips it over to read the serial number, which matches a stolen unit. Is the laptop admissible?
A: Yes, because officers were lawfully inside, and plain view requires only reasonable suspicion that an item is contraband.
B: No, because the discovery was not inadvertent; Ruiz went back to the living room hoping to find stolen goods.
C: Yes, because search incident to arrest covers any room of a home in which the arrest occurs.
D: No. Flipping the laptop to expose the serial number was a separate search, and its incriminating character was not immediately apparent, so it required probable cause and the authority to be in that spot after the arrest was complete.
Answer: D
Authorities: Horton v. California; Arizona v. Hicks; Chimel v. California; Payton v. New York
Explanation: Rule: Plain view permits a warrantless seizure when (1) the officer is lawfully at the vantage point, (2) the item's incriminating character is immediately apparent, meaning probable cause, and (3) the officer has lawful access to the object (Horton). Moving an item to expose concealed information is itself a search (Hicks). Search incident to arrest reaches the arrestee's person and the area within her immediate control at the time of arrest (Chimel). Application: Ruiz had only a hunch, not probable cause, and turning the laptop over was a new invasion. Strand was already in the car, so nothing in the living room was within her reach, and the arrest warrant's authority ended once she was secured. Why not the others: A wrongly lowers the standard to reasonable suspicion. B relies on an inadvertence requirement that Horton eliminated. C overstates Chimel, which rejected whole-house searches incident to arrest. Policy: Requiring probable cause even for "minimal" movements prevents plain view from becoming a general search. Horton's rejection of inadvertence rests on objective standards, so courts do not have to probe officers' hopes and motives.

## ccp-04-q08
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Search incident to arrest: smartwatch notifications
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 8
Prompt: After a lawful arrest for selling stolen bicycles, an officer sees a dark smartwatch on Caspian Rowe's wrist. Without a warrant, the officer taps the face, which lights up and shows the preview of the latest unopened message: "Got 3 more bikes. Same price?" The officer does not unlock the watch. Is the preview admissible under search incident to arrest?
A: Yes. A full search of the arrestee's person, including items worn on the body, is automatically permitted after a custodial arrest.
B: Probably not. Activating the watch to display stored message content is a search of digital data, which is categorically different from the physical items the arrest-search rule was built for. It requires a warrant absent a genuine exigency.
C: Yes, because it was reasonable to believe evidence of the crime of arrest would be found in the device.
D: No, but only because the watch screen was dark; had it already been lit, the officer could have scrolled through its messages.
Answer: B
Authorities: Riley v. California; Chimel v. California; United States v. Robinson; Arizona v. Gant; Bond v. United States
Explanation: Rule: Search incident to arrest permits a search of the arrestee's person and immediate area, justified by officer safety and evidence preservation (Chimel; Robinson). Riley held that these rationales do not extend to digital data on phones: data cannot harm officers, and remote wiping can be addressed in other ways. Officers must get a warrant. Application: Whether a single tap to reveal a lock-screen preview counts as a search under Riley is not definitively settled. But tapping the device to make it display stored message content is a manipulation done to obtain information (compare Bond), and it reveals exactly the kind of private communication Riley protected. The best answer is that a warrant was required. Why not the others: A applies Robinson's categorical rule, which Riley refused to extend to digital content. C borrows Gant's "reason to believe" rule, which applies only to vehicle passenger compartments. D is backwards: a lit screen might put the preview itself in plain view, but scrolling through messages would still be a Riley search. Policy: Riley recognizes that modern devices hold "the privacies of life." A bright warrant line is easy for officers to apply and protects against sweeping intrusions. The cost is delay, which exigency and the ease of electronic warrants reduce.

## ccp-04-q09
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Automobile exception: motor home functioning as a residence
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 9
Prompt: For three weeks Gideon Marsh has lived in a motor home at a Halvorsen Bay campground. It is hooked to campground water, power, and sewer lines, raised on leveling jacks with its slide-out rooms extended, and screened by a canvas awning wall. The campground has a paved exit to the highway. Officers develop probable cause that fentanyl is inside and search it without a warrant. Does the automobile exception apply?
A: Probably not. The connected utilities, raised jacks, extended slide-outs, and three-week stay show it was being used as a residence rather than as a readily mobile vehicle, so the reasons behind the exception weaken and a warrant was needed.
B: Yes, because any vehicle with a working engine falls within the automobile exception regardless of how it is being used.
C: Yes, but only if officers had seen it being driven within the previous 24 hours.
D: Yes, because officers may always conduct an inventory search of any vehicle they could lawfully impound.
Answer: A
Authorities: Carroll v. United States; California v. Carney; Colorado v. Bertine
Explanation: Rule: The automobile exception allows a warrantless search of a readily mobile vehicle when there is probable cause to believe it contains contraband or evidence. It rests on mobility and on the reduced expectation of privacy that comes from pervasive regulation of vehicles (Carroll). For hybrid vehicles, courts ask whether it is readily mobile and appears used for transportation, looking at location, whether it is on wheels or blocks, utility hookups, and road access. Application: Everything except highway access points to a residence: hookups, jacks, slide-outs, an awning wall, and a stay of weeks. Both reasons for the exception, ready mobility and a reduced privacy expectation, are weak here. Why not the others: B ignores the functional analysis. C invents a timing rule. D misstates inventory doctrine: an inventory must follow a lawful impoundment conducted under standardized procedures and may not be a pretext for an evidence search (Bertine). Policy: The exception trades a warrant for speed where the evidence could literally drive away. When a vehicle is someone's home, the home's core protection should control. Bright lines help officers, but here they would let the label "vehicle" override the realities of how people live.

## ccp-04-q10
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Border searches: forensic laptop download
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 10
Prompt: Returning to the United States on an international flight that lands at Halvorsen Bay's airport, Priya Nandakumar is selected at random. Officers take her laptop to a back room, connect it to software that copies and analyzes the entire drive, including deleted files, and find evidence of an export-control violation. They had no suspicion. Which answer best states the law that applies?
A: The search was valid because government power is at its peak at the border, so no search of any belongings there needs suspicion, however thorough or destructive.
B: The issue is unsettled. A manual look through a device is likely a routine border search needing no suspicion. Her strongest argument is that a full forensic copy of a digital device is so much more invasive, given the private data such devices hold, that it is non-routine and requires at least some individualized suspicion.
C: The search was invalid because a warrant and probable cause are always required to search a digital device, at the border or elsewhere.
D: The border exception does not apply because an inland airport is not the physical border.
Answer: B
Authorities: Border search exception; United States v. Flores-Montano; Riley v. California
Explanation: Rule: At the border or its functional equivalent, routine searches require no warrant, probable cause, or reasonable suspicion, because the government's interest in controlling what enters the country is at its zenith. Flores-Montano upheld removing and disassembling a gas tank without suspicion. Riley recognized that digital devices hold uniquely vast and private information. Application: The Supreme Court has not decided whether forensic device searches at the border need suspicion, and the circuits disagree. The best answer frames both sides: manual review fits routine-search doctrine, while a forensic download arguably is non-routine because of Riley's insight about digital privacy. Why not the others: A treats Flores-Montano as resolving digital searches. That case involved a gas tank and stressed the low privacy interest in a vehicle. C overreads Riley, which addressed search incident to arrest, not the border. D ignores the functional-equivalent doctrine: an international arrival airport counts. Policy: The government says sovereignty, contraband interdiction, and national security demand broad border authority. Travelers say a phone or laptop carries their whole life, and suspicionless copying turns every trip abroad into a general warrant.

## ccp-04-q11
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Third-party consent: house-sitter and a locked home office
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 11
Prompt: While Arlo Dunmore is away, his neighbor Tessa house-sits, feeding the cat and watering plants. She has a front-door key. Investigating a tip that Arlo runs an illegal betting book, officers ask Tessa whether they can look around. She lets them in and says, "Sure, look wherever." The home office door is locked. Tessa says she has never been inside but knows where Arlo hides that key, and she retrieves it. Officers search the office and find betting ledgers. Is the search of the office valid?
A: Yes, because holding a key to the house gives a person common authority over every room in it.
B: Yes, because the officers sincerely believed Tessa could consent, and a sincere belief is enough for apparent authority.
C: No. Tessa's limited caretaking role, the locked door, and her statement that she had never been inside would lead reasonable officers to doubt her authority over the office. Without asking more, they could not rely on apparent authority.
D: No, because a third party can never consent to the search of someone else's home.
Answer: C
Authorities: United States v. Matlock; Illinois v. Rodriguez; Schneckloth v. Bustamonte
Explanation: Rule: A third party may consent if she has common authority, meaning mutual use and joint access or control for most purposes (Matlock). Apparent authority validates a search when the facts available would lead a reasonable officer to believe the consenter had authority (Rodriguez). The test is objective, and officers may not proceed blindly in the face of ambiguity. Application: A house-sitter's front-door key and care of the pets suggest limited authority over common areas. The locked office and Tessa's admission that she never went in are red flags that defeat any reasonable belief in her authority over that room. Why not the others: A equates a key with common authority over everything; authority is defined by use and access, not property law. B substitutes the officers' subjective belief for the objective standard. D contradicts Matlock. Policy: Third-party consent reflects assumption of risk: we accept that people who share space can let others in. Limiting it to areas they actually control respects the private zones people keep even inside shared spaces, while still letting police rely on reasonable appearances.

## ccp-04-q12
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Emergency aid triggered by an automated fall-detection call
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 12
Prompt: A smartwatch's fall-detection feature automatically calls 911 from Wren Abernathy's address and plays a recording: "The wearer has taken a hard fall and is not responding." Officers arrive, knock, and get no answer. Through a front window they see a man lying motionless on the floor beside a coffee table. They force the door, find him conscious but dazed, call medics, and see bags of cocaine on the coffee table. While medics work, one officer searches the bedrooms and finds a handgun. Which ruling is correct?
A: The entry was unlawful because the call came from a device rather than a person, and an automated alert cannot supply the basis for entering a home.
B: The entry was unlawful because one of the officers later said he hoped to find drugs.
C: The entry was lawful, and once inside the officers could search the whole house for contraband.
D: The entry was lawful under emergency aid because officers had an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needed immediate help. The cocaine in plain view is admissible, but the bedroom search exceeded the scope of the emergency.
Answer: D
Authorities: Michigan v. Fisher; Warden v. Hayden; Horton v. California
Explanation: Rule: Officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe an occupant is seriously injured or in imminent danger (Fisher). The search must be strictly limited to the emergency and must end when it ends. Items in plain view during a lawful entry may be seized (Horton). Application: An automated fall alert, plus no answer, plus a person seen motionless on the floor, gives an objectively reasonable belief that someone needs help now. The cocaine was in plain view in the room where the emergency was. Searching bedrooms had nothing to do with a fallen man already being treated. Why not the others: A has no support; the question is whether the basis was objectively reasonable, and corroboration through the window makes it so. B is wrong because emergency aid is judged objectively, not by an officer's motive (Fisher). C ignores scope limits. Policy: Emergency aid reflects the community-caretaking role society wants police to play; no one wants officers waiting for a warrant while someone dies. Strict scope limits keep a rescue from becoming a pretext for an evidence search.

## ccp-04-q13
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Terry stop and frisk; plain feel limits
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: medium
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 13
Prompt: At 2 a.m. in the Halvorsen Bay ferry garage, which has had a string of car break-ins, an officer watches Silas Quayle walk the rows for ten minutes, peering into car windows and trying three door handles. A long steel lock-picking tool sticks out of his back pocket. The officer stops him and pats him down. In a front pocket the officer feels a soft, squishy lump. Not thinking it is a weapon, he rolls it between his fingers several times until he concludes it is a baggie of pills, then removes it. Which statement is correct?
A: The stop was unlawful because the officer never saw Quayle actually break into a car, so all evidence must be suppressed.
B: The pills are admissible because the plain-feel doctrine lets an officer keep manipulating an object until he identifies it.
C: The pills are admissible as a search incident to arrest, because probable cause arose once the officer identified the pills.
D: The stop and frisk were lawful, but the pills must be suppressed, because their identity became apparent only through continued manipulation after the officer knew the lump was not a weapon.
Answer: D
Authorities: Terry v. Ohio; Minnesota v. Dickerson
Explanation: Rule: Officers may briefly stop a person on reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. They may frisk the outer clothing for weapons if they reasonably believe the person is armed and dangerous (Terry). Under Dickerson, an officer may seize contraband whose identity is immediately apparent during a lawful pat-down, but further squeezing or manipulation after concluding the object is not a weapon exceeds Terry's scope. Application: Peering into cars, trying handles, carrying a heavy steel tool, and doing it at 2 a.m. amid a string of break-ins gives reasonable suspicion of attempted car break-ins, and the tool supports concern about a weapon. But the officer did not think the lump was a weapon and identified it only through repeated manipulation. Why not the others: A demands more than reasonable suspicion. B misstates plain feel. C gets the sequence wrong: the search that produced probable cause was itself unlawful, so it cannot justify itself. Policy: Terry balances officer safety against a serious intrusion on personal dignity. Limiting frisks to weapons keeps a safety measure from becoming an evidence-gathering tool on mere suspicion.

## ccp-04-q14
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: When a seizure occurs: force vs. show of authority
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 14
Prompt: An officer shouts "Stop, police!" at Lazlo Fenn, who runs. The officer fires a taser, and one dart pierces Fenn's jacket and lodges in his shoulder. Fenn pulls it out and keeps running. Two blocks later he tosses a backpack over a fence, and a minute after that he is tackled. When was Fenn first seized?
A: When the officer shouted "Stop, police!", because a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave.
B: Never before the tackle, because Fenn never submitted until he was physically caught.
C: When the dart struck him. Applying physical force to the body with intent to restrain is a seizure even if the person does not submit, though that seizure lasts only as long as the force is applied.
D: Only when the backpack was recovered, because a seizure of a person requires a seizure of property.
Answer: C
Authorities: California v. Hodari D.; Torres v. Madrid; United States v. Mendenhall
Explanation: Rule: A seizure by show of authority requires actual submission (Hodari D.). A seizure by physical force happens the moment force is applied to the body with intent to restrain, even if the person does not submit, but it lasts only as long as the force is applied (Torres). Mendenhall's "free to leave" test asks whether there was a show of authority; it does not decide when a non-submitting suspect is seized. Application: The shout was a show of authority Fenn ignored, so no seizure then. The dart applied force to his body with intent to restrain, so that was a momentary seizure. When he kept running he was no longer seized, so the backpack was abandoned during flight rather than seized from him. Why not the others: A ignores Hodari's submission requirement. B ignores Torres. D confuses the seizure of persons with the seizure of property. Policy: Hodari's rule avoids rewarding flight and gives police a clear line. Torres ties the common-law concept of arrest-by-touch to the Fourth Amendment so that the use of force is always reviewable. Critics of Hodari argue it lets police make unjustified shows of authority with no constitutional consequence when people run.

## ccp-04-q15
Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Sixth Amendment deliberate elicitation; offense-specific attachment
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 15
Prompt: Kestrel Moody is indicted for setting fire to his own bait shop and is released on bail with appointed counsel. Detectives also suspect, but have not charged, that he filed a separate fraudulent insurance claim on an unrelated boat months earlier. They fit his coworker with a hidden recorder and tell her to "get him talking" about both the fire and the boat claim. Over lunch Moody makes damaging statements about each. Which statements are admissible at trial on each charge?
A: All of them, because Moody was not in custody, so his statements were voluntary.
B: The statements about the fire are inadmissible in the arson trial because the government deliberately elicited them after the right to counsel attached. The statements about the uncharged boat claim are admissible in a later fraud prosecution because the Sixth Amendment right is offense-specific.
C: None of them, because once counsel is appointed on any charge, the government may never deliberately elicit statements about any crime.
D: All of them, because the coworker is a private citizen, not a government agent.
Answer: B
Authorities: Massiah v. United States; Texas v. Cobb; Kuhlmann v. Wilson
Explanation: Rule: Once adversary judicial proceedings begin (indictment, information, or arraignment), the Sixth Amendment bars the government from deliberately eliciting statements from the accused without counsel (Massiah), including through secret agents. The right is offense-specific (Cobb): it covers the charged offense and offenses that are the same under the elements test, not uncharged, factually distinct crimes. Application: The coworker acted at police direction and was told to get him talking, which is deliberate elicitation, not passive listening (contrast Kuhlmann). The arson statements are barred in that prosecution. The boat-fraud claim is a separate, uncharged offense, so the right had not attached for it. Why not the others: A confuses Miranda custody with Massiah, which applies regardless of custody. C ignores Cobb. D ignores the agency created by police direction. Policy: Massiah protects the fairness of the adversary process once the state has committed to prosecuting: it cannot bypass defense counsel. Offense-specificity keeps the government free to investigate new crimes, which matters for public safety.

Title: Exam 1, Question 16

Privately, the officer has already decided to arrest him once he is discharged. Blackwell admits to six beers. Was he in custody for Miranda purposes?

D: Yes, because any questioning about a crime by a uniformed officer is custodial interrogation.
Answer: A
Authorities: Miranda v. Arizona; Stansbury v. California; Oregon v. Mathiason; Howes v. Fields
Explanation: Rule: Miranda applies to custodial interrogation. Custody is objective: given the circumstances, would a reasonable person have felt free to end the questioning, and does the setting present the inherently coercive pressures of station-house questioning? An officer's undisclosed views do not count (Stansbury). Being in a restricted setting is not automatically custody (Howes, prison questioning). D collapses custody and interrogation into "any police questioning," which Mathiason rejects. Course: Constitutional Criminal Procedure I
Topic: Midstream warnings and curative measures
Set: CCP-04
Coverage: Classes 1-26 (Aug. 17-Nov. 16, 2026), cumulative
Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 17
Prompt: Following department training, Detective Haas questions Sabine Lowry without warnings in a locked interview room and gets her to confess to a hit-and-run. Two hours later, at a different station, a different detective gives full warnings and adds: "Anything you said earlier cannot be used against you. This is a fresh start." Lowry waives and confesses again. Under the controlling approach in the assigned reading, is the second confession admissible?
A: No, because any statement that follows an unwarned confession is automatically fruit of the poisonous tree.
B: Yes, because an unwarned but voluntary first statement never affects a later warned one, even when police use the two-step method deliberately.
C: Probably yes. Even if the two-step method was deliberate, the curative measures (a substantial break in time and place, a new interrogator, and an explicit statement that the first confession could not be used) let a reasonable suspect understand the warnings as a genuine choice.
D: No, because a valid waiver is impossible once a suspect has made an unwarned confession.
Answer: C
Authorities: Oregon v. Elstad; Missouri v. Seibert
Explanation: Rule: Under Elstad, a voluntary but unwarned statement does not automatically taint a later warned statement. In Seibert, the plurality asked whether midstream warnings could effectively inform the suspect of her rights. Justice Kennedy's narrower concurrence, which many courts treat as controlling, excludes the warned statement only if police deliberately used the two-step technique and took no curative measures, such as a substantial break in time and circumstances or an explicit warning that the earlier statement is inadmissible. Application: The first interrogation was deliberate (it followed training), but the curative measures here are strong: two hours, a new place, a new detective, and an explicit "can't be used" statement. Under either approach the warnings likely functioned. Why not the others: A contradicts Elstad. B ignores Seibert's limit on deliberate strategies. D is wrong because a later waiver can be valid, as Elstad and Seibert both contemplate. Policy: Seibert prevents police from evading Miranda by design, which protects the deterrent purpose of the warnings. Title: Exam 1, Question 18
Prompt: After warnings and a waiver, Corwin Tate is questioned about a marina burglary. Early on he says, "I think I might want a lawyer, but let's see what you've got first." Questioning continues. An hour later he says, "I'm done. Get me my attorney." Officers stop. He stays in a holding cell, and three hours later a different detective gives fresh warnings and asks only about an unrelated arson. Tate waives and confesses to the arson. Which is correct?
A: The first remark was not an unambiguous request for counsel, so questioning could continue. After the clear request, police could not reinitiate interrogation about any crime while he remained in continuous custody, so the arson confession must be suppressed.
B: The first remark invoked the right to counsel, so everything after it must be suppressed.
C: The arson confession is admissible because police waited a significant period, gave fresh warnings, and asked about a different crime.
D: The arson confession is admissible because three hours in a holding cell is a break in custody that ends the protection.
Answer: A
Authorities: Davis v. United States; Edwards v. Arizona; Michigan v. Mosley; Maryland v. Shatzer
Explanation: Rule: Invocation of counsel must be unambiguous; officers need not stop for, or clarify, an equivocal reference (Davis). Once a suspect clearly asks for counsel, police may not interrogate him again about any crime until counsel is present, unless the suspect himself initiates (Edwards), or there has been a break in custody of at least 14 days (Shatzer). Davis's clarity requirement gives officers a workable standard but is criticized for burdening suspects who speak tentatively.

## ccp-04-q20

Theme: Halvorsen Bay
Difficulty: hard
Type: mc
Title: Exam 1, Question 20
Prompt: Halvorsen Bay pays a private vendor to run its warrant database. During a lawful traffic stop, an officer's terminal shows an active warrant for the driver, Fiona Calloway. The officer arrests her and finds a stolen pistol in her purse. The warrant had been recalled four months earlier, but a vendor employee failed to update the record. The record shows this was the first such error in years. Calloway moves to suppress. What is the best answer?
A: Suppress, because the arrest lacked a valid legal basis, and after Mapp all evidence from an unconstitutional arrest must be excluded.
B: Never suppress, because the error was made by a private company, so the Fourth Amendment does not apply at all.
C: Suppress, because the good-faith exception applies only when officers rely on a warrant issued by a neutral magistrate.
D: Probably not suppressed. Exclusion is reserved for deliberate, reckless, or grossly negligent conduct, or recurring systemic negligence, that suppression can meaningfully deter. An isolated recordkeeping lapse is unlikely to qualify, but proof of systemic errors could change the result.
Answer: D
Authorities: Mapp v. Ohio; United States v. Leon; Herring v. United States; Arizona v. Evans; Illinois v. Krull
Explanation: Rule: Exclusion is a judicially created remedy, used only where its deterrent benefits outweigh its substantial costs (Leon). Good faith covers reasonable reliance on statutes (Krull), on court-clerk records (Evans), and on police recordkeeping errors that are isolated negligence (Herring). Exclusion remains available for deliberate, reckless, or grossly negligent conduct, or recurring or systemic negligence. Application: Whether a private vendor's error is treated like a court clerk's or the police's own is a new question. Either way, Herring's deterrence focus asks whether the error was isolated or systemic. A first-in-years lapse looks isolated; evidence of chronic vendor errors would shift the balance. Why not the others: A states a pre-Leon automatic rule. B confuses the private-search doctrine with the officer's arrest, which is government action even when it relies on a vendor's data. C is wrong because Krull, Evans, and Herring extend good faith beyond magistrate warrants. Policy: The good-faith exception reflects the view that suppression should target culpable police conduct, because freeing the guilty is a serious social cost. Critics respond that tolerating database errors gives agencies little reason to keep accurate records.
